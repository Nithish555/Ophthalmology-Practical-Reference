## 1. ROLES AND THE STANDARD

Every card passes through two senior roles, given to different sub-agents so the examiner reads the card fresh.

**Pass 1 — the writer: a senior teaching assistant.** You sit with the candidate in the week before the exam. For every
point, write what it is, why it matters and how to say it to the examiner. Never leave a term, sign or test unexplained.
Never write a question without its answer. Gather every fact from the books before you write (the claims ledger, §6).
Write nothing the books do not support.

**Pass 2 — the reviewer: a senior examiner** who has sat on TNMGRMU M.S. practical panels (EXAMINER_SPEC.md).

Then an **independent fact-check** tests every claim, including anything the examiner added (FACTCHECK_SPEC.md).

**The standard every card must meet:**
1. **Enough on its own.** After reading the card he can present the case, perform the examination and answer the viva,
   without opening a book.
2. **Plain English, exact clinical terms.** Short sentences, one idea per line. Every finding, sign, test,
   classification, drug and operation uses the exact term examiners use ("grade 2 relative afferent pupillary defect",
   never "pupil reacts less"). Explain a hard term once, in brackets, after the term — never instead of it.
3. **Correct.** Every fact, number, grade and dose comes from the three books (Baidya 2024 > Namrata Sharma > Aravind
   FAQ 2013). **Never use memory or the web for a clinical fact.** Omit anything you cannot verify. Books disagree → the
   newer book on the card; the disagreement goes in the notes file, not on the card. Kanski only as the tagged fallback
   (CONSISTENCY.md).
4. **Priority-ordered.** Basics first, then important points, then minor ones (§2.1).
5. **Well structured, with the examiner's keywords.** Each answer has the shape for its kind of question, opens with its
   keyword, and starts from the standard definition (§2.3–§2.5). Simple English, professional register (§2.6).
6. **Case-specific and consistent.** A card holds only what is special to its case. The generic proforma is in the index
   file (00). The shared drug, laser and surgery profiles are on the subject's toolkit card (GX for glaucoma). The same
   headings appear in the same order on every card. Tables for comparisons, numbered steps for procedures.

## 2. HOW DEEP TO GO, AND HOW TO WRITE EACH ANSWER

### 2.1 Three tiers — basics, then important, then minor
In every section write the basics first, then the important points, then the minor ones.

| Tier | What belongs here | Share | How shown |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Basic** | What every pass candidate must know; missing it fails the station: the definition, the must-do examination in order, how to record each finding, the complete diagnosis, the main differentials, first-line investigations, first-line treatment with its mechanism and main side effect, the standard operation and its indications | about 60% | First in every section. Never cut. |
| **Important** | Commonly asked; separates a good candidate: named classifications and grades, second-line treatment, combinations, choosing between operations, complications and their management | about 30% | After the basics. |
| **Minor** | Asked only of a first-rank candidate: newer drugs, genetics, trials, rarer variants | at most 10% | Last, tagged *(extra)*. Cut first. |

### 2.2 Depth — detailed, not exhaustive
- **Fact plus reason.** Each point is the fact plus its reason ("…because…"). A viva answer runs 1–3 lines (up to 4 when
  the question asks for a list); a list answer up to 6 short items; a management step 2–4 lines.
- **One follow-up deep.** Answer the question and the examiner's most likely next question ("Why?", "What else?",
  "What next?"), then stop.
- **Never telegraphic.** If a junior could not follow a line without the book, it is too short. Write full short
  sentences. In tables, make each cell a complete thought. (The v2 cards failed here: "Rules out angle closure (haloes =
  corneal oedema)" must become a sentence that says what a "yes" means and how to tell the causes apart.)
- **Leave out:** the history of eponyms; rare variants mentioned only in passing; trial details beyond the name and a
  one-line result; numbers no examiner would ask.

### 2.3 Answer structure — the same shape for the same kind of question
Lead with the answer, never with background.

| The question asks for | Structure of the answer |
|---|---|
| **A definition** | 1. The standard definition, in the book's wording, key terms unchanged. 2. One line in plain words. 3. The 2–3 features that make the diagnosis. |
| **A classification** | 1. Name the system. 2. Its grades in order (table or numbered list). 3. Which grade this patient has. |
| **Causes** | 1. Group them (congenital / acquired, ocular / systemic). 2. Most common first in each group. 3. The mnemonic, if the mnemonics file has one. |
| **Symptoms and signs** | 1. Symptoms first. 2. Signs in examination order: lids → conjunctiva → cornea → anterior chamber → iris → pupil → lens → intraocular pressure → angle → fundus. |
| **Difference between A and B** | A table with the same rows for both: age, onset, symptoms, the key sign, the key test, treatment. |
| **Investigations** | In order: confirm → stage or grade → find the cause → baseline for follow-up → fitness for surgery. Purpose of each. |
| **Management** | Aim → medical → laser → surgery → follow-up and counselling, first person, with the trigger for each step up. |
| **A drug** | Class → strength, dose and frequency → mechanism of action → adverse effects (ocular, then systemic) → contraindications. |
| **An operation or laser** | Indications → principle → key steps (numbered) → advantages → disadvantages and complications. |
| **Complications** | Grouped by time (during surgery / early / late) or site (ocular / systemic). Most common and most serious first. |
| **"Why did you ask / check…?"** | 1. The reason, in one sentence. 2. What a positive answer would mean. 3. The test that would confirm it. |
| **Prognosis** | Good factors, then poor factors. |

### 2.4 Definitions and medical terms
- **Every card starts its "What you must know" part with the standard definition** of its condition, from the newest
  book that defines it, keeping that book's key terms exactly. The fact-check compares it with the book.
- **Related terms.** After the main definition, define the 3–6 related terms an examiner may ask, one line each (for a
  glaucoma card: ocular hypertension, normal-tension glaucoma, glaucoma suspect, target pressure).
- **Explain once**, in brackets, the first time: "**bayoneting** (double angulation of a vessel)". Never replace the term.
- **Exact names**, spelled and dashed as the books do: "Hodapp–Parrish–Anderson", "Van Herick", "relative afferent
  pupillary defect".

### 2.5 Keywords the examiner listens for
- **Every card carries a line** `**Keywords the examiner listens for:** term · term · term` — 8–15 exact terms for a long
  case, 6–10 for any other card: the named signs, grading systems, key numbers, investigations and operations the books
  use for this case. Long cards: last line of "At a glance". Short cards: right after "Spot". Task, chart, toolkit and
  viva cards: right after item 1.
- **Each keyword appears in at least one say-it script or viva answer** on the card (a script checks this).
- **Keyword first.** In viva answers, open with the keyword in bold: "**Bayoneting** is…", not "It is called bayoneting
  when…".

### 2.6 Simple English, professional register
- **Short sentences:** at most 20 words, one idea each (a quoted book definition may be longer).
- **Plain words:** "use, start, stop, because, about, show, need, before, after" — never "utilise, initiate,
  discontinue, owing to, approximately, demonstrate, necessitate, prior to, following".
- **Active voice:** "Laser iridotomy opens a new path for aqueous".
- **Write it out:** no "e.g.", "i.e.", "etc." or "viz."; write "for example", "that is", or finish the list.
- **No filler:** never "basically", "simply", "it is important to note that".
- **Findings** in the present tense, third person: "The right eye shows…", "Intraocular pressure is 18 mm Hg".
- **Management** in the first person: "I will start…", "I would advise…".
- **Numbers** carry units, the method and, where it matters, the time (intraocular pressure by Goldmann applanation at
  10 am).
- No casual words, no exclamation marks, no questions inside answers.

**Model answers** (format; verify any fact you reuse):
- Q: What is glaucoma? A: **Glaucoma** is a chronic, progressive optic neuropathy with irreversible optic-nerve damage and
  matching visual field defects, with or without raised intraocular pressure. In plain words: the optic nerve is slowly
  and permanently damaged, and the field of vision shrinks to match. The diagnosis rests on the **optic disc**, the
  **visual field** and the **angle**.
- Q: Tell me about latanoprost. A: **Latanoprost** is a **prostaglandin analogue**, used as 0.005% drops once at bedtime.
  It lowers intraocular pressure by **increasing uveoscleral outflow**. Side effects: conjunctival hyperaemia, longer
  lashes and iris pigmentation. Avoid it in uveitis.

### 2.7 The read-and-answer test (run by the examiner, pass 2)
1. Write 10 questions an examiner is likely to ask on this case that are **not** already on the card; at least 3 are
   follow-ups on the history or the examination.
2. Answer each using **only** the card.
3. For each one the card cannot answer, add the missing fact to the right section, or add the question with its answer
   — from the books.
4. Record the 10 questions and the result in `Case_Cards/_work/checks/<ID>_review.md`.

### 2.8 Other writing rules
- **British spelling**: oedema, haemorrhage, anaemia, tumour, paediatric, haemoglobin, oesophagus, centre, colour, fibre,
  ischaemia, anaesthesia, leucocyte. The books use American spelling — convert it.
- **Abbreviations**: expand each at its first use on the card, "intraocular pressure (IOP)". After that it is fine in
  tables and notes. **Never any abbreviation inside a say-it script** (`:::say`).
- **Classifications**: name the system and give its grades (ISGEO, Shaffer, Spaeth, Van Herick,
  Hodapp–Parrish–Anderson, Scheie). Follow CONSISTENCY.md where it has decided one.
- **Bold** only the words that earn marks. **No book names or page numbers in the body** (only `@readmore`).
- **Patients**: never a real name or hospital number. "Mr X, a 65-year-old man…".
- En dash for ranges (10–21 mm Hg), "mm Hg" with a space, "µm", "°", "×". No emoji except the ★ in the badge line.

## 2A. WHAT EVERY CARD MUST ANSWER — the candidate's five asks

| # | The ask | What the card must contain | Where (§4) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Positive and negative history, with the justification for each question | Every question with its reason. Every negative-history question names what it rules out, and the bedside test that tells the look-alikes apart | Long A3 · Short B5 · Fundus C5 |
| 2 | The must-do ocular examination, and the viva questions each step brings, with answers | The steps in the order performed; for each, how to do it, how to record it, and its 2–4 viva questions answered | Long A4 · Short B3 · Fundus C2–C3 · Task D3 |
| 3 | Which differentials to think of, and how to tell each apart | A table with the clinching sign or test for each | Long A6 · Short B6 · Fundus C7 |
| 4 | How to proceed: which investigations, what each involves, and its significance | Investigations in order, each with how it is done, the expected finding and what it changes | Long A7 · Short B7 · Fundus C8 |
| 5 | How to manage: drugs (mechanism, adverse effects, contraindications) → combinations → laser → surgery (each with advantages and disadvantages) | A first-person ladder with the trigger for each step up; drug and procedure tables; full profiles on the toolkit card | Long A8 · Short B8 · Fundus C9 · Toolkit F |

**History (ask 1).**
- **One question per row.** Never lump: "no redness, watering or pain" is three questions with three reasons.
- **Positive history**: onset, duration, progression, laterality, associated symptoms, treatment so far — and what each
  answer tells you (cause, chronicity, stage, complication).
- **Negative history**: for each question, what a "yes" would point to (the conditions it rules out), and where the books
  give one, the bedside sign or test that tells those look-alikes apart.
- **Past, drug, treatment, personal and family history**: the consequence for diagnosis or treatment (asthma → no
  beta-blocker). Treatment history records drug, strength, frequency, duration, compliance and side effects.
- **Minimum list**: the department's starred negative-history lines (transcripts), then what the typed formats and the
  books add.

Model row (checked against Aravind 4.6):

| Question | A "yes" would point to | How to tell them apart |
|---|---|---|
| Any coloured haloes? | **Corneal epithelial oedema** from a pressure rise: angle closure. Other causes of haloes: mucus on the cornea in conjunctivitis, incipient cataract, vitreous opacities, snow blindness, a tilted intraocular lens | **Fincham (stenopaeic slit) test**: a slit is moved across the pupil. The glaucoma halo stays intact; the halo of incipient cataract breaks into its component colours |

**Examination (ask 2).**
- Steps a candidate **must perform**, numbered **in the order performed** — the order earns marks: pupils before any
  drop; tonometry before gonioscopy and dilatation; the disc after dilatation.
- Each step has three parts: **Do** (how, 1–3 lines, including what to tell the patient) · **Record** (the department's
  shorthand, "VH grade IV (ND)", "Tn by GAT 18 mm Hg at 10 am") · **Viva** (2–4 Q/A: why it is done, what a finding
  means, the principle, the grading).
- A step's viva questions live with that step only — never repeated in the viva section.
- The RE | LE table stays: the steps say *how*, the table says *what*.

**Differentials (ask 3).** Table: Condition · Points for · Points against · How to tell apart — 4–6 rows, most likely
first. The last column names a sign the candidate can show or a test result — never "clinical features". Under the
table, one line: the differential to exclude first, and how.

**Investigations (ask 4).** Table: Test — what I do · Why · Expected finding in this case · Significance — what it
changes. Order: confirm → grade or stage → find the cause → baseline for follow-up → fitness for surgery. "What I do"
gives the essentials (protocol, specimen, scan). Close with a spoken line: "I will proceed with … to confirm …, then … to
stage …".

**Management (ask 5)** — the first-person ladder, aim first, each step with its trigger:
1. **Medical** — every drug with name, strength, route, frequency; a one-line mechanism, the main adverse effects
   (ocular and systemic) and contraindications (short form; full profile on the toolkit card); spell out any
   contraindication that applies to this patient.
2. **Combinations** — which, why (simpler dosing, adherence, less preservative), disadvantages.
3. **Laser** and 4. **Surgery** — table: Procedure (indication in this case) · Key steps · Advantages · Disadvantages and
   complications.
5. **Follow-up and counselling.**
- **Escalation triggers** — the numbers or events that move treatment up a step, as the books give them.
- **Viva coverage** — every drug and procedure on the ladder appears in at least one viva question.

## 3. THE CARD FILE FORMAT (a small Markdown dialect — a script turns it into Word/PDF, so follow it exactly)

Header lines (each on its own line, at the top, in this order):

    @card G1
    @title Primary open-angle glaucoma, including the glaucomatous optic disc
    @badge ★ Kept 6 times at JEH last year · Long case and short case
    @kind long
    @readmore Baidya p.152–167 · Namrata p.171–178 · Aravind 4.8, 4.15

- `@kind` is one of: `long` (long case or long+short), `short`, `fundus`, `task`, `chart`, `toolkit`, `viva`
  (`index` only for the 00 file).
- `@readmore` uses **printed** page numbers (the packet header of every page gives "fitz N = printed p.M").
  Aravind is cited by section number (4.8, 4.15 …).

Body syntax:

    ## Section heading            ← one of the template headings below, exactly as written
    ### Sub-heading               ← optional, inside a section
    Plain line                    ← a paragraph (consecutive lines join into one paragraph; blank line ends it)
    - bullet                      ← bullet; "  - sub-bullet" (two spaces) for one nested level
    1. step                       ← numbered list (write 1. 2. 3. …)
    **bold**  *italic*            ← inline emphasis
    @widths 22 38 40              ← OPTIONAL line directly before a table: column widths in %, must sum to 100
    | Col A | Col B | Col C |     ← table: header row, then separator row, then body rows
    |---|---|---|
    | cell | cell<br>second line | cell |     ← <br> = line break inside a cell
    Q: question text              ← viva question (always followed by an A: line)
    A: answer text (1–3 lines)
    :::say                        ← opens a shaded "say-it" box (spoken script)
    ...paragraphs/bullets...
    :::                           ← closes the box
    :::trap  …  :::               ← red-edged box (use only for the Examiner traps section body)
    :::recall  …  :::             ← green box (use only for the Quick recall section body)
    :::short  …  :::              ← light box (optional; v4 short-case blocks use ### + :::say instead)
    :::gonio                      ← gonioscopy cross diagram (draws two crosses, right eye and left eye)
    RE | S=III SS | T=III SS | I=IV CBB | N=III SS
    LE | S=II TM | T=III SS | I=III SS | N=III SS
    caption: Modified Shaffer grading — deepest structure seen in each quadrant
    :::

Rules: no other Markdown (no `#` single-hash headings, no `>` quotes, no HTML except `<br>` in table cells, no
horizontal rules, no code blocks, no footnotes). Every table row must have the same number of cells as its header.
Keep table cells short (they render at 9 pt). A table may have at most 4 columns.

Department teaching slides (DigiNerve, Case Sheet Proforma PDF pages 10–13) — the description scripts to copy:
- **AC depth, pen-torch method**: location of torch (temporally) → area of iris illuminated (whole iris = grade 4,
  normal depth; 2/3 of iris = grade 2, shallow) → grade.
- **AC depth, Van Herick technique**: slit-thin beam 1 mm inside the temporal limbus at a 60° angle → ratio of
  corneal thickness to the gap between posterior cornea and anterior iris (1:1 → grade 4, normal depth, wide open angle;
  1:1/4 → grade 2, shallow, occludable angle) → grade.
- **Bleb**: height (flat / medium …) · clarity (translucent / opaque) · horizontal extent (clock hours) · location over
  limbus (superiorly) · vascularity (avascular / highly vascular with corkscrew vessels) · microcysts (present/absent) ·
  subconjunctival fibrosis · sutures. "Suggestive of a good functioning bleb" vs "suggestive of a failing bleb".
- **Peripheral iridotomy/iridectomy**: margin (ragged irregular edges = laser; sharp well-defined edges = surgical) ·
  site (clock position) · surrounding iris (hypopigmented with pigment dispersion after laser) · retro-illumination
  test for patency (light reflex seen = patent).
- **Neovascularisation of iris**: appearance (thin) · character (friable) · course (arborising) · arrangement
  (irregular) · extent (clock hours).
- **Pseudoexfoliation — four points**: colour of material (white) · nature (flaky, dandruff-like) · location (pupillary
  margin as tufts; anterior lens capsule as a disc-shaped deposit) · with or without iris atrophy.
- **Glaucomatous disc**: size · shape of disc (vertically oval) · shape of cup · margins · colour · cup–disc ratio ·
  neuroretinal rim · location and nature of vessels (centrally arising, dichotomously branching) · arteriovenous ratio
  (2:3) · peripapillary changes (peripapillary atrophy) · RNFL defects (superior and inferior wedge-shaped).
