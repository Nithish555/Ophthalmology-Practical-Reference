## At a glance
- **Age-related macular degeneration (AMD)**: a progressive degeneration of the macula after 50 years. Early disease shows drusen and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) changes; late disease is **geographic atrophy (GA, dry)** or **choroidal neovascularisation (CNV, wet)**.
- Dry (non-neovascular) AMD is 85–90% of cases, but wet (neovascular) AMD causes most of the severe visual loss.
- Kept because the signs are stable for years: drusen, GA and **disciform scars** do not change before the exam, and the patient has a real low-vision problem to discuss.
- **S.No 36**: "Both eyes late (end-stage) neovascular age-related macular degeneration with disciform scars and scattered soft drusen."
- **S.No 1**: "Both eyes age-related macular degeneration, non-neovascular, intermediate stage (large soft drusen with pigmentary change)" — stage each eye separately if one has GA or a scar.
- **S.No 8**: "Left eye disciform macular scar, probably end-stage neovascular AMD" — a one-eyed scar must be argued; fellow-eye drusen support AMD, a myopic or traumatic fundus does not.

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
Mrs X, a 75-year-old former smoker with hypertension, has gradual painless loss of central vision in both eyes over 4 years, with distortion before the blur; she has had no injections. Best-corrected visual acuity is 3/60 and N36 in the right eye and 5/60 and N24 in the left, with a central scotoma on the Amsler grid in each eye; the anterior segments show early nuclear sclerosis and the intraocular pressures are normal. Each macula has a white-yellow, raised subfoveal fibrous scar about 2 disc diameters across with pigment at its edge, and scattered large soft drusen around it. There is no fresh blood, fluid or exudate at the margins, and the discs and periphery show no myopic, traumatic or inflammatory signs. My diagnosis is both eyes late (end-stage) neovascular age-related macular degeneration with disciform scars and scattered soft drusen. I would confirm that the scars are inactive on OCT, give AREDS2 supplements and smoking advice, prescribe low-vision aids, and reassure her that side vision will remain.

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Define AMD.
A: A degenerative disorder of the macula in people over 50 years, with drusen and RPE pigmentary change, progressing to GA or CNV, in the absence of another cause; visual acuity is not part of the definition.

Q: What are the risk factors?
A: Age (strongest); smoking (doubles the risk, dose-related); family history and genes, especially complement factor H (CFH, chromosome 1q31) and the ARMS2–HTRA1 region (10q26); white race; hypertension and cardiovascular disease; obesity and high-fat diet; light iris; possibly sunlight and cataract surgery.

Q: What are drusen and how are they sized?
A: Extracellular deposits between the RPE basement membrane and the inner collagenous layer of Bruch's membrane (lipid, complement, apolipoprotein E). Small (hard) ≤63 µm, medium 63–125 µm, large >125 µm, judged against a major vein at the disc margin (about 125 µm). Soft drusen are ill-defined and coalesce; reticular pseudodrusen lie above the RPE and carry a high risk.

Q: Give the Beckman clinical classification.
A: Lesions within 2 disc diameters (DD) of the fovea: no ageing change (no drusen); normal ageing (only drupelets ≤63 µm); early AMD (medium drusen, no pigment change); intermediate AMD (large drusen or any AMD pigmentary change); late AMD (neovascular AMD or any GA).

Q: Give the AREDS categories.
A: Category 1, none or few small drusen; 2, early (many small or few intermediate drusen, mild RPE change); 3, intermediate (extensive intermediate drusen, one or more large drusen, or GA sparing the foveal centre); 4, advanced (central GA or neovascular AMD in one eye).

Q: Outline the pathogenesis.
A: Ageing RPE fails to clear outer-segment debris; lipofuscin builds up; basal deposits and drusen thicken Bruch's membrane and block diffusion; complement-driven inflammation and oxidative stress cause RPE and choriocapillaris loss (GA); hypoxic RPE releases vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and new vessels breach Bruch's membrane (CNV).

### Examination and signs
Q: How do you examine the macula?
A: Distance and near acuity, Amsler grid each eye, then stereoscopic slit-lamp biomicroscopy with a +90 D or +78 D lens, red-free light for drusen, indirect ophthalmoscopy for the periphery, and always the fellow eye.

Q: Describe the Amsler grid.
A: A 10 cm square of 5 mm boxes held at about 30 cm; each box subtends 1°, so it tests the central 10° from fixation. Wavy lines mean metamorphopsia and a missing area means a scotoma; it is given for weekly home checks of the better eye.

Q: What is a disciform scar?
A: The end stage of neovascular AMD: a white-yellow, raised subretinal fibrovascular scar under the fovea with pigment clumps and surrounding atrophy. Vision is usually 6/60 or worse with a dense central scotoma, but the peripheral field is kept, so the patient never becomes totally blind.

Q: What is the differential diagnosis of a disciform scar?
A: Myopic CNV (Fuchs spot, lacquer cracks, staphyloma, younger); polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV: Asians, orange nodules, peripapillary, massive bleeds); presumed ocular histoplasmosis-like picture (peripapillary atrophy, punched-out peripheral spots, no vitritis; in India usually multifocal choroiditis or punctate inner choroidopathy); traumatic choroidal rupture (crescent concentric to the disc); angioid streaks; old toxoplasma scar; end-stage Best disease.

Q: What are the types of PED?
A: Drusenoid (confluent soft drusen, no CNV); serous (orange dome, sharp edge, transilluminates); fibrovascular (irregular, means occult type 1 CNV); haemorrhagic (dark dome from CNV or PCV).

Q: What is an RPE tear?
A: A rip at the edge of a large vascularised PED, spontaneous or after anti-VEGF injection or PDT: a sharply bordered bare-choroid area beside a dark scrolled flap; FFA shows a bright bare area and blocked fluorescence from the flap.

### Investigations
Q: What does OCT show?
A: Dry: drusen as RPE bumps; GA as loss of RPE and outer retina with signal passing into the choroid (hypertransmission). Wet: intraretinal fluid, subretinal fluid, PED and subretinal hyperreflective material; type 1 CNV lies under the RPE, type 2 above it. A disciform scar is a dense hyperreflective subretinal mass with outer retinal loss and no fluid when inactive.

Q: Describe FFA in classic and occult CNV.
A: Classic: well-defined lacy hyperfluorescence in early transit, leaking progressively so the borders blur late. Occult: fibrovascular PED with stippled hyperfluorescence at 1–2 minutes and late leakage, or late leakage of undetermined source. Drusen stain, GA gives a window defect, and a scar stains late.

Q: When is ICGA needed?
A: The infrared, protein-bound dye sees through blood and pigment: polyps with a branching vascular network in PCV (gold standard), a hot spot and retinal–choroidal anastomosis in retinal angiomatous proliferation (RAP), occult CNV as a plaque or hot spot, and CNV hidden by haemorrhage.

Q: What do OCTA and FAF add?
A: OCTA shows flow in the neovascular network (sea-fan or medusa shapes) without dye but cannot show leakage. FAF maps lipofuscin: GA is dark, and a bright rim around it predicts faster growth.

Q: Classify CNV by type and location.
A: Type 1 sub-RPE (occult), type 2 subretinal (classic), type 3 intraretinal (RAP); PCV is an aneurysmal type 1 variant. Location: extrafoveal 200 µm or more from the foveal centre, juxtafoveal 1–199 µm, subfoveal.

### Management
Q: What are the AREDS and AREDS2 formulations?
A: AREDS: vitamin C 500 mg, vitamin E 400 international units (IU), beta-carotene 15 mg, zinc oxide 80 mg, cupric oxide 2 mg daily; cut progression to advanced AMD by about 25% and moderate visual loss by 19% over 5 years. AREDS2 replaced beta-carotene (lung cancer in smokers) with lutein 10 mg and zeaxanthin 2 mg; omega-3 added nothing and zinc 25 mg worked as well.

Q: Who should take them?
A: Only AREDS category 3, or category 4 in one eye; so S.No 8 and 36 (late AMD in at least one eye) qualify. There is no benefit in early AMD or normal eyes.

Q: Name the anti-VEGF drugs and doses.
A: Intravitreal, 3.5–4 mm behind the limbus: ranibizumab 0.5 mg/0.05 mL; bevacizumab 1.25 mg/0.05 mL (off-label, cheap); aflibercept 2 mg/0.05 mL (high-dose 8 mg); brolucizumab 6 mg/0.05 mL; faricimab 6 mg/0.05 mL (blocks VEGF-A and angiopoietin-2). Pegaptanib 0.3 mg is historical.

Q: What regimens are used?
A: Three monthly loading injections, then either as needed (pro re nata, PRN, with monthly OCT) or treat-and-extend, lengthening the interval by 2 weeks while the macula stays dry, up to 12–16 weeks; aflibercept 2 mg is given every 8 weeks after loading.

Q: Name the landmark wet AMD trials.
A: MARINA (occult or minimally classic, ranibizumab versus sham) and ANCHOR (predominantly classic, ranibizumab versus PDT): about 95% kept vision and a third gained 15 letters. CATT and IVAN: bevacizumab equal to ranibizumab. VIEW 1 and 2: aflibercept every 8 weeks equal to monthly ranibizumab. TENAYA and LUCERNE: faricimab up to every 16 weeks.

Q: Describe PDT.
A: Verteporfin 6 mg/m² of body surface area infused over 10 minutes; 15 minutes from the start, a 689 nm diode laser at 600 mW/cm² for 83 seconds (50 J/cm²) over a spot 1,000 µm larger than the lesion; it closes new vessels by photothrombosis. Avoid sunlight for 48 hours; contraindicated in porphyria. Now used mainly with anti-VEGF for PCV.

Q: What are the complications of intravitreal injection?
A: Endophthalmitis (about 0.02–0.05% per injection), IOP spike, lens touch and cataract, retinal tear or detachment, vitreous haemorrhage, RPE tear, subconjunctival haemorrhage; inflammation and occlusive vasculitis with brolucizumab; a theoretical stroke and heart-attack risk.

Q: How do you manage a large submacular haemorrhage?
A: Within 1–2 weeks: pneumatic displacement with intravitreal expansile gas (sulphur hexafluoride or perfluoropropane), with or without tissue plasminogen activator, and face-down posture; or vitrectomy with subretinal tissue plasminogen activator and gas; then anti-VEGF for the underlying CNV.

Q: How do you treat a disciform scar?
A: The scar itself cannot be reversed; treat an active edge (fluid or fresh blood on OCT) with anti-VEGF; otherwise low-vision rehabilitation, AREDS2 and Amsler monitoring for the fellow eye, smoking cessation and disability certification.

Q: Which low-vision aids help?
A: Near: high-plus reading spectacles, hand-held, stand and illuminated magnifiers, closed-circuit television or electronic magnifiers, large print and good lighting. Distance: telescopes. Training in eccentric viewing to a preferred retinal locus. An implantable miniature telescope suits stable bilateral end-stage disease.

Q: Is there treatment for GA?
A: Nothing restores vision. Intravitreal complement inhibitors, pegcetacoplan (C3) and avacincaptad pegol (C5), approved in the United States in 2023, slow GA growth by about 15–20% with monthly or alternate-month injections; risks are conversion to wet AMD and, with pegcetacoplan, occlusive retinal vasculitis. Lampalizumab failed.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: What predicts progression to late AMD?
A: Large drusen, pigment change, drusenoid PED, reticular pseudodrusen, late AMD in the fellow eye and smoking. On the AREDS simple scale (1 point per eye each for large drusen and pigment change), 5-year risk is 0.5%, 3%, 12%, 25% and 50% for 0 to 4 points.

Q: What is the prognosis of wet AMD?
A: Untreated, most eyes reach 6/60 or worse within 2 years and end as a disciform scar. With prompt anti-VEGF, about 90–95% keep their vision; results depend on starting acuity, lesion size, fibrosis and adherence. When one eye already has late AMD, the fellow eye has roughly a 40% chance of late AMD over 5 years.

Q: What is Charles Bonnet syndrome?
A: Vivid, formed visual hallucinations in a mentally normal person with severe visual loss, common with bilateral late AMD; the patient knows they are not real, and explanation and reassurance are the treatment.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: What is PCV and why does it matter in India?
A: Aneurysmal polyps at the end of a branching vascular network in the inner choroid, common in Asians; orange subretinal nodules, serosanguineous PEDs and massive subretinal haemorrhage; ICGA is diagnostic; treat with anti-VEGF with or without PDT (EVEREST II).

Q: What is RAP?
A: Type 3 neovascularisation starting in the deep retinal capillary plexus; elderly, often bilateral; small intraretinal haemorrhages, oedema and soft drusen; ICGA hot spot with a hairpin retinal–choroidal anastomosis; responds to anti-VEGF.

Q: Why has OCT replaced FFA for follow-up?
A: It is quick, non-invasive and measures fluid at every visit to decide retreatment; FFA is kept for diagnosis, unexplained visual loss, and with ICGA for suspected PCV or RAP.

Q: What is new in wet AMD?
A: Faricimab and aflibercept 8 mg for longer intervals, biosimilar ranibizumab and aflibercept, the port delivery system for ranibizumab, gene therapy for continuous anti-VEGF production, and home OCT or hyperacuity monitoring.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Calling every macular scar AMD: check age, refraction, disc, periphery and fellow-eye drusen first (S.No 8 is one-eyed).
- Describing drusen as "soft" or "hard" without size against the 125 µm vein.
- Missing fresh blood or fluid at the edge of a scar: that edge is active and still treatable.
- Giving AREDS to early AMD, or beta-carotene to a smoker.
- Saying anti-VEGF will improve vision in a fibrotic disciform scar.
- Quoting the verteporfin dose per kilogram: it is 6 mg per square metre of body surface area.
- Forgetting near vision, the Amsler grid in each eye, and low-vision counselling.
:::
