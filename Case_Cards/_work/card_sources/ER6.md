## At a glance
- **What it is:** a full-thickness macular hole (FTMH) — a defect at the foveola interrupting every neural retinal layer from the internal limiting membrane (ILM) to the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE).
- **Cause:** mostly idiopathic (primary), from vitreofoveal traction during perifoveal posterior vitreous detachment (PVD); secondary holes follow trauma, high myopia, cystoid macular oedema (CME) and other disease.
- **Who:** women over 60 (about 2:1), prevalence about 3 per 1,000; the fellow eye develops a hole in 10–15% within 5 years.
- **Why examiners keep it:** a slit-lamp sign case (Watzke–Allen), two staging systems (Gass and the International Vitreomacular Traction Study, IVTS), and a surgery that now closes about 90% of holes.
- **S.No 38 (patient already here):** "Left eye idiopathic full-thickness macular hole, Gass stage __, IVTS small, medium or large (minimum diameter __ µm) with or without vitreomacular traction (VMT); right eye normal or with vitreomacular adhesion; lens status __."
- **Before you present:** know the duration of symptoms, the hole size on optical coherence tomography (OCT), the vitreous status, the lens, the fellow eye and whether she can posture.

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
Mrs X, 66, noticed distortion and then a central blur in her left eye over four months. Best-corrected vision is 6/6 in the right eye and 6/36 in the left, and the left lens shows early nuclear sclerosis. At the left macula there is a round, punched-out red defect about one-third of a disc diameter across, with a grey cuff of subretinal fluid and yellow dots in its base, and the Watzke–Allen test shows a broken beam. Optical coherence tomography shows a full-thickness defect with a minimum diameter of 450 micrometres, cystic edges and the posterior hyaloid still attached at the hole edge. The right macula is normal and both peripheries are healthy. She can lie face-down and has no travel plans.

**Diagnosis (S.No 38):** left eye idiopathic full-thickness macular hole, Gass stage 3, large with vitreomacular traction by the International Vitreomacular Traction Study classification, of about four months' duration; left eye early nuclear cataract; right eye normal.
:::

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Define a full-thickness macular hole.
A: A foveal lesion with full-thickness interruption of all neural retinal layers from the ILM to the RPE (IVTS definition). Idiopathic holes are round and caused by vitreofoveal traction.

Q: What are the risk factors?
A: Age over 60–65 and female sex (about 2:1) are the only proven systemic risks. Ocular: VMT in the eye, a hole in the other eye, high myopia, blunt trauma, eclipse exposure, CME.

Q: Explain the pathogenesis.
A: Gass blamed tangential traction of the prefoveal cortex. OCT shows that holes begin during perifoveal PVD: the vitreous stays stuck to the foveola and pulls anteroposteriorly, splitting the inner fovea into a pseudocyst (1A), breaking the outer retina (1B), then opening the roof (2). Tangential pull then retracts the edges centrifugally (3). Little tissue is lost, which is why closure restores vision.

Q: Give the Gass staging.
A: 1A — impending hole, yellow spot (foveolar detachment); 1B — occult hole, yellow ring; 2 — small full-thickness hole under 400 µm, often eccentric or crescentic; 3 — full-thickness hole of 400 µm or more with a cuff of fluid and an operculum, vitreous not fully detached; 4 — any full-thickness hole with complete PVD (Weiss ring).

Q: Give the IVTS OCT classification.
A: Vitreomacular adhesion (VMA) — perifoveal separation with foveal attachment within 3 mm and no retinal change; VMT — the same with distortion of the fovea; both focal (≤1500 µm) or broad (>1500 µm). FTMH — by minimum width: small ≤250 µm, medium >250–400 µm, large >400 µm; with or without VMT; primary or secondary. Gass 1 = VMT; 2 = small or medium FTMH with VMT; 3 = medium or large with VMT; 4 = FTMH without VMT.

Q: What is an operculum?
A: A disc of condensed vitreous cortex and glial tissue on the detached hyaloid over the hole; about 40% contain photoreceptor elements. Most are pseudo-opercula — the hole forms mainly by retraction, not tissue loss.

Q: What is a "stage 0" hole?
A: Vitreomacular adhesion in the fellow eye of a patient with a hole; it carries a higher risk of a hole forming than in an eye with complete PVD.

### Examination and signs
Q: Describe the Watzke–Allen test.
A: With a fundus contact lens or a 90 dioptre lens, a thin vertical then horizontal slit beam is placed across the centre of the lesion. A patient with an FTMH sees the line broken (gap); with a pseudohole, lamellar hole or cyst the line is intact but thinned or bent.

Q: What is the laser aiming beam test?
A: A 50 µm helium–neon aiming beam is placed in the hole: the patient cannot see it, but sees it again on surrounding retina. It is thought to be more sensitive and specific than Watzke–Allen.

Q: Give the differential diagnosis.
A: Lamellar macular hole, macular pseudohole with epiretinal membrane, CME with a large central cyst, VMT with foveal pseudocyst, solar retinopathy, central serous chorioretinopathy, foveal drusen, choroidal neovascularisation (CNV), macular atrophy and haemorrhage.

Q: How do lamellar hole and pseudohole differ from FTMH on OCT?
A: Lamellar — irregular inner foveal defect with intraretinal splitting between outer plexiform and outer nuclear layers, photoreceptors intact, often yellowish epiretinal proliferation. Pseudohole — epiretinal membrane with a central opening, steep heaped edges and near-normal foveal thickness, no tissue loss. Both lack a cuff of fluid, and Watzke–Allen is negative.

Q: What does the Amsler grid show?
A: Central distortion with lines bowed towards the centre and sometimes a small central scotoma; it documents early holes and monitors the fellow eye.

Q: What is the typical vision by stage?
A: Stage 1–2 about 6/9–6/24; stages 3–4 about 6/30–6/120. Vision worse than 6/60 is rare and should prompt a search for another cause.

### Investigations
Q: What does OCT measure, and which indices predict outcome?
A: Minimum linear diameter (narrowest point, used for IVTS size), base diameter at the RPE, height, VMT, epiretinal membrane and ellipsoid zone. Macular hole index (height ÷ base) ≥0.5, hole form factor (sum of arm lengths ÷ base) >0.9, and tractional hole index (height ÷ minimum diameter) >1.4 predict closure and better vision; a minimum diameter under about 310 µm does well.

Q: What do fluorescein angiography and autofluorescence show?
A: Angiography: an early, well-defined window defect that does not enlarge and does not leak — this separates it from CME and CNV. Autofluorescence: a bright foveolar spot because the macular pigment that normally masks the RPE is displaced.

Q: Any other tests?
A: Microperimetry (dense central scotoma, fixation stability), multifocal electroretinography (reduced central response) and B-scan when media are hazy to judge the vitreous.

Q: How is closure described on postoperative OCT?
A: Type 1 — tissue bridges the hole (U-shaped best, then V); type 2 — edges flattened but bare RPE remains (W or "flat-open"), with poor vision and risk of reopening.

### Management
Q: How do you manage by stage?
A: Stage 1 (VMT) — observe with OCT and Amsler every 2–4 months; about half progress to a full-thickness hole, the rest abort when the vitreous releases. Stages 2–4 — vitrectomy. Selected small holes with VMT — pharmacological or pneumatic vitreolysis.

Q: What is ocriplasmin?
A: Recombinant truncated human plasmin; a single intravitreal 125 µg injection cleaves the vitreoretinal interface. MIVI-TRUST trials: VMT release in about 26% versus 10% with placebo; FTMH closure in about 40% of holes ≤400 µm with VMT. Best in small holes, focal adhesion, no epiretinal membrane. Side effects: transient vision loss, dyschromatopsia, electroretinogram changes; now little used.

Q: What is pneumatic vitreolysis?
A: An office intravitreal injection of 0.3 mL pure perfluoropropane (C3F8) gas to separate the vitreous from the fovea; it releases most VMT, but closes only a minority of full-thickness holes and carries a risk of retinal tears.

Q: Describe the operation.
A: Three-port 23–25 gauge pars plana vitrectomy (PPV); induction of PVD (triamcinolone to see the hyaloid, active suction); ILM staining with brilliant blue G 0.025–0.05%; pinch-and-peel of ILM 1–2 disc diameters around the hole; indented peripheral search and laser to breaks; fluid–air exchange; non-expansile gas (sulphur hexafluoride, SF6 20%, lasts about 2 weeks; or C3F8 14%, about 6–8 weeks); face-down positioning.

Q: Why peel the ILM?
A: It removes residual cortex and tangential traction, takes away the scaffold for epiretinal membrane, and stimulates glial repair of the hole. Closure rises to about 90–98% and late reopening falls.

Q: How long must the patient lie face-down?
A: Traditionally 7–10 days; most surgeons now ask for 3–5 days, longer for holes over 400 µm. Holes under 400 µm often close with simply avoiding lying on the back. Patients who cannot posture may be given silicone oil, but closure and vision are better with gas.

Q: What do you tell the patient about the gas?
A: No air travel, high-altitude trips or nitrous oxide anaesthesia until the bubble has gone — expansion raises the intraocular pressure and can occlude the central retinal artery. Vision is poor until the bubble falls below the visual axis.

Q: How do you manage large or failed holes?
A: Inverted ILM flap — a hinged flap of ILM is left at the edge and folded over or into the hole as a scaffold (closure about 98% versus 88% with plain peel in large holes). Others: free autologous ILM plug, lens capsule or amniotic membrane plug, autologous retinal graft, platelet concentrate, longer-acting gas.

Q: Would you combine cataract surgery?
A: In phakic patients over about 50, combined phacovitrectomy is reasonable because 80–98% develop nuclear cataract within a few years; aim slightly myopic, since gas causes a small myopic shift. Cataract surgery later can reopen a closed hole, especially if CME develops.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: List the complications of macular hole surgery.
A: Cataract (commonest); raised intraocular pressure from gas; peripheral breaks (3–17%) and retinal detachment (RD; 1–5%, usually inferior); visual field loss (10–20% in older series, from air infusion drying the nerve fibre layer); reopening (2–10%); dye toxicity (indocyanine green); endophthalmitis (rare).

Q: What predicts the visual outcome?
A: Hole size (minimum diameter under 400 µm, and especially ≤250 µm, best), duration under 6 months, better preoperative vision, intact ellipsoid zone, favourable OCT indices, idiopathic rather than traumatic or myopic cause, and closure at the first operation.

Q: What results do you quote?
A: Anatomical closure about 90–100% for recent idiopathic holes; vision improves in 80–90%, and about 65% reach 6/12 or better; up to 10% lose vision.

Q: What is the risk to the fellow eye?
A: About 10–15% over 5 years; very low once that eye has a complete PVD, higher with VMA or VMT on OCT. Teach Amsler monitoring and repeat OCT.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: How is a traumatic macular hole different?
A: Young patient after blunt trauma (sudden vitreous traction and contusion); irregular, often larger, PVD often absent; commotio or choroidal rupture may coexist. Many close on their own within 3–6 months, so observe first, then operate (PPV, ILM peel or flap, gas).

Q: What is special about a myopic macular hole?
A: It arises in a posterior staphyloma with foveoschisis; it may lead to macular hole RD, especially with axial length over 30 mm. The ILM is stiff and the staphyloma resists closure, so an inverted flap, longer tamponade or silicone oil, or a macular buckle is used.

Q: What is the CLOSE surgical size grading?
A: A newer surgical extension of the IVTS sizes: large 400–550 µm, extra-large 550–800 µm, giant over 800 µm — peel or flap for large, inverted flap for extra-large, and other techniques (retinal graft, hydrodissection) for giant holes.

Q: Which dye stains which tissue?
A: Brilliant blue G (0.025–0.05%) — ILM; trypan blue (0.15%) — epiretinal membrane; triamcinolone — vitreous; indocyanine green stains ILM well but is the most toxic to RPE and nerve fibres.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Calling every red foveal spot a hole — do the Watzke–Allen test and OCT; a pseudohole with epiretinal membrane is the commonest mimic.
- Mixing Gass stages with IVTS size: IVTS uses the minimum linear diameter at its narrowest point, not the base diameter.
- Defining stage 4 by size — stage 4 means complete PVD with a Weiss ring, whatever the size.
- Offering laser to the hole itself; laser is only for peripheral breaks.
- Forgetting posture, air travel, nitrous oxide and the one-eyed patient before advising gas.
- Missing the fellow eye and not warning a phakic patient that cataract will follow.
- Accepting vision worse than 6/60 as "just the hole" without looking for another cause.
:::
