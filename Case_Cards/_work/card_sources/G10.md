## What it is
- The **Humphrey field analyser** does **static automated threshold perimetry**: a dim, still light is brightened at each point until it is seen. It is the **gold standard** for diagnosing and following glaucoma.
- **Threshold** is the intensity seen **50%** of the time. A higher threshold means lower sensitivity.
- The stimulus is **Goldmann size III, white**, on a **31.5 apostilb (asb)** background. Sensitivity is in **decibels (dB)**: **0 dB = 10,000 asb** (brightest), **40 dB = 1 asb**. Higher dB = more sensitive.
- Programmes: **24-2** (54 points) and **30-2** (76 points) on a **6°** grid; **10-2** (68 points, **2°** grid) for advanced glaucoma.
- **Swedish interactive threshold algorithm (SITA)** takes about half the time, with little or no loss of accuracy.

**Keywords the examiner listens for:** reliability indices · pattern deviation · glaucoma hemifield test · mean deviation · pattern standard deviation · visual field index · Bjerrum's area · arcuate scotoma · nasal step · Anderson and Patella criteria

## Reading order
Read zone by zone, then conclude. Judge reliability before you name any defect.
1. **Zone 1, patient data** (top strip): eye and age. A wrong age compares him with the wrong normals.
2. **Zone 2, test conditions**: programme, strategy, pupil (dilate if under 2 mm), visual acuity, correcting lens.
3. **Zone 3, reliability indices** (top left): fixation losses, false positives, false negatives, foveal threshold.
4. **Zone 4, grey scale**: a picture of the raw values, without statistics; for orientation only.
5. **Zone 5, raw data**: threshold in dB at each point; 0 dB is an absolute scotoma.
6. **Zone 6, deviation plots** (lower half; numbers above, probability symbols below). **Total deviation** = patient minus age-matched normal. **Pattern deviation** removes overall depression, leaving localised defects: name the defect here.
7. **Zone 7, glaucoma hemifield test**: five sectors above the horizontal raphe against their mirror images below.
8. **Zone 8, global indices**: mean deviation (MD), pattern standard deviation (PSD), visual field index (VFI).
9. **Conclude**: the defect, the criteria, the stage, and the matching disc or nerve fibre layer damage. A superior defect matches the inferior pole.

## Findings to name
@widths 36 64
| Finding | What it means |
|---|---|
| Fixation losses 20% or more | Unsteady fixation: unreliable |
| False positives over 15% | A "trigger-happy" patient; white scotomas on the grey scale; unreliable |
| False negatives over 33% | Fatigue, inattention or advanced glaucoma; a clover-leaf grey scale; unreliable |
| Low foveal threshold, good acuity | Wrong optical correction |
| Total deviation depressed, pattern deviation normal | Uniform depression: cataract, media opacity, small pupil, uncorrected refraction |
| Cluster on the pattern deviation plot | A localised defect, typical of glaucoma |
| Hemifield test outside normal limits (p<1%) or borderline (p<3%) | Upper and lower halves differ, as in glaucoma. "General reduction of sensitivity" = diffuse loss |
| MD more negative | Overall loss; normal is typically within 2 dB of 0 |
| PSD high | Focal loss; a low PSD means no loss or diffuse loss |
| VFI | Percentage of a normal field for age (100 normal, 0 blind). Built from pattern deviation, so cataract does not affect it |

### Glaucomatous defects, in order of appearance
They begin in **Bjerrum's area** (10–25° from fixation) and respect the **horizontal meridian**.
@widths 36 64
| Defect | What it is |
|---|---|
| Generalised depression | The earliest change, but not diagnostic |
| Baring of the blind spot | Early, very non-specific |
| Paracentral scotoma | The earliest clinically significant defect; usually superonasal, ending at the horizontal midline |
| Seidel's scotoma | A paracentral scotoma joined to the blind spot: sickle-shaped |
| Arcuate (Bjerrum's) scotoma | From the blind spot, arching above or below fixation to the horizontal raphe |
| Ring (double arcuate) scotoma | Superior and inferior arcuates joined |
| Nasal (Roenne's) step | A right-angled step at the horizontal meridian, nasally |
| Dense hemifield (altitudinal) defect | A dense upper or lower half, with deep focal notching of the rim. Also the commonest defect of ischaemic optic neuropathy |
| Tubular vision and temporal island | End stage: only a central and a temporal island remain |

## Grading or classification
### Is it glaucomatous? Anderson and Patella criteria
Shown on at least **2 consecutive reliable fields**:
- A cluster of **3 or more contiguous non-edge points** on the 30-2 pattern deviation plot, all at **p<5%**, one at **p<1%**.
- **PSD** (or corrected PSD) at **p<5%**.
- **Glaucoma hemifield test outside normal limits**.

### How severe?

| Parameter | Early | Moderate | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| MD | **−6 dB or better** | **−6 to −12 dB** | **−12 dB or worse** |
| Pattern deviation points at p<5% / p<1% | <18 / <10 | <37 / <20 | >37 / >20 |
| Central 5° points under 15 dB | None | One hemifield | Both hemifields |
| Central 10° | Spared | Spared | **Involved** |

## What each finding leads to (management)
- **Unreliable**: do not diagnose. Re-instruct and repeat, because the criteria need 2 reliable fields.
- **Uniform depression, normal pattern deviation**: correct the refraction, dilate a pupil under 2 mm, look for cataract, then repeat.
- **Criteria met, matching the disc**: a glaucomatous field defect. Stage it and set the target pressure (card G1).
- **Central 10° involved**: follow with serial **10-2** fields; a **size V** stimulus shows the macular split.
- **Serial fields**: progression is a worsening MD or VFI, a deeper or wider scotoma, or a new defect matching the disc. Trend and glaucoma progression analyses show it. Progression on maximal medical therapy is an indication for trabeculectomy.
- **How often**: every 6 months in mild to moderate glaucoma, every 3 months in advanced glaucoma.
- **Does not fit** (respects the vertical meridian, no matching disc, pallor more than cupping, rapid progression): think of retinal or neurological disease; order neuroimaging.

## Viva questions
Q: Why name the defect on the pattern deviation plot?
A: **Pattern deviation** shifts total deviation so its 7th best point reads 0. This removes generalised depression and unmasks localised defects.
Q: How does a cataract change the printout?
A: Total deviation and **mean deviation** fall, but pattern deviation stays near normal. **Pattern standard deviation** stays low; the **visual field index** is unaffected.
Q: What does p<2% mean?
A: Fewer than 2% of normal people have such low sensitivity at that point. It shows significance, not depth.
Q: How do glaucomatous and neurological defects differ?
A: Glaucomatous defects respect the horizontal meridian (a **nasal step**), and are relative and incongruous. Neurological defects respect the vertical meridian, and are absolute and mostly congruous.
Q: What else causes an arcuate scotoma?
A: An **arcuate scotoma** also occurs with myopic peripapillary atrophy, disc drusen, optic pit, ischaemic optic neuropathy and pituitary adenoma.
Q: Why is the superior field hit first?
A: Inferior arcuate fibres are more susceptible. Central vision goes last: its fibres arise closest to the disc and exit its centre.
Q: What is Bjerrum's area?
A: **Bjerrum's area** is the central 25° above and below fixation. Glaucomatous defects begin 10–25° from fixation.
Q: Which changes mean progression?
A: A reproducible fall of over 7 dB in a scotoma (deepening), over 9 dB beside it (enlarging), or over 11 dB at a normal point (new).
Q: False positives are 20%. What will you do?
A: Over 15% is unreliable: a trigger-happy patient with white scotomas. I re-instruct and repeat.
Q: What is a decibel in perimetry?
A: 0.1 log unit of attenuation of the brightest stimulus. Seeing a higher dB means seeing a dimmer light, so the point is more sensitive.
Q: What is the basis of the glaucoma hemifield test?
A: Glaucomatous loss is asymmetrical above and below the horizontal raphe. The **glaucoma hemifield test** compares five sectors with their mirror images.
Q: Which tests detect glaucoma earlier?
A: Short-wavelength (blue-on-yellow) perimetry and frequency-doubling technology. Nuclear sclerosis can mimic defects on blue-on-yellow testing.
Q: *(extra)* What else causes tubular vision?
A: Retinitis pigmentosa, high myopia, extensive panretinal photocoagulation, chronic atrophic papilloedema, and hysteria or malingering.
