## At a glance
- **Diabetic retinopathy (DR)**: a retinal microangiopathy with leakage (oedema, exudate) and closure (ischaemia, new vessels), graded as non-proliferative (NPDR) or proliferative (PDR), with diabetic macular oedema (DME) possible at any stage; a leading cause of blindness in working-age adults.
- Kept because it is common, both eyes are full of signs, and it tests grading (the 4-2-1 rule, high-risk characteristics, clinically significant macular oedema or CSME), laser settings, anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) trials and systemic control.
- **S.No 11**: "Both eyes severe non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy with clinically significant macular oedema" (state centre-involving or not on optical coherence tomography, OCT).
- **S.No 42**: "Both eyes proliferative diabetic retinopathy (with or without high-risk characteristics) with clinically significant macular oedema."
- **S.No 51**: "Both eyes proliferative diabetic retinopathy with high-risk characteristics, for panretinal photocoagulation (PRP)"; add "partial PRP done" if scars are present.
- **S.No 34**: "Both eyes proliferative diabetic retinopathy with glaucoma (open-angle or neovascular)" plus a left eye old branch retinal vein occlusion (see card ER3).

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
Mr X, a 52-year-old man with type 2 diabetes for 14 years on insulin, with poor control and hypertension, has had gradual blurring in both eyes for 8 months and floaters in the right eye for 2 weeks; he has had no laser or injections. Best-corrected visual acuity is 6/24 in the right eye and 6/18 in the left; there is no RAPD, no new vessels on the irides, and the IOP is 14 mmHg in each eye. Both fundi show dot-blot haemorrhages, microaneurysms and cotton-wool spots in all four quadrants with venous beading; the right eye has new vessels on the disc of about one-third disc area with a small preretinal haemorrhage, and the left has a half-disc-area frond of new vessels on the superotemporal arcade without haemorrhage. Both maculae show central thickening with a circinate ring of hard exudate within 500 µm of the fovea. My diagnosis is both eyes proliferative diabetic retinopathy with clinically significant macular oedema, with high-risk characteristics in the right eye. I would do OCT and FFA, give intravitreal anti-VEGF for the centre-involving oedema, start PRP in the right eye first, and work with his physician on sugar, blood pressure and lipids.

S.No 11: Both eyes severe non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy with clinically significant, centre-involving macular oedema.

S.No 51: Both eyes proliferative diabetic retinopathy with high-risk characteristics, for panretinal photocoagulation.
:::

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Give the ETDRS-based international classification.
A: No apparent retinopathy; mild NPDR (microaneurysms only); moderate NPDR (more than microaneurysms, less than severe); severe NPDR (4-2-1 rule, no new vessels); PDR (NVD or NVE, preretinal or vitreous haemorrhage). DME is graded separately as absent, non-centre-involving or centre-involving.

Q: State the 4-2-1 rule.
A: Severe NPDR if any one of: more than 20 intraretinal haemorrhages in each of 4 quadrants; definite venous beading in 2 or more quadrants; prominent IRMA in 1 or more quadrants. Two or more features make it very severe. The 1-year risk of PDR is about 50% for severe and 75% for very severe NPDR.

Q: Define CSME.
A: ETDRS, any one of: retinal thickening at or within 500 µm of the centre of the macula; hard exudate at or within 500 µm of the centre with adjacent thickening; thickening of 1 disc area or more, any part within 1 disc diameter of the centre. It is a biomicroscopic definition, independent of vision.

Q: What are the DRS high-risk characteristics?
A: NVD of one-quarter to one-third disc area or more (standard photograph 10A), with or without haemorrhage; any NVD with vitreous or preretinal haemorrhage; NVE of half a disc area or more with vitreous or preretinal haemorrhage. Untreated, about 26% of such eyes fall below 5/200 in 2 years; PRP cuts this by more than half.

Q: Outline the pathogenesis.
A: Hyperglycaemia acts through the polyol (aldose reductase), advanced glycation end-product, protein kinase C and oxidative pathways; pericyte loss, basement membrane thickening and endothelial damage cause microaneurysms and leakage, while capillary closure causes ischaemia that drives VEGF release, IRMA and new vessels.

Q: How do you tell IRMA from NVE?
A: IRMA are shunt or remodelled vessels inside the retina at the edge of non-perfusion; they do not cross major vessels and leak little on FFA. NVE lie on the retinal surface, may cross vessels, form fronds and leak heavily.

Q: Why is NVD more dangerous than NVE?
A: There is no internal limiting membrane over the disc and the vitreous is attached there, so disc vessels bleed and contract readily; NVD also means widespread ischaemia.

### Examination and signs
Q: How do you tell a microaneurysm from a dot haemorrhage?
A: Both are small red dots; on FFA a patent microaneurysm is a bright dot that may leak, while a haemorrhage blocks fluorescence.

Q: Compare hard exudates and cotton-wool spots.
A: Hard exudates are yellow, waxy, sharp-edged lipoprotein in the outer plexiform layer, often in circinate rings around leaking microaneurysms (a sign of leakage). Cotton-wool spots are fluffy white swellings of the nerve fibre layer from blocked axoplasmic flow after arteriolar closure (a sign of ischaemia).

Q: What is advanced diabetic eye disease?
A: Vitreous or subhyaloid haemorrhage, fibrovascular proliferation, tractional detachment (concave, immobile), combined tractional-rhegmatogenous detachment, and NVI with neovascular glaucoma.

Q: What glaucoma may S.No 34 have?
A: Neovascular glaucoma (NVI and NVA, open angle at first, then synechial closure), coexisting primary open-angle glaucoma (commoner in diabetics), steroid-induced glaucoma after injections or implants, or ghost-cell glaucoma after vitreous haemorrhage; gonioscopy decides.

Q: Which ocular conditions protect against DR?
A: High myopia, extensive chorioretinal scarring, retinitis pigmentosa and optic atrophy reduce retinal oxygen demand; one-sided carotid stenosis does the opposite and worsens retinopathy on that side.

### Investigations
Q: How does OCT classify DME?
A: Patterns: diffuse spongy thickening, cystoid oedema, serous neurosensory detachment, and vitreomacular traction or a taut posterior hyaloid. It measures central subfield thickness (normal about 250–300 µm, machine-specific) and shows prognostic markers: disorganisation of the retinal inner layers (DRIL), ellipsoid zone loss and hyperreflective foci.

Q: What does FFA add?
A: Focal leaking microaneurysms versus diffuse capillary leakage, macular ischaemia (enlarged irregular foveal avascular zone, which predicts poor results), capillary non-perfusion and leaking new vessels; wide-field FFA finds peripheral disease. OCT angiography (OCTA) shows non-perfusion and new vessels without dye.

Q: When is B-scan ultrasound needed?
A: When vitreous haemorrhage hides the fundus: to look for tractional or rhegmatogenous detachment and the state of the posterior vitreous, which decides the timing of vitrectomy.

### Management
Q: What are the indications for PRP?
A: High-risk PDR (treat now) and any NVI or NVA; also non-high-risk PDR or very severe NPDR when follow-up is unreliable, in type 2 diabetes, before cataract surgery, in pregnancy, or when the fellow eye did badly.

Q: Describe your PRP technique.
A: Consent, dilate, topical anaesthesia; a wide-field contact lens that magnifies the spot about 2 times (so set about 250 µm to get 500 µm on the retina); 532 nm green laser; 500 µm spot, 0.1 s, power raised from about 150–200 mW to a moderate grey-white burn, one burn-width apart; 1,200–1,600 burns in 3–4 sittings, inferior retina first in case haemorrhage later hides it.

Q: Where do the burns go?
A: From 500 µm outside the nasal disc margin and 2 disc diameters (3,000 µm) from the foveal centre, outside the temporal arcades, out to the equator and beyond; avoid the macula, papillomacular bundle, major vessels, haemorrhages, fibrovascular tissue and the long ciliary nerves at 3 and 9 o'clock.

Q: Why split PRP into sittings?
A: Treating everything at once risks severe pain, worse macular oedema, choroidal effusion, exudative detachment, and angle closure from forward rotation of the ciliary body.

Q: How do you judge the response?
A: Review about 3 months after completion: regressing vessels thin, whiten and turn fibrotic, and leakage falls. Persistent or new vessels need fill-in laser between and in front of old scars, anti-VEGF, or vitrectomy.

Q: How do you treat centre-involving DME?
A: Anti-VEGF first line when vision is reduced: ranibizumab 0.5 mg, aflibercept 2 mg or bevacizumab 1.25 mg, monthly for 4–6 doses then as needed or treat-and-extend. Switch to a dexamethasone implant 0.7 mg (or fluocinolone 0.19 mg) for poor response, in pseudophakes, or when monthly visits are impossible. With acuity of 6/7.5 or better, close observation is acceptable (Diabetic Retinopathy Clinical Research Network, DRCR, Protocol V).

Q: Is macular laser still used?
A: Yes, for non-centre-involving CSME with focal leaking microaneurysms 500–3,000 µm from the centre: 50–100 µm, 0.05–0.1 s, light burns that whiten the microaneurysm; a modified grid for diffuse leakage; or deferred, added to anti-VEGF (Protocol I). Not for an ischaemic macula.

Q: What did Protocols S and T show?
A: Protocol S: ranibizumab 0.5 mg was non-inferior to PRP for vision in PDR at 2 years, with less field loss, less DME and fewer vitrectomies, but only with strict follow-up (CLARITY: aflibercept beat PRP at 1 year). Protocol T: in DME the three drugs were similar when vision was 6/12 or better; with 6/15 or worse, aflibercept did best at 1 year.

Q: PDR with CSME: what comes first?
A: The macula, or both together: anti-VEGF for the oedema, then PRP soon after (within 1–2 weeks in high-risk eyes), because PRP alone worsens oedema; anti-VEGF also speeds regression of the new vessels.

Q: What are the indications for vitrectomy?
A: Non-clearing vitreous haemorrhage (1–3 months; sooner in type 1 diabetes, bilateral haemorrhage, an only eye, or detachment on B-scan); tractional detachment involving or threatening the macula; combined tractional-rhegmatogenous detachment; dense premacular haemorrhage; active proliferation despite full PRP; DME with traction; haemorrhage with NVI. Give anti-VEGF 3–7 days before to reduce bleeding.

Q: What do the landmark glycaemic control trials teach?
A: Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT, type 1): intensive control cut the onset of retinopathy by 76% and progression by 54%, with lasting benefit (metabolic memory) despite possible early worsening. United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS, type 2): intensive glucose control cut microvascular end points by 25%; tight blood pressure control cut retinopathy progression by about a third. ACCORD and FIELD: fenofibrate slowed progression and reduced laser need.

Q: What are the screening and follow-up intervals?
A: Type 1: 5 years after diagnosis (not usually before puberty), then yearly. Type 2: at diagnosis, then yearly. Pregnancy: before conception or in the first trimester, then each trimester. Follow-up: no DR yearly; mild NPDR 6–12 months; moderate 4–6 months; severe 2–3 months; non-high-risk PDR about 2 months or treat.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: What are the complications of PRP?
A: Early: pain, blur, worse macular oedema, choroidal effusion, exudative detachment, angle closure, vitreous haemorrhage. Errors: foveal burn, Bruch's membrane rupture with choroidal neovascularisation, corneal, iris or lens burns. Late: constricted field (may bar driving), night blindness, poorer contrast and colour, loss of accommodation and a dilated pupil, contraction of fibrovascular tissue with tractional detachment, and scar expansion.

Q: How do you plan cataract surgery in a diabetic?
A: Control sugar and blood pressure; treat DME first with anti-VEGF or steroid, or inject at the end of surgery; complete PRP for PDR before surgery (or soon after if the view is poor), since surgery accelerates retinopathy and rubeosis; large capsulorhexis and a 6 mm acrylic intraocular lens (IOL), avoiding silicone (in case silicone oil is needed) and multifocal IOLs with maculopathy; topical non-steroidal drops for 4–6 weeks; close review.

Q: What is the prognosis?
A: Timely PRP halves severe visual loss and anti-VEGF improves vision in most DME eyes. The outlook is poor with macular ischaemia, a foveal exudate plaque, long-standing cystoid oedema, tractional detachment, neovascular glaucoma or poor systemic control; PDR also marks a high risk of kidney and cardiovascular disease.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: Why is anti-VEGF alone for PDR risky in India?
A: Vessels regress only while injections continue; patients who miss visits can return with tractional detachment or neovascular glaucoma, so PRP stays the durable standard when follow-up is uncertain.

Q: What is the crunch phenomenon?
A: Rapid contraction of fibrovascular membranes with worsening tractional detachment after anti-VEGF (or heavy PRP) in eyes with extensive proliferation; avoid it by operating within a few days of the injection.

Q: What did ETDRS conclude about early PRP and aspirin?
A: Early scatter laser is not advised for mild or moderate NPDR but may be considered for severe NPDR or early PDR, especially in type 2 diabetes; aspirin neither helps nor worsens retinopathy or haemorrhage. Focal laser halved moderate visual loss in CSME (about 24% to 12% at 3 years).

Q: What is new?
A: Faricimab (VEGF-A and angiopoietin-2) and aflibercept 8 mg for longer intervals; biosimilars; the fluocinolone implant lasting up to 3 years; pattern scan laser (PASCAL) with 10–30 ms burns for faster, less painful PRP; subthreshold micropulse laser for DME; and anti-VEGF in moderate to severe NPDR (Protocol W cut progression but gave no visual gain).

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Calling PDR "high-risk" without sizing new vessels in disc areas or noting haemorrhage.
- Using CSME and "centre-involving DME" interchangeably: CSME is clinical (ETDRS); centre involvement is on OCT.
- Starting PRP in an eye with centre-involving oedema without treating the macula.
- Dilating before checking the pupil margin and doing gonioscopy for NVI and NVA.
- Confusing IRMA with NVE, or old laser scars with chorioretinitis (laser scars are regular and spare the macula and disc).
- Giving PRP settings without spot size, duration, spacing, number of burns and sittings.
- Forgetting HbA1c, blood pressure, kidneys and lipids.
:::

- Anti-VEGF first for centre-involving DME; Protocol S: ranibizumab non-inferior to PRP if follow-up is assured.
