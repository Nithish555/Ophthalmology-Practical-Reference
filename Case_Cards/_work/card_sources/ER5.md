## At a glance
- **What it is:** a congenital gap in the uvea and the tissues it induces, from failure of the embryonic (optic) fissure to close in the 5th–7th week. *Typical* = inferonasal, along the fissure line; *atypical* = anywhere else.
- **Spectrum on tomorrow's list:** iris (keyhole pupil), retinochoroidal (choroidal) and optic disc coloboma — often together, often with microphthalmos and microcornea.
- **Why examiners keep it:** embryology, Ida Mann classification, the peculiar retinal detachment (RD), the excavated-disc differential and the syndromes.
- **S.No 18 and S.No 45:** "Both eyes typical retinochoroidal coloboma, Ida Mann type __ (disc involved or spared, macula involved or spared), retina attached; with or without microphthalmos and nystagmus."
- **S.No 6 (patient already here):** "Left eye typical (inferonasal) iris coloboma with keyhole pupil; right eye microcornea with corectopia — bilateral congenital anomaly in the coloboma spectrum; fundus and disc coloboma and RD to be excluded in both eyes."
- **S.No 49:** "(Side) eye epibulbar lipodermoid (dermolipoma) with optic disc coloboma — Goldenhar syndrome (oculo-auriculo-vertebral spectrum) to be excluded."
- **Rule:** every iris coloboma earns a dilated fundus examination of both eyes; every coloboma earns a look at the ears, heart, face and family.

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
Mr X, 19, has had poor vision in both eyes since childhood, with no recent change. Both eyes are small with microcornea and a horizontal pendular nystagmus; best-corrected vision is 6/36 in the right eye and 6/24 in the left. Each fundus shows a large, sharply margined, glistening white defect below and nasal to the disc, with pigmented edges, bare sclera in a depressed floor and retinal vessels dipping over its rim. In the right eye the defect includes the disc and extends above it; in the left eye it reaches the upper border of the disc. Both maculae are spared, the retina is attached and no break is seen at either margin. The ears, heart and face are normal.

**Diagnosis (S.No 18 and S.No 45):** both eyes typical retinochoroidal coloboma — right eye Ida Mann type I, left eye type II — with microphthalmos, microcornea and nystagmus; maculae spared; retina attached.

**S.No 6:** left eye typical iris coloboma with keyhole pupil; right eye microcornea with corectopia; both fundi to be examined for coloboma.

**S.No 49:** right eye epibulbar lipodermoid with optic disc coloboma — Goldenhar syndrome to be excluded.
:::

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: What does "coloboma" mean, and which colobomas share one embryology?
A: Greek for "curtailed" — a congenital gap in an ocular tissue. Iris, ciliary body, zonular, retinochoroidal and disc colobomas all come from incomplete closure of the embryonic fissure; a lid coloboma is a separate defect of the lid folds.

Q: Describe the embryology of the embryonic (optic) fissure.
A: In the 4th week the optic vesicle invaginates into a two-layered optic cup whose inferior wall is deficient — the embryonic fissure, through which the hyaloid artery enters. Closure starts about day 33 at the equator and runs forwards and backwards, complete by the 7th week (7–14 mm stage). Failure at any point leaves a coloboma there.

Q: Why are typical colobomas inferonasal?
A: The fissure lies on the inferior aspect of the cup and stalk, slightly nasal to the vertical meridian; so typical iris, choroidal and disc colobomas lie inferonasally and the field defect is superior.

Q: Why is bare sclera seen in a choroidal coloboma?
A: At the unclosed fissure the outer layer of the cup fails to form retinal pigment epithelium (RPE); RPE induces the choroid and inner sclera, so the choroid is absent and the sclera thin and ectatic. The retina is reduced to a thin glial "intercalary membrane" through which white sclera shows.

Q: What are typical and atypical colobomas?
A: Typical — along the fissure line (inferonasal). Atypical — any other site; a macular "coloboma" is often the scar of intrauterine inflammation such as toxoplasmosis rather than a fissure defect.

Q: Classify iris coloboma.
A: Site — typical (inferonasal) or atypical; depth — complete (stroma and pigment epithelium absent) or incomplete (one layer, seen on transillumination); extent — total (to the root, keyhole pupil) or partial (margin only, notch). A strand across the gap is a bridge coloboma.

Q: How common is coloboma and how is it inherited?
A: About 0.5–2.2 per 10,000 births; bilateral in 60–70%. Most are sporadic; familial cases are usually autosomal dominant with variable penetrance. Genes include PAX6, PAX2, SOX2 and CHD7 (microphthalmia–anophthalmia–coloboma spectrum).

### Examination and signs
Q: Give the Ida Mann classification of retinochoroidal coloboma.
A: Type I — includes the disc and extends above it; II — reaches the upper border of the disc; III — below the disc, separated from it by normal retina; IV — disc coloboma alone; V — isolated gap along the fissure line with normal retina above and below; VI — pigmentary disturbance only; VII — extreme peripheral coloboma.

Q: What is Gopal's classification of the disc in fundus coloboma?
A: I — disc outside the coloboma and normal; II — outside but abnormal; III — outside and itself colobomatous; IV — inside but normal; V — inside and colobomatous (commonest, about 44%); VI — disc not identifiable. Types I–III see better and are often myopic; IV–VI go with microphthalmos.

Q: Describe an optic disc coloboma.
A: An enlarged disc holding a sharply defined, glistening white, bowl-shaped excavation decentred inferiorly; thin or absent inferior rim with the superior rim spared; normal vessels from the edges; no glial tuft. It may join a retinochoroidal coloboma and can cause serous macular detachment.

Q: How do you tell disc coloboma from morning glory disc anomaly?
A: Coloboma — excavation within the disc, decentred inferiorly, no glial tuft, little pigment, normal vessels, often bilateral and familial, with iris and fundus colobomas. Morning glory — disc within a central funnel-shaped excavation, white glial tuft, pigmented annulus, many straight radial vessels, usually unilateral, linked to transsphenoidal basal encephalocele; serous RD in 26–38%.

Q: How do optic disc pit and peripapillary staphyloma differ?
A: Optic pit — small round grey-white depression, usually temporal, in a normal-sized disc; about 1 in 11,000; serous maculopathy in 25–75%, in the 3rd–4th decade. Peripapillary staphyloma — deep cup-shaped excavation around a relatively normal disc, with no glial tuft or vascular anomaly.

Q: What field defect does a typical coloboma produce?
A: An absolute superior defect matching the inferior gap, often unnoticed because it is congenital; a disc coloboma adds an enlarged blind spot. Central vision depends on sparing of the macula and papillomacular bundle.

Q: What is a keyhole pupil and what mimics it?
A: A round pupil continuous with an inferonasal iris gap reaching the root; it still reacts through the remaining sphincter. Mimics: surgical iridectomy (usually superior, cut edges), iridodialysis (peripheral D-shaped gap after trauma), Axenfeld–Rieger anomaly and iridocorneal endothelial syndrome (corectopia, polycoria).

Q: What is "lens coloboma"?
A: A misnomer: zonules are missing in that sector, so the lens equator flattens or notches for lack of tension. True loss of lens tissue is rare.

Q: In S.No 6, how do you present the right microcornea and corectopia with the left iris coloboma?
A: As one bilateral developmental anomaly. Dilate both eyes for fundus and disc coloboma, measure corneas and axial lengths, check intraocular pressure and gonioscopy, and exclude Axenfeld–Rieger anomaly (posterior embryotoxon, iris strands) as the cause of the corectopia.

### Investigations
Q: Which investigations would you order?
A: B-scan (excavation, RD in hazy media); OCT of macula, disc and margin; axial length; UBM for ciliary coloboma and before lens surgery; wide-field photography; perimetry. Systemic — echocardiography, hearing test, renal ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain if neurological signs, gene panel.

Q: What does OCT show at the margin of a coloboma?
A: Near the edge the retina splits: inner layers continue over the gap as the intercalary membrane, while outer layers turn back and fuse with the RPE at the margin — the "locus minoris resistentiae", where breaks form. OCT shows small breaks, schisis and shallow detachment of the membrane.

Q: What systemic work-up does S.No 49 need?
A: Examine ears (tags, microtia), face (hemifacial microsomia), lids (upper lid coloboma), motility (Duane syndrome) and spine; hearing test, spine X-ray for hemivertebrae, echocardiography; MRI of the orbit if the lipodermoid extends deep.

### Management
Q: Outline the management of a retinochoroidal coloboma.
A: Cycloplegic refraction and glasses; amblyopia and squint treatment; low-vision aids; dilated review every 6–12 months with RD symptoms explained; prophylactic laser; cataract surgery when needed; protective polycarbonate glasses if one eye is poor; genetic counselling.

Q: How is prophylactic laser done, and does it work?
A: Two to three rows of moderate burns (200–300 µm, 0.1–0.2 s, 532 nm green or diode; indirect delivery under anaesthesia in children) on normal retina just outside the margin, sparing disc and macula — temporally below the inferotemporal arcade. No burns inside the coloboma (no RPE). In one Indian series RD occurred in 2.9% of lasered versus 24.1% of untreated eyes.

Q: Describe the patterns of RD in coloboma.
A: A break in the intercalary membrane alone lifts only the membrane; breaks in the membrane and at the margin give a clinical RD; a peripheral break alone gives an RD that stops at the coloboma edge. Gopal: type I — RD not entering the coloboma; type II — RD extending into it (A subclinical, B break inside, C inside and outside, D peripheral only, E none seen).

Q: Why is vitrectomy preferred to scleral buckling for coloboma RD?
A: Breaks are posterior, at the edge or inside the coloboma and hard to see; cryotherapy cannot create adhesion without RPE and choroid; the sclera is thin. Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) removes traction, finds breaks under magnification and allows endolaser along the whole margin to wall off the coloboma.

Q: Which tamponade, and how do these eyes do?
A: Usually silicone oil (perfluoropropane, C3F8, gas is an alternative). Re-detachment after oil removal is common, so oil is often kept long term. A buckle alone suits only an RD from a peripheral break that does not enter the coloboma.

Q: What are the problems in cataract surgery in these eyes?
A: Microcornea and microphthalmos (crowded chamber; scleral tunnel helps); zonular weakness in the coloboma sector (capsular tension ring or segment); unreliable biometry with an ectatic posterior wall. Glare from an iris coloboma: tinted or printed-iris contact lens, McCannel or Siepser pupilloplasty, or an iris-print intraocular lens (IOL) at surgery.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: List the complications of uveal coloboma.
A: RD (23–42% with retinochoroidal coloboma); early cataract and subluxation; amblyopia, anisometropia and sensory strabismus; nystagmus; choroidal neovascularisation (CNV) at the margin; serous macular detachment and macular hole with disc coloboma; microphthalmos with orbital cyst; secondary glaucoma.

Q: Where do the breaks lie in coloboma RD?
A: Most within 2 DD of the margin — about 54% at the margin, 25% within the coloboma and 8% at the macula; the rest peripheral. Several breaks often coexist.

Q: How is CNV at the coloboma margin treated?
A: It arises where Bruch's membrane ends at the margin. Give intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) — ranibizumab 0.5 mg or bevacizumab 1.25 mg — monthly until inactive, then as needed.

Q: What decides the prognosis?
A: Sparing of the macula and papillomacular bundle (Ida Mann III, V and VII and Gopal I–III do best), amblyopia control and early detection of RD. Surgery reattaches most eyes, but vision is limited by the anomaly itself.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: Expand CHARGE.
A: Coloboma, Heart defects, Atresia of the choanae, Retarded growth and development, Genital hypoplasia, Ear anomalies and deafness; most cases carry a CHD7 mutation.

Q: Which syndromes are associated with uveal coloboma?
A: CHARGE; trisomy 13 (Patau — microphthalmos, cleft lip and palate, polydactyly) and trisomy 18; cat-eye syndrome; 13q deletion; Aicardi syndrome; Walker–Warburg; Meckel–Gruber; basal cell naevus syndrome; Goldenhar; papillorenal syndrome (PAX2).

Q: What is cat-eye syndrome?
A: Partial tetrasomy of chromosome 22q11: a vertical inferior iris coloboma gives a cat-like pupil, with anal atresia, preauricular tags or pits, and heart and renal defects.

Q: What is Goldenhar syndrome?
A: The oculo-auriculo-vertebral spectrum (first and second branchial arch defect): epibulbar dermoid or lipodermoid, upper lid coloboma, Duane syndrome, preauricular tags, microtia, hemifacial microsomia and hemivertebrae; disc or choroidal coloboma may coexist — hence S.No 49.

Q: What else can look like a fundal coloboma?
A: Healed toxoplasma or toxocara scar; myopic chorioretinal atrophy and staphyloma; morning glory and peripapillary staphyloma; laser or cryotherapy scars; North Carolina macular dystrophy and torpedo maculopathy (macular lesions).

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Calling a superior, straight-edged iris gap a coloboma — that is usually a surgical iridectomy; typical colobomas are inferonasal.
- Presenting an iris coloboma (S.No 6) without dilating both eyes; examiners expect a search for fundus and disc coloboma and RD.
- Saying the field defect is inferior — the coloboma is inferior, so the field defect is superior.
- Missing an RD because the floor already looks white — look for loss of the excavated contour, mobile grey retina and breaks at the margin.
- Offering cryotherapy and a buckle for a break inside the coloboma, or laser inside it — there is no RPE or choroid to form an adhesion.
- Confusing disc coloboma with morning glory (glial tuft, radial vessels, disc inside the excavation).
- Forgetting the ears, heart, face, spine and family, or treating a lid coloboma as a fissure defect.
:::
