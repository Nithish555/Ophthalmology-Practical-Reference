## How to say a management plan in this subject
**Keywords the examiner listens for:** target pressure · prostaglandin analogue · uveoscleral outflow · aqueous suppressant · fixed combination · laser peripheral iridotomy · selective laser trabeculoplasty · trabeculectomy with mitomycin C · glaucoma drainage device · cyclophotocoagulation

:::say
I will first set a target pressure from the baseline pressure and the stage of damage. I will start one drug, a prostaglandin analogue at bedtime, and review after two months. If the target is not met, I will switch, or add an aqueous suppressant, preferably as a fixed combination. In angle closure, laser peripheral iridotomy comes first. If drops cannot be used reliably, I will offer selective laser trabeculoplasty. If pressure stays above target on maximal tolerated therapy, or the fields progress, I will advise trabeculectomy with mitomycin C. A glaucoma drainage device is kept for failed filtering surgery or scarred conjunctiva. Cyclophotocoagulation is for eyes with poor visual potential.
:::

### Drugs in the acute attack
1. **Mannitol 20% intravenously, 1–2 g/kg over 20–30 minutes**, first, because miotics fail at very high pressure.
2. If no nausea or vomiting: **acetazolamide 250–500 mg stat, then 250 mg four times a day**. Or **glycerol 50% 1–1.5 g/kg**, after ruling out diabetes. Add **apraclonidine 0.5%**.
3. Once IOP is **below 40 mm Hg**: **pilocarpine 2%** four times in 30 minutes, then 6-hourly.
4. **Timolol 0.5% twice a day** with **brimonidine 0.2% three times a day**, 10 minutes apart.
5. **Prednisolone acetate 1% four times a day**; ibuprofen 500 mg twice a day.
6. Review hourly. When the cornea clears, laser peripheral iridotomy in both eyes (card G4).

## Combinations
A fixed combination needs complementary mechanisms and similar dosing.

@widths 30 38 32
| Combination | Why use it | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Timolol 0.5% + dorzolamide 2%** | Lowers IOP more than either alone; two suppressants that still add; no electrolyte upset | Contraindications of both apply: asthma, heart block, sulpha allergy |
| **Prostaglandin analogue + timolol** | Complementary: more uveoscleral outflow and less inflow; IOP falls 13–37% | Timing clash: timolol works best in the morning, the prostaglandin at bedtime |
| **Brimonidine + timolol** | Two suppressants; a choice after iridotomy and in uveitic glaucoma | Brimonidine allergy; beta-blocker cautions |
| Prostaglandin + brimonidine, or + topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitor | Outflow drug plus inflow suppressant, with no beta-blocker | Brimonidine or sulpha allergy |
| **Never prostaglandin + pilocarpine** | — | Pilocarpine contracts the ciliary muscle and cuts the uveoscleral outflow the prostaglandin needs |

**Overall, for:** simple dosing, adherence, less preservative and systemic toxicity, lower cost. **Against:** fixed timing, altered potency, and exposure to a component that may not help.

### Antimetabolites with trabeculectomy
@widths 18 41 41
| Feature | Mitomycin C (MMC) | 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) |
|---|---|---|
| Action | Alkylating agent; acts in **all phases** of the cell cycle; stops fibroblasts and vessel ingrowth; much more potent | Pyrimidine analogue; acts in the **S phase**; stops fibroblast proliferation only |
| Dose and method | **0.2–0.5 mg/ml for 1–5 minutes** on a 4.5 × 4.5 mm sponge under conjunctiva and Tenon's capsule; rinse with balanced salt solution; tighter flap | **50 mg/ml for 5 minutes** at surgery; or subconjunctival **5 mg** injections 90°–180° away, twice daily in week 1, daily in week 2 (21 injections, 105 mg) |

**Lens extraction in angle closure:** phacoemulsification with an intraocular lens deepens the chamber and opens the angle. Details are on card G4. *(Kanski — not in your practical books)*

## Choosing between them
### Trabeculectomy versus drainage device
@widths 22 39 39
| Point | Trabeculectomy | Drainage device |
|---|---|---|
| Usual place | First operation after failed medical or laser treatment | Failed filtering surgery, scarred conjunctiva, refractory secondary glaucoma |
| IOP fall | Larger, about 41% | Smaller; comparable in neovascular glaucoma |
| Bleb | At the limbus, with microcysts | Far back over the plate; organised capsule; no microcysts |
| Main risks | Leak, overfiltration, bleb infection | Tube erosion, tube–cornea touch, diplopia |

### Valved versus non-valved implants
@widths 22 39 39
| Point | Valved (Ahmed) | Non-valved (Molteno, Baerveldt) |
|---|---|---|
| Flow | One-way valve opens only above **8–10 mm Hg** (Venturi effect) | No valve; the capsule around the plate gives the resistance |
| Surgery | One stage; works at once | Two stages: tube tied for 2–3 weeks while the capsule forms |

## Viva questions
### Drugs
Q: What is the aim of medical treatment, and what are its advantages and disadvantages?
A: **Target pressure**: I aim for an IOP that will arrest or prevent optic nerve damage and field progression. For: serious side effects are rare, and one or two drugs control most patients. Against: drugs lower IOP less than surgery, are costly and lose effect with time. Compliance is hard, and long use may harm later surgery.

Q: How would you classify antiglaucoma drugs by mechanism?
A: **Aqueous suppressants**: beta-blockers, alpha-2 agonists, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors. **Outflow drugs**: prostaglandin analogues (uveoscleral) and miotics (trabecular). **Hyperosmotics** shrink the vitreous. Brimonidine both suppresses and raises outflow.

Q: Why is a prostaglandin analogue the first choice, and why is it given at bedtime?
A: **Prostaglandin analogues** give the largest fall, 25–32%, once daily, with almost no systemic effects. They raise **uveoscleral outflow** by relaxing the ciliary muscle and remodelling its matrix. The effect peaks 10–14 hours after the drop, and bedtime use means fewer vasodilatation symptoms.

Q: Why is timolol best given in the morning, and why may its effect fade?
A: **Timolol** suppresses aqueous more in the morning; production falls naturally at night. A morning dose blunts the early-morning rise and avoids hypotension in sleep. Its effect can fade by **short-term escape** and **long-term drift**, as beta receptors respond to constant blockade.

Q: Which beta-blocker is cardioselective, and where is it useful?
A: **Betaxolol**, a beta-1 blocker with minimal respiratory side effects. It is preferred in normal-tension glaucoma for optic-nerve blood flow.

Q: How do you reduce systemic absorption of eye drops?
A: **Lacrimal occlusion**: press the inner corner of the eye and keep the eyes closed for 3 minutes after each drop.

Q: Why are alpha-2 agonists avoided in infants?
A: **Brimonidine crosses the blood–brain barrier** in children. Under 2 years it can cause somnolence, hypotension, bradycardia, apnoea, seizures and coma.

Q: In an acute attack, why wait until IOP is below 40 mm Hg before pilocarpine?
A: **Sphincter ischaemia**: at very high pressure the iris sphincter is paralysed, so miotics fail. I first lower the pressure with mannitol and acetazolamide.

Q: Which drugs are contraindicated in uveitic and neovascular glaucoma?
A: **Miotics and prostaglandin analogues**: they break down the blood–aqueous barrier and increase inflammation. I use an **aqueous suppressant** with steroids and atropine.

Q: How do hyperosmotic agents act, and what do you tell the patient after mannitol?
A: **Plasma osmolarity rises** and draws water out of the vitreous. After intravenous mannitol he must not get up at once: hypotension, rarely brain coning.

### Lasers
Q: Why is Nd:YAG preferred to argon for iridotomy, and when is argon used?
A: **Photodisruption**: Nd:YAG (1064 nm) cuts any iris colour, with fewer late closures; argon is thermal and needs pigment. Argon suits thick brown irides, anticoagulated patients and bleeding disorders.

Q: How do you know an iridotomy is patent?
A: **Lens capsule seen through the opening** confirms it. At the time: a gush of aqueous or pigment, and the chamber deepens. Retro-illumination alone is not proof.

Q: How does selective laser trabeculoplasty work, and why is it called selective?
A: **Selective**: the 532 nm, 3-nanosecond pulse hits only melanin in pigmented meshwork cells, without heat spread. Cytokines then raise matrix metalloproteinases, making the meshwork more porous.

Q: How does cyclophotocoagulation lower IOP, and why spare 3 and 9 o'clock?
A: **Ciliary epithelium destruction** cuts aqueous production; outflow through the pars plana may also rise. The long posterior ciliary vessels and nerves lie at 3 and 9 o'clock.

Q: How do you treat malignant glaucoma, and where does the laser come in?
A: **Aqueous misdirection**: aqueous is forced backwards into the vitreous. I start strong topical mydriatics, then intravenous mannitol. If these fail, **Nd:YAG hyaloidotomy** opens the anterior hyaloid face through a patent iridectomy. If the laser fails, pars plana vitrectomy removes the trapped fluid.

### Surgery
Q: What is the principle of trabeculectomy, and why is the name a misnomer?
A: **Partial-thickness filtration**: aqueous passes through a limbal fistula, around the scleral flap, into the subconjunctival space. The block removed need not include meshwork; it is a peripheral posterior keratectomy.

Q: Which eyes are at high risk of trabeculectomy failure?
A: **Poor-prognosis factors**: age under 40 years, a previous failed filter, aphakia or pseudophakia, and neovascular glaucoma. Active uveitis, congenital glaucoma, Stevens–Johnson syndrome and ocular pemphigoid also count. In these eyes I use mitomycin C, or consider a drainage device.

Q: Why do long-term drops lower the success of trabeculectomy?
A: **Conjunctival changes**: more fibroblasts and inflammatory cells, and fewer goblet cells, so the bleb scars. Drops for over 3 years are an indication for mitomycin C.

Q: Compare mitomycin C with 5-fluorouracil.
A: **Mitomycin C** is an alkylating agent that acts in all phases of the cell cycle. It is far more potent, and toxic to the endothelium. In contrast, **5-fluorouracil** acts in the S phase only. It is toxic to the epithelium.

Q: When will you advise a glaucoma drainage device rather than trabeculectomy?
A: **Refractory glaucoma**: neovascular, uveitic, congenital, post-keratoplasty, aniridia; failed trabeculectomy with antimetabolite; heavily scarred conjunctiva. Useful visual potential is a must. In primary glaucoma trabeculectomy comes first: its IOP fall is larger.

Q: How do you manage a painful blind eye, and how does cyclocryotherapy help?
A: **Pain relief** is the aim, because vision cannot return. I start antiglaucoma drops, topical steroid and atropine. Then cyclocryotherapy: ice crystals and ischaemic necrosis destroy the ciliary processes and the corneal nerves, so pain eases. If pain persists: retrobulbar alcohol injection, then evisceration or enucleation.
