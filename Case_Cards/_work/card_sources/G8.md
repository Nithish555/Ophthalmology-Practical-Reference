## Indications
- **Every glaucoma case, both eyes, every visit**, to check the target pressure. Goldmann applanation tonometry (GAT) is the preferred method for intraocular pressure (IOP): **reliable, reproducible, not influenced by scleral rigidity**.
- **When GAT is unsuitable:** **Tono-Pen** for a scarred, oedematous or irregular cornea. **Perkins** (portable, hand-held applanation) for a bed-bound or anaesthetised patient or a child. **Non-contact** for screening. **Digital** for a corneal ulcer.

**Keywords the examiner listens for:** Imbert–Fick law · 3.06 mm · green semicircular mires · inner edges just touch · dial reading × 10 · central corneal thickness · phasing · before gonioscopy · 10–21 mm Hg

## Instrument check
- **Slit lamp** with the Goldmann tonometer; the patient sits.
- **Calibration** checked each session with the manufacturer's **calibration arm**.
- **Dial at 1** (10 mm Hg), never 0: at 0 **microvibrations** can cause **corneal erosions**.
- **Prism:** for **astigmatism over 3 dioptres**, turn it so the **red line** on the housing meets the **minus-cylinder axis**.
- **Ready:** anaesthetic drops, fluorescein (strip or 0.25% drops), disinfectant.

## Steps
@widths 48 52
| Step | Why — the viva answer |
|---|---|
| 1. **Order:** after visual acuity and pupils, **before gonioscopy and dilatation** | Gonioscopy opens the angle, so IOP falls: a later reading is falsely low. Dilatation raises IOP 4–5 mm Hg: falsely high |
| 2. **Introduce yourself, explain, take consent:** "Numbing drops and a dye, then a gentle touch on the eye. It does not hurt." | Examiners tick it. Informed patients do not squeeze |
| 3. **Disinfect the prism tip:** 70% isopropyl alcohol, 3% hydrogen peroxide or 1:10 sodium hypochlorite | The tip can spread adenovirus, herpes simplex type 1, human immunodeficiency virus and hepatitis B |
| 4. **Topical anaesthetic drop** (for example 0.5% proparacaine), then **fluorescein**, into the conjunctival sac | Contact is painless. Fluorescein shows the tear meniscus |
| 5. **Instruct:** chin on the rest, forehead firmly on the headband. "Look straight ahead. Keep both eyes open. Do not squeeze. Breathe normally." | Squeezing raises IOP (orbicularis tone). Breath-holding (Valsalva) raises it through episcleral venous pressure |
| 6. **Cobalt blue filter**, maximal light, light and microscope about 60° apart | Fluorescein glows yellow-green under cobalt blue |
| 7. Part tight lids gently, **without pressing the globe** | Pressure on the globe raises the reading |
| 8. Advance the prism until it **just touches the corneal apex**: two **green semicircular mires**, above and below the horizontal midline | The mires are the tear meniscus |
| 9. Centre: semicircles **equal in size** | Unequal semicircles read falsely high |
| 10. Turn the dial until the **inner edges just touch** | Now exactly 3.06 mm of cornea is flattened |
| 11. **Dial × 10** = mm Hg (1.8 → 18). Repeat in the other eye; note the time | IOP varies through the day |
| 12. **Disinfect the prism again** | Protects the next patient |

## How to record the result
**Eye · method · value · time.** Case sheet: "Tn by GAT" (Tn = tension). Right eye (RE) 18 mm Hg, left eye (LE) 16 mm Hg, at 10 am. Write central corneal thickness (CCT) under investigations, not in the clinical table.

:::say
Intraocular pressure by Goldmann applanation tonometry is 18 millimetres of mercury in the right eye and 16 in the left. It was measured at 10 in the morning, before gonioscopy.

## Normal values and interpretation
- **Normal 10–21 mm Hg.** Asymmetry of **4 mm Hg or more** between the eyes is significant.
- **Diurnal variation:** usually highest in the morning, lowest in the evening. It swings 3–6 mm Hg normally; a swing of 8 mm Hg or more is significant. Record the time.
- **CCT:** GAT is calibrated for a mean CCT of **520 µm**. A thinner cornea reads falsely **low** and a thicker one falsely **high**, by about **0.7 mm Hg per 10 µm**. Thin is under 500 µm (also after refractive surgery); thick is over 570 µm. Ultrasonic **pachymetry** measures it.
- **Corneal oedema** under-reads: assume a higher IOP and repeat after it settles.
- **Ocular hypertension:** over 21 mm Hg on 2 consecutive applanation readings, with a normal disc, field and open angles.

## Common errors
@widths 45 55
| Error | Effect on the reading |
|---|---|
| Mires too thick (wide meniscus) / too thin | Falsely **high** / falsely **low** |
| Unequal semicircles (poor vertical alignment) | Falsely **high** |
| Astigmatism (elliptical contact) | With-the-rule **low**, against-the-rule **high**, about 1 mm Hg per 4 dioptres. Over 3 dioptres: red-line method, or average two readings 90° apart |
| Squeezing, breath-holding, pressure on lids or globe | Falsely **high** |
| Quick repeated readings | Slight **fall** |
| Oedematous, scarred or irregular cornea; after refractive surgery | **Low** or unreliable. Use a Tono-Pen |

## Viva questions
Q: What is the principle of applanation tonometry?
A: The **Imbert–Fick law** (P = F/A). The pressure inside an ideal dry, thin-walled sphere equals the force needed to flatten it, divided by the area flattened.
Q: Why is the flattened area 3.06 mm?
A: At **3.06 mm**, corneal rigidity (resisting) and the tear meniscus's capillary attraction (pulling) cancel. Then 0.1 g equals 1 mm Hg, hence **dial reading × 10**.
Q: What do you see, and what is the end point?
A: A blue central circle (flattened cornea) between two **green semicircular mires** (tear meniscus). End point: equal semicircles whose **inner edges just touch**.
Q: Central corneal thickness 600 µm, GAT 26 mm Hg. Comment.
A: It is 80 µm above 520 µm, so GAT over-reads by about 8 × 0.7 = 5.6 mm Hg. The corrected IOP is about 20 mm Hg. Always record **central corneal thickness**.
Q: Why is 21 mm Hg the upper limit?
A: Normal **10–21 mm Hg** is the mean (15.5 ± 2.57) plus 2 standard deviations, about 20.5. That covers about 95% of people. The curve is skewed right, so 21 is approximate.
Q: What factors change the IOP?
A: **Diurnal variation** and **posture**: lying down raises IOP by 0.3–6 mm Hg. Valsalva and lid squeezing raise it. Most general anaesthetics lower it, but ketamine raises it. Caffeine raises it; alcohol lowers it.
Q: What is phasing?
A: **Phasing** records IOP every 3 hours for 24 hours. A difference of 8 mm Hg or more between two readings is significant. It finds the peak, times the drops and explains progression despite good clinic readings.
Q: Classify tonometers.
A: **Contact**: indentation (Schiotz) or applanation, either variable-force (Goldmann, Perkins, Mackay–Marg) or variable-area (Maklakov). **Non-contact**: air puff.
Q: How do you use a Schiotz tonometer? Why is applanation preferred?
A: **Schiotz** is an **indentation** tonometer. Its plunger (5.5 g, or 7.5, 10, 15 g) sinks deeper when IOP is lower. I check zero on the test plate, then rest the footplate on the anaesthetised cornea of a supine patient. A **conversion chart** gives mm Hg. **Scleral rigidity** skews it (low in high myopia, high in high hypermetropia); applanation avoids this.
Q: What is the Tono-Pen?
A: A **Mackay–Marg** tonometer. It works on scarred corneas, over a bandage contact lens and in any position. Compared with GAT, it under-reads below 24 mm Hg and over-reads above.
Q: How does a non-contact tonometer work?
A: An **air puff** flattens the cornea; the time taken relates to IOP. It needs no contact or anaesthetic, so it suits screening. Average at least three readings.
Q: How is digital tonometry done?
A: The patient looks down. Two fingertips press through the upper lid, above the tarsal plate: one still, one indenting.
