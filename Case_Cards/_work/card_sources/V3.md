## Case as presented
**Mr X, 67, a farmer.**
- **Complaint:** defective vision in the left eye; worse for the past 1 week.
- **History of presenting illness:** sudden loss of vision in the left eye 8 months ago. A local clinic referred him to a higher centre but he did not go. The vision has worsened over the last week.
- **Negative history given:** no redness, pain, discharge, watering, photophobia, headache, glare, haloes, diplopia, distortion, flashes, floaters, trauma or frequent change of glasses.

- **General examination:** pulse 74/min, regular; both carotids palpable and equal; BP 130/90 mm Hg.

## Think like this
1. **Risk factors for CRVO, in categories:**
   - Systemic vascular: age, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia.
   - Blood: hyperviscosity (polycythaemia, multiple myeloma), thrombophilia (antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, protein C or S deficiency; hyperhomocysteinaemia in the young).
   - Inflammatory: sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, other vasculitis.
   - Local: raised IOP or glaucoma, orbital compression.

   Then: "in my patient the likely cause is the vasculopathy of diabetes and hypertension; I would investigate to exclude the rest".
2. **Ischaemic or non-ischaemic?**
   - Vision worse than 6/60, an RAPD, NVI or NVG, and many cotton-wool spots favour ischaemic.
   - Few cotton-wool spots after 8 months suggest a non-ischaemic occlusion that has converted.
3. **Why the iris and not the disc?** The ischaemic retina releases vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). In a CRVO the retina is so widely damaged that it cannot grow new vessels itself, so the VEGF acts forward on the iris and angle. With less ischaemia you see new vessels on the disc (NVD) or elsewhere (NVE).
4. **Why look at the disc in any vein occlusion?** Glaucoma is a risk factor; the disc shows collaterals or new vessels; hypertensive changes show on the vessels.
5. **Management in three parts**, after the physician controls the systemic disease:
   - lower the IOP;
   - treat the macular oedema;
   - make the new vessels regress.

## Viva: questions and expected answers
Q: What risk factors did you rule out for CRVO? (0:47:55)
A: Age; hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia; hyperviscosity (polycythaemia, multiple myeloma); thrombophilia (antiphospholipid syndrome, protein C or S deficiency, hyperhomocysteinaemia in young patients); inflammation (sarcoidosis, tuberculosis); raised IOP or glaucoma.
Q: What do you look for on examination in a CRVO? (0:50:42)
A: Vision (worse than 6/60 suggests ischaemia); RAPD; NVI at the pupillary margin; gonioscopy for angle new vessels (NVA). Also lids (xanthelasma), proptosis, conjunctiva and AC cells or flare for a systemic or inflammatory cause.
Q: Why does NVI occur in vein occlusion, and why not NVD or NVE here? (0:53:45)
A: Ischaemic retina releases VEGF. In a CRVO the damage is so extensive that the retina cannot respond, so new vessels form on the iris and in the angle. Lesser ischaemia produces NVD or NVE.
Q: Why did the vision drop suddenly in the last week? (0:57:58)
A: An acute rise of IOP from angle closure can cause epithelial corneal oedema; the macular oedema may also have increased.
Q: Gonioscopy shows 360° closure — synechial or appositional? (0:59:30)
A: On **indentation gonioscopy** the angle did not open, so it is synechial closure from contraction of the fibrovascular membrane. Know how to differentiate PAS from iris processes.
Q: How do you classify retinal vein occlusions? (1:00:44)
A: Central, hemi-central and branch; each ischaemic or non-ischaemic.
Q: Where is the block in a CRVO? (1:01:05)
A: At or just behind the lamina cribrosa. The candidate said ischaemic occlusions are at the lamina and non-ischaemic ones further back, where collaterals are possible.
Q: Which quadrant has the most branch vein occlusions, and why? (1:01:33)
A: **Superotemporal**, because it has the most arteriovenous crossings. (added) It also drains the macula, so it is the most often symptomatic.
Q: Is this CRVO ischaemic or non-ischaemic, and why? (1:02:24)
A: Ischaemic — vision counting fingers, RAPD, NVI and NVG. The examiner noted the lack of cotton-wool spots, suggesting a non-ischaemic CRVO that has become ischaemic.
Q: How do you tell collaterals from new vessels on the disc? (1:04:24)
A: Collaterals are looped vessels that do not branch and do not leak on FFA; new vessels branch and leak.
Q: What arteriovenous crossing changes do you see in hypertensive retinopathy? (1:05:10)
A: (added) Gunn's sign (tapering of the vein on either side of the crossing), Salus' sign (deflection of the vein), Bonnet's sign (banking of the vein distal to the crossing), plus arteriolar narrowing and copper or silver wiring.
Q: How do you describe flame, dot and blot haemorrhages, and a microaneurysm? (1:06:13)
A: Flame-shaped haemorrhages lie in the nerve fibre layer and follow its fibres; dot and blot haemorrhages lie deeper, in the inner nuclear and outer plexiform layers. (added) A microaneurysm is a sharp-edged red dot that fills with dye and leaks on FFA; a dot haemorrhage blocks fluorescence. Be ready to list all the red lesions of the fundus.
Q: How will you lower the IOP? (1:08:13)
A: Acetazolamide by mouth; topical timolol 0.5%, brimonidine and dorzolamide. **Avoid prostaglandin analogues** (they worsen macular oedema and inflammation) and **miotics** (more inflammation, more synechiae).
Q: What other topical drugs will you give? (1:09:24)
A: **Topical steroids** and **atropine**. Atropine is right even though the angle is closed: this is secondary angle closure from neovascularisation, not pupil block, and it reduces inflammation and pain.
Q: What type of glaucoma is this, and how do you classify secondary glaucomas? (1:09:53)
A: Secondary angle-closure glaucoma due to neovascularisation. Secondary glaucomas are classified as open-angle or closed-angle.
Q: How do you treat the macular oedema? (1:11:40)
A: Intravitreal anti-VEGF, named with the dose: (added) ranibizumab 0.5 mg/0.05 mL, bevacizumab 1.25 mg/0.05 mL or aflibercept 2 mg/0.05 mL. Review the IOP, the NVI and the OCT at follow-up.
Q: What does the OCT show in macular oedema, and what is normal? (1:13:05)
A: Altered foveal contour, increased central retinal thickness and cystic spaces in the inner layers. Normal central thickness is about 250 µm; above 270–280 µm suggests oedema.
Q: Which other intravitreal drugs can you use? (1:14:11)
A: Steroids — dexamethasone implant 0.7 mg, or triamcinolone (added) 4 mg/0.1 mL. Monitor the IOP closely with steroids.
Q: If you had found new vessels in the angle, what would you add, and when? (1:15:02)
A: Pan-retinal photocoagulation (PRP), sparing the macula. (added) The Central Vein Occlusion Study advises PRP promptly once there are 2 clock hours of NVI or any NVA, not as prophylaxis.
