## What the talk covered
A short, practical talk for postgraduates, not a textbook lecture. Its points are listed below.

## Intraocular pressure — why it rules management
- IOP is the **main modifiable risk factor**; most management decisions rest on it.
- What the landmark trials showed (as listed on the slides, 3:54:20):
@widths 22 78
| Trial | Finding |
|---|---|
| EMGT (Early Manifest Glaucoma Trial) | **Every mm Hg counts** |
| OHTS (Ocular Hypertension Treatment Study) | Lowering IOP **reduces conversion of ocular hypertension to glaucoma**; central corneal thickness (CCT) matters when you judge IOP, and is an **independent risk factor** |
| EGPS (European Glaucoma Prevention Study) | Confirmed the OHTS findings |
| CNTGS (Collaborative Normal-Tension Glaucoma Study) | A **30% reduction slowed disease** in some patients; some normal-tension glaucoma is **IOP-independent** |
| AGIS (Advanced Glaucoma Intervention Study) | **IOP in the low teens** benefits advanced glaucoma |
| CIGTS (Collaborative Initial Glaucoma Treatment Study) | **IOP fluctuation** is an independent risk factor for progression |

- **Central corneal thickness:** once thought very important. Now simply remember whether a patient has a thick or thin cornea when you read their pressures.
- **IOP fluctuation** is hard to detect when you see the patient for a few minutes a day. Diurnal variation testing helps.

## Measuring IOP well
- All decisions rest on the reading, so: **patient comfortably seated, correct technique, calibrate the tonometer once a month, careful disinfection**.
- **Home (self) tonometry:** rebound tonometers on the cornea; transpalpebral devices (a phosphene-based pen and an indentation pen applied over the upper lid). Patients may soon send you their readings.
- **Continuous 24-hour monitoring** devices — an implantable sensor ring placed with an IOL, and a contact-lens sensor — may show how much fluctuation matters in an individual patient, like an ambulatory BP monitor.

## The drugs — classes and points made
- Drugs either **reduce aqueous production** or **increase outflow**.
  - Until recently, outflow drugs acted mainly on the **uveoscleral** pathway (prostaglandin analogues).
  - The new **Rho kinase (ROCK) inhibitors** act on the **conventional trabecular** pathway.
- **Beta-blockers:** very effective, limited by **systemic side effects**.
- **Brimonidine:** works well, but many patients develop **topical allergy**.
- **Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (topical):** work, but **less effective** than prostaglandin analogues or beta-blockers.
- **Pilocarpine:** still used in selected cases.
- **Timeline:** pilocarpine and timolol alone for a long time → topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (dorzolamide) → latanoprost (which changed practice) → newer prostaglandin analogues → fixed combinations, including **brimonidine + brinzolamide**.

## Newer drugs ("the new kids on the block")
@widths 30 70
| Drug | Points from the talk |
|---|---|
| **Ripasudil** (ROCK inhibitor) | Approved in **Japan in 2014**; Indian preparations available (about ₹200–250); used **twice daily** |
| **Netarsudil** (ROCK inhibitor) | Approved in the **USA in 2017**; **once daily at night**; about ₹400–450 in India. Three actions: relaxes the trabecular meshwork (more outflow), reduces aqueous production (norepinephrine-transporter inhibition) and **lowers episcleral venous pressure** — a new mechanism |
| ROCK inhibitors in general | **Still lower IOP by a few mm Hg in patients already on two or three drugs** — useful as add-on therapy; may become first line with time and experience |
| **Latanoprostene bunod** | Approved by the US FDA in **2017**; a **nitric oxide-donating prostaglandin F2α analogue** — acts on both outflow pathways |
| **Netarsudil + latanoprost** | Fixed combination targeting both the trabecular and uveoscleral pathways; approved abroad, not yet in India (at the time of the talk) |
| **Bimatoprost implant** | A biodegradable rod injected into the **anterior chamber** with a pre-loaded applicator; it rests in the inferior angle. Concern about **endothelial cell loss** led to a lower concentration; approved **March 2020** |
| **Travoprost punctal plug** | A plug with a drug-eluting core placed in the lower punctum and canaliculus; studied mainly in Singapore; response over time awaited |
| **Preservative-free drops** | Benzalkonium chloride damages the ocular surface. Companies moved to reduced and then **zero-preservative** formulations |

## A case from the talk — topical allergy
- A patient on several drops (a prostaglandin–timolol combination plus a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor) developed a **severe skin allergy** around the eyes, with few conjunctival follicles.
- The patient blamed the prostaglandin combination; brimonidine is the usual culprit, but it could have been the preservative.
- **What was done:** stop **all** topical drops, keep the IOP controlled on **oral acetazolamide** for a few weeks until the skin clears, then **reintroduce the drops one at a time** to find the culprit.

## A logical order for choosing drugs
1. **Prostaglandin analogue first** if affordable — once a day and now cheaper.
2. **Beta-blocker** if cost is the problem (and there is no contraindication).
3. **Carbonic anhydrase inhibitor** for mild to moderate reduction.
4. **Good response but target not reached** → add a second drug. **Poor first response** → switch to a different drug rather than add.
5. Patients respond differently — one may fail on one prostaglandin and respond to another. Find out by **trial and error**.

## Beyond drugs
- Ask about the **daily routine, compliance and activities** that change IOP.
- Glaucoma is a **chronic disease**: unlike a cataract patient you will see them for years, so build a rapport.
- The closing message: aim for a safe IOP without disturbing the patient's life. Treat the person, not just the disease.

### Where the drugs act (3:56:20)
:::figure
src: ../video/slides/t_outflow_pathways.jpg
caption: Anterior segment diagram — aqueous inflow at the ciliary body, trabecular outflow and uveoscleral outflow, with the drug classes acting at each (3:56:20)
width: 11
:::
- **What it shows:**
  - **Inflow (ciliary body):** beta-blockers, alpha-2 agonists, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors reduce production.
  - **Trabecular (conventional) outflow:** muscarinic agonists (pilocarpine). ROCK inhibitors act here too; nitric oxide also acts on this pathway.
  - **Uveoscleral outflow:** prostaglandin analogues.
- **Point:** classify every glaucoma drug by **"reduces production" or "increases drainage"** and name the pathway. Latanoprostene bunod and the netarsudil–latanoprost combination act on **both** outflow pathways.

### Timeline of glaucoma drugs (3:57:10)
:::figure
src: ../video/slides/t_drug_timeline.jpg
caption: Timeline of antiglaucoma medications 1875–2014; the speaker's red circles mark the new classes (3:57:10)

- **What it shows:**
  - Pilocarpine (1875), epinephrine (1925), timolol (1978);
  - **dorzolamide (1994)**;
  - **latanoprost and brimonidine (1996)**;
  - brinzolamide (2000);
  - **bimatoprost and travoprost (2001)**;
  - tafluprost (2008);
  - fixed combinations from 1992 onwards, ending with the beta-blocker-free **brimonidine–brinzolamide combination (2014)**.

  A footnote says systemic carbonic anhydrase inhibitors have been available since 1955.
- **Point:** for a long time there were only pilocarpine and timolol. Topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, then prostaglandin analogues, then combinations followed; the ROCK inhibitors are the newest class.

- **What they show:**
  - **First photograph:** both upper and lower lids red, dry, scaly and pigmented (eczematous) with congested eyes.
  - **Second photograph:** milder lid redness and pigmentation with watering and conjunctival injection.
- **Point:** the allergy may be to the **drug or to the preservative**. Brimonidine is the usual culprit, but with several drops you cannot tell. Stop everything, cover the IOP with **oral acetazolamide**, let the skin settle, then **reintroduce one drop at a time**.

:::recall
- IOP = the main modifiable risk factor; every mm Hg counts; low teens in advanced disease.
- ROCK inhibitors: ripasudil (Japan 2014, twice daily), netarsudil (USA 2017, once daily, also lowers episcleral venous pressure) — work as add-ons.
- Latanoprostene bunod = NO-donating PGF2α analogue (2017). Bimatoprost implant (2020) — endothelial loss concern.
- Allergy on many drops → stop all, oral acetazolamide, reintroduce one by one.
- Order: prostaglandin → beta-blocker → CAI; switch if the first response is poor, add if it is good but insufficient.
:::
