## Case as presented
**Mrs X, 55, a housewife, with hypertension for 4 years.**
- **Complaint:** sudden decrease of vision in the right eye with severe pain since this morning. For two weeks before that she had redness, watering, foreign-body sensation and photophobia, slowly getting worse.
- **The sudden drop** followed poking the eye with her own finger.
- **Already treated:**
  - an ophthalmologist diagnosed a corneal ulcer 2 weeks ago;
  - scraping was sent: Gram and KOH smears negative, no growth on culture at 7 days;
  - she was started on natamycin, moxifloxacin, atropine and oral ketoconazole.
- **Negative history:** foreign body, dry eye, contact lens, lagophthalmos, recent surgery, earlier eye drops, diabetes or autoimmune disease; the other eye is normal.

## Think like this
1. **Give two diagnoses, aetiological and morphological.**
   - The pictures show a large peripheral ulcer running around the circumference with central involvement, iris plugging a perforation, and unhealthy lid margins.
   - So consider a **peripheral ulcerative keratitis (PUK) that has perforated, with secondary infection**, against an **infective keratitis (keratomycosis) with secondary bacterial infection**.
   - Keep the other possibilities open: Mooren's ulcer, systemic vasculitis, viral keratitis.
2. **The goal in infectious keratitis** is to **eliminate infection and prevent complications** — restoring vision comes later. Every step should serve those two aims.
3. **The order of management:**
   - control the infection;
   - look for signs that it is controlled;
   - then treat the underlying cause (immune therapy for PUK or Mooren's);
   - then repair the tissue.
4. **A Mooren's ulcer is deceptive**: the overhanging edge hides a larger ulcer — lift the edge to see its true extent.

## Viva: questions and expected answers
Q: What is your aetiological and morphological diagnosis? (2:25:39)
A: Infective (or inflammatory/autoimmune with secondary infection), confirmed by repeat scraping and an immunological work-up. Morphologically: a large ulcer with peripheral, circumferential involvement and perforation. The examiner favoured perforated PUK with secondary infection, or keratomycosis.
Q: How do you differentiate bacterial from fungal keratitis? (2:27:56)
A: **Bacterial:** rapid onset, symptoms out of proportion, marked congestion, sharply demarcated ulcer, dense stromal infiltrate, sterile mobile hypopyon. **Fungal:** slower course over 1–2 weeks, history of vegetable-matter injury, dry raised ulcer with **feathery margins**, **satellite lesions**, thick immobile hypopyon with a convex top that may contain fungus.
Q: What investigations will you do? (2:34:33)
A: Corneal scraping for Gram stain, KOH mount and cultures for bacteria and fungi. Start empirical cover for gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria and fungi while waiting, then narrow it to the organism. Look at the KOH smear under the microscope yourself to see hyphae.
Q: Where do you scrape from? (2:35:27)
A: The **leading edge (margins) and the base** of the ulcer, after removing necrotic debris. Avoid thinned areas, where you could cause a perforation.
Q: What other investigation would you do when the fundus is not visible? (2:36:13)
A: **B-scan ultrasound**, to rule out posterior spread or endophthalmitis — essential before you take a perforated eye to surgery.
Q: What antibacterial therapy is used in infectious keratitis? Is monotherapy acceptable? (2:37:00)
A: Fortified cefazolin 5% (for gram-positive organisms) with a fortified aminoglycoside, or a fluoroquinolone (moxifloxacin, gatifloxacin). Fluoroquinolone monotherapy is acceptable when fortified drops are not available and the ulcer is small or moderate (under 2–5 mm), off the visual axis and responding.
Q: How do you differentiate Mooren's ulcer from other PUK? (2:38:04)
A: **Watson criteria for Mooren's ulcer:** crescent-shaped peripheral ulcer with an undermined edge, **no scleritis**, **no detectable systemic disease** (a diagnosis of exclusion). Pain is out of proportion. PUK from systemic autoimmune disease often has **adjacent scleritis** — always examine the neighbouring sclera — and the immunological work-up is positive. Also look for blepharitis or meibomian disease (marginal keratitis) at the lid margin.
Q: What signs show that the infection is coming under control? (2:41:40)
A: Smaller, less dense infiltrate; less AC reaction; hypopyon resolving; vessels growing in; re-epithelialisation.
Q: If it were a Mooren's ulcer, what local procedure would you do? (2:42:06)
A: **Conjunctival resection (peritomy)** next to the ulcer, extending beyond it on either side. The candidate said two clock hours; the examiner said three clock hours. Then steroids once infection is controlled, and systemic immunosuppression.
Q: What is the goal of treating infectious keratitis? (2:43:30)
A: Eliminate the infection and prevent complications. Restoring vision is not the immediate aim.
Q: How will you manage this large perforation? (2:44:52)
A: Glue only suits perforations **smaller than 2 mm** with no uveal tissue in them — here it is contraindicated. Control the infection with topical and systemic drugs, then do an early **therapeutic penetrating keratoplasty** under general anaesthesia (not an optical keratoplasty).
Q: If it were only thinning with impending perforation, which investigation would you use to follow it? (2:47:00)
A: **Serial anterior segment OCT** to measure thinning, so that you can operate before it perforates. Examiners do ask about recent investigations relevant to your case.

## Images and their points

:::recall
- Adnexa first: lid margin, meibomian glands, tear film. Syringe every infectious keratitis.
- No digital tonometry on a perforated eye.
- Diagram: frontal view + **three slit sections**; lids, lashes, tear meniscus; colour code.
- Scrape the **leading edge and base**; look at the KOH smear yourself.
- Glue only for perforations < 2 mm with no iris in them → otherwise therapeutic PK.
- Goal: **eliminate infection, prevent complications**.
