## At a glance
- **Pseudoexfoliation (PXF):** white, flaky material on the pupillary margin, lens capsule, angle and cornea of older patients. **40%** develop glaucoma or ocular hypertension. **Pseudoexfoliation glaucoma (PXG)** is worse than primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG).
- Kept last year as "POAG with pseudoexfoliation glaucoma" (tasks: gonioscopy, applanation, Humphrey field); also on the short-case list.
- Examiners test: **four-point description**, **three zones**, **Sampaolesi's line**, true exfoliation, PXG vs POAG, PXF vs pigment dispersion, **cataract-surgery precautions**.

## Case format — history
**Chief complaint:** "Mr X, a 68-year-old man, has come for regular follow-up for glaucoma in the right eye" (or "…with diminishing distance vision in the right eye for 1 year").

|---|---|

### Special tests
**Slit lamp — the house four points**
1. Undilated: pupillary margin (tufts, loss of ruff); retro-illuminate for transillumination defects.
2. Iridodonesis, phacodonesis; AC depth of both eyes.
3. Dilate after gonioscopy (note poor mydriasis): the three zones.
4. Describe: **colour → nature → location → with or without iris atrophy**.

Record: "White, flaky, dandruff-like material seen in the pupillary margin as tufts and on the anterior lens capsule as a disc-shaped deposit, with no associated iris atrophy — suggestive of pseudoexfoliation."

## Differential diagnosis
@widths 22 30 48
| Condition | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Pigment dispersion syndrome, pigmentary glaucoma | Angle pigment; Krukenberg spindle; transillumination defects | Young, myopic, bilateral; **mid-peripheral** defects; **uniform** dark band |
| True exfoliation (capsular delamination) | Material on the anterior capsule | Clear scroll split from the capsule; heat or infrared; glaucoma infrequent |
| Primary amyloidosis | Whitish powdery deposits; glaucoma | Systemic; bilateral; fine deposits throughout the eye |
| POAG | Open angle; raised IOP; cupping | White material; usually one eye; Sampaolesi's line |
| Uveitic glaucoma | Flare; pigment; posterior synechiae | No AC cells or other signs of uveitis |

## What you must know
### Definition
- **Pseudoexfoliation syndrome (PXF, also PEX):** an age-related systemic syndrome — gradual deposition of fibrillary, white, flaky material, mainly on the lens capsule, ciliary body, zonules, corneal endothelium, iris and pupillary margin, causing secondary glaucoma.
- **Glaucoma capsulare:** glaucoma in an eye with PXF; usually unilateral.
- **True exfoliation:** **lamellar delamination** of the anterior capsule into thin, clear scrolls that may float in the AC; from heat (**glassblower's cataract**) or infrared; prevented by goggles; also follows trauma, inflammation or age.
- **Who:** after 50, mostly in the 7th decade; men and women equally; commonest in Scandinavians; incidence **1.5–27%**; mostly bilateral.
- **Risk factors:** age above 70; Scandinavian; genetic predisposition (**LOXL1** gene); high blood homocysteine.
- **Systemic:** myocardial infarction, stroke, hypertension, aortic aneurysm; deposits in skin, heart, lungs, liver, kidneys, gall bladder, meninges.

### Classification
Clinical classification by anterior lens capsule morphology:

@widths 30 70
| Stage | Anterior capsule |
|---|---|
| Preclinical | Clinically invisible |
| Suspected PXF | Precapsular layer |
| Mini PXF | Focal defect, starting superonasally |
| Classic PXF | The three zones (below) |

**Three zones (target sign):** translucent central disc, edges may curl (**absent in 20%**) · intermediate clear zone, rubbed clean by the moving iris · peripheral granular zone with radial striations (**seen only after dilatation**).

### Pathogenesis
- **Material:** grey-white fibrillogranular matrix — fibrillin, α-elastin and laminin coated with glycosaminoglycans (hyaluronic acid) — from abnormal basement membranes of ageing epithelial cells.
- **Open angle:** material made in and deposited on the meshwork → swollen juxtacanalicular meshwork, narrowed Schlemm's canal; liberated iris pigment; trabecular cell dysfunction.
- **Angle closure:** lens–iris diaphragm forward on weak zonules; thick cataract; increased iris–lens adhesiveness.
- **Zonules and iris:** material invades zonule attachments and its proteolytic enzymes break them; sphincter atrophy → poor mydriasis, peripupillary defects; iris rubbing on the capsule sheds pigment.

### Investigations
| Test | Why | Expected finding |
|---|---|---|
| Diurnal variation of IOP | True IOP fall; target pressure | Wider swings than in POAG |
| Perimetry; RNFL imaging | Baseline and follow-up | Usually diffuse RNFL loss |
| Specular microscopy | Endothelium before surgery | Low cell count; altered size (polymegathism) and shape |
| Biometry (A-scan, keratometry); B-scan | IOL power; retina behind a mature cataract | Axial length; retina and disc |
| Ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) | Phacodonesis | Extent of zonular dialysis |

### Management
Treat **aggressively, with frequent follow-up**; allow for the wider diurnal swing in the target pressure.

1. **Medical:** the POAG classes — beta-blockers, alpha-adrenergic agonists, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, prostaglandin analogues (strengths: card G1). **Monotherapy is usually insufficient: combine.**
2. **Laser:** argon or selective laser trabeculoplasty (ALT, SLT) — **higher success than in POAG, but shorter-lasting**.
3. **Surgery:** **trabeculectomy** if target IOP is not reached or glaucoma progresses (card G2); **combined with cataract surgery** for intractable IOP with cataract; **trabecular aspiration** removes intertrabecular debris.
4. **Other:** lower homocysteine if high; antioxidants.
5. **Follow-up:** **yearly** without glaucoma, **3-monthly** with glaucoma.

**Cataract surgery.** First check endothelium, phacodonesis, AC depth asymmetry (zonular dialysis: confirm by UBM), dilatation, synechiae, subluxation.

### Complications
- **Glaucoma:** open-angle, angle-closure, acute pressure rises.
- **Lens:** nuclear cataract, phacodonesis, zonular dialysis, subluxation.
- **Iris:** posterior synechiae, uveitis, sphincter degeneration, poor mydriasis.
- **Cornea, retina:** early endothelial decompensation; central retinal vein occlusion, retinal detachment.
- **Surgical:** vitreous loss, IOL decentration, corneal decompensation, inflammation, IOP rise.

### Prognosis and counselling
- Worse than POAG: higher IOP, faster progression, poor drug response.
- Counsel: lifelong drops; riskier cataract surgery; yearly review of the other eye; a heart check.

### Recent advances
- **LOXL1 gene:** an exon 1 polymorphism is associated with exfoliation syndrome. Nothing else in your practical books.

## Viva questions
### Why did you ask / examine…?
Q: Why did you ask about heart attack, stroke and blood pressure?
A: PXF is systemic: linked with myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular events, hypertension and aortic aneurysm.

Q: Why did you ask about glass blowing?
A: To exclude true exfoliation — capsular delamination from heat or infrared.

Q: Why did you look at the pupil before dilating?
A: The hallmark flecks and loss of ruff are on the pupillary margin; poor dilatation is itself a sign.

Q: Why did you dilate, and look for phacodonesis?
A: The peripheral zone appears only after dilatation. Phacodonesis and asymmetric AC depth mean zonular dialysis, which changes cataract surgery.

### Diagnosis
Q: What is pseudoexfoliation syndrome?
A: Age-related systemic deposition of fibrillary, white, flaky material on the lens capsule, zonules, ciliary body, cornea, iris and pupillary margin, causing secondary glaucoma.

Q: How is true exfoliation different?
A: The capsule itself delaminates into clear scrolls after heat or infrared exposure (glassblower's cataract); glaucoma is infrequent.

Q: What is glaucoma capsulare? How often does PXF cause glaucoma?
A: Glaucoma in an eye with PXF; usually unilateral. Glaucoma or ocular hypertension in 40%; PXG in up to 50% of eyes.

Q: What are the hallmark and the most consistent signs?
A: Hallmark: flecks on the pupillary margin. Most consistent: the whitish powdery ring on the anterior capsule.

Q: Describe the lens deposit.
A: Target sign: central disc (absent in 20%), clear zone rubbed by the iris, peripheral granular zone after dilatation.

Q: What is Sampaolesi's line? Is it specific?
A: Dark, dense, scalloped pigment on or anterior to Schwalbe's line. Not specific: also in pigment dispersion and chronic inflammation.

Q: How does PXF raise IOP?
A: Open angle: material and liberated pigment clog the meshwork; trabecular cells fail. Angle closure (occludable in 9–18%): lens forward on weak zonules, thick cataract, iris–lens adhesion.

Q: How does PXG differ from POAG?
A: Older, usually unilateral, higher and more fluctuating IOP, diffuse rim damage, faster progression, poor drug response.

Q: How do you tell PXF from pigment dispersion?
A: PDS: myopic men aged 30–50, posterior iris bowing, mid-peripheral defects, uniform dark band. PXF: after 50, target sign, blotchy pigment, Sampaolesi's line.

Q: Why are the zonules weak?
A: Material invades their attachments and its proteolytic enzymes digest them; PXF zonules are modified fibres.

### Investigations
Q: What does specular microscopy show?
A: Low endothelial cell density; altered cell size and shape.

Q: When will you order ultrasound biomicroscopy?
A: Phacodonesis before cataract surgery: it confirms the extent of zonular dialysis.

### Management and surgery
Q: How will you treat this glaucoma?
A: Aggressively: combination drops, laser trabeculoplasty, then trabeculectomy if target IOP is not reached; combined surgery with cataract.

Q: Is laser trabeculoplasty useful?
A: Yes: more successful than in POAG, but shorter-lasting.

Q: What precautions will you take for cataract surgery?
A: Pupil: hooks or ring. Endothelium: dispersive viscoelastic. Zonules: chop, capsular tension ring, 3-piece IOL. After: longer steroids, acetazolamide (Diamox).

### Complications
Q: What complications do you expect with cataract surgery?
A: Zonular dialysis, vitreous loss, IOL decentration or late prolapse into the posterior segment, corneal decompensation, inflammation, IOP rise.

Q: What are the posterior-segment complications of PXF?
A: Central retinal vein occlusion; retinal detachment.

### Recent advances
Q: Which gene is associated with exfoliation syndrome?
A: LOXL1 — a polymorphism in exon 1.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Confusing pseudoexfoliation with true exfoliation (capsular delamination from heat or infrared).
- Dilating before showing the pupillary margin, or calling the lens clear without dilating.
- Calling Sampaolesi's line specific, or swapping the angles: **blotchy = PXF**, **uniform dark band = pigment dispersion**.
- Treating PXG like POAG: one drug, one IOP reading, no phasing.
- Offering cataract surgery without checking phacodonesis, AC depth of both eyes, dilatation and endothelium; forgetting the other eye.
:::

## Short-case version
:::short
**Spot diagnosis:** "White, flaky, dandruff-like material on the pupillary margin and anterior lens capsule — suggestive of pseudoexfoliation."

**Focused examination, in order:**
1. Undilated pupillary margin: white tufts, loss of ruff; four-point description.
2. Iris transillumination (retro-illumination): defects at the pupillary margin.
3. Phacodonesis, iridodonesis; AC depth of both eyes.
4. Gonioscopy, before dilating: blotchy pigment, Sampaolesi's line, angle width.
5. After dilatation: poor mydriasis; three zones on the anterior capsule.

**3 signs to demonstrate:** white flecks on the pupillary margin (hallmark) · three-zone target sign · Sampaolesi's line.
:::

## Say-it script
:::say
Mr X, a sixty-eight-year-old man, has come for regular follow-up of glaucoma in the right eye, on two eye drops for three years, with gradual, painless diminution of distance vision in that eye for one year. There is no history of coloured haloes, recurrent red eye, trauma or glass blowing.

Visual acuity is 6/24 in the right eye, 6/12 with pinhole, and 6/9 in the left. The right eye shows white, flaky, dandruff-like material in the pupillary margin as tufts and on the anterior lens capsule as a disc-shaped deposit, with no associated iris atrophy, suggestive of pseudoexfoliation. The pupil dilates poorly, without phacodonesis, and there is an immature senile cataract. Intraocular pressure by Goldmann applanation is 18 millimetres of mercury in the right eye and 15 in the left at ten in the morning. Gonioscopy shows open angles, with blotchy pigmentation and Sampaolesi's line on the right. The right disc has a cup–disc ratio of 0.8 with diffuse rim thinning. The left eye shows flakes at the pupillary margin.

My diagnosis is right eye pseudoexfoliation glaucoma with immature senile cataract, intraocular pressure controlled on medication; left eye pseudoexfoliation syndrome.

I would do diurnal variation of intraocular pressure, perimetry and specular microscopy, continue combination therapy, and plan cataract surgery with precautions for poor dilatation and weak zonules, keeping a capsular tension ring ready.
:::

## Quick recall
:::recall
- **Four points:** colour (white) · nature (flaky, dandruff-like) · location (margin tufts; disc on the capsule) · with or without iris atrophy.
- **Hallmark:** flecks on the pupillary margin. **Most consistent:** whitish ring on the anterior capsule.
- **Three zones:** central disc (absent in 20%) · clear zone · peripheral granular zone (only after dilatation).
- **Sampaolesi's line:** on or in front of Schwalbe's line; not specific.
- **40%** develop glaucoma or ocular hypertension; occludable angle in **9–18%**.
- **PXG:** over 60, usually unilateral, IOP often above 40 mm Hg, wide diurnal swings, poor drug response.

**Mnemonic:** **PIGMENT** — causes of trabecular pigmentation: Pseudoexfoliation and Pigment dispersion syndrome · Iritis · Glaucoma (after angle closure) · Melanosis of the angle (oculodermal melanosis) · Endocrine (diabetes, Addison's) · Naevus (Cogan–Reese syndrome) · Trauma.
:::
