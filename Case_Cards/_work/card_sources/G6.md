## At a glance
- **Pseudoexfoliation syndrome (PXF)** is an age-related systemic disease. White, flaky material coats the pupillary margin, lens capsule, angle and cornea. **40%** develop glaucoma or ocular hypertension.
- **Pseudoexfoliation glaucoma (PXG)** is a secondary open-angle glaucoma, more aggressive than primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG). It was kept last year as "POAG with pseudoexfoliation glaucoma", with gonioscopy, applanation and a Humphrey field. It is also a short case.
- Examiners test the **pupillary margin before dilatation**, the four points, the **three zones**, **Sampaolesi's line**, true exfoliation and the **cataract-surgery precautions**.

**Keywords the examiner listens for:** pupillary margin · moth-eaten · transillumination defects · poor mydriasis · three zones · phacodonesis · zonular dialysis · Sampaolesi's line · blotchy pigmentation · diffuse neuroretinal rim · diurnal variation · laser trabeculoplasty · capsular tension ring · true exfoliation

## History — positive and negative, with reasons
**Chief complaint:** "Mr X, a 68-year-old man, has come for regular follow-up of glaucoma in the right eye." Many patients have no symptoms; some give "gradually diminishing vision for distance".

### Step 1 — Visual acuity
**Do:** Test distance acuity in each eye, then with a pinhole, then near acuity. Tell him: "Cover one eye and read the smallest line you can."

**Record:** "RE 6/24, PH 6/12; LE 6/9."

Q: Why is vision often worse in PXG than in POAG?
A: **Nuclear sclerosis** is more common in PXF, so the loss of vision is more marked. The glaucoma is also more severe, with greater field loss.

Q: What can cause sudden loss of vision in this patient?
A: An **acute rise of pressure** with corneal oedema, or a central retinal vein occlusion, which is linked with PXF. After trabeculectomy in advanced glaucoma, "snuff-out" can also occur.

### Step 2 — Pupils, before any drop
**Do:** In dim light, note the size, shape and symmetry of the pupils. Test the direct and consensual reactions, then look for a relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD).

**Record:** "RE: 3 mm, round, sluggish, RAPD present. LE: 3 mm, RTL."

Q: Why examine the pupils before any drop?
A: **Dilating drops** abolish the reactions and hide the margin signs. The mnemonic PART B: check the pupillary reflex, angle, rubeosis, tension and best-corrected acuity before dilating.

Q: Why look for a relative afferent pupillary defect?
A: A **relative afferent pupillary defect** appears in advanced glaucoma. PXG is usually unilateral, so the affected eye can show one.

### Step 3 — Slit lamp before dilatation
**Do:** Examine the cornea, chamber, iris and pupillary margin at high magnification. Grade the chamber by Van Herick in both eyes. Retro-illuminate the iris: set the beam coaxial, then move it to the pupil margin. Watch the iris and lens for quivering.

**Record:** "RE: white flaky material at the pupillary margin as tufts; moth-eaten margin; transillumination defects at the margin; VH grade III."

Q: What is the hallmark sign of PXF?
A: **Flecks of material on the pupillary margin** are the hallmark. The whitish powdery ring on the anterior capsule is the more consistent and diagnostic sign.

Q: What is a moth-eaten margin, and where are the transillumination defects?
A: A **moth-eaten** margin is loss of the pupillary ruff with transillumination defects at the margin, from sphincter atrophy. In pigment dispersion the defects lie in the mid-periphery.

Q: Why compare the chamber depth of the two eyes?
A: The chamber may be shallow in PXF. Unequal depth is evidence of **zonular dialysis**, which I confirm by ultrasound biomicroscopy before cataract surgery.

### Step 4 — Tonometry
**Do:** Measure both eyes by Goldmann applanation, after an anaesthetic drop and fluorescein, before gonioscopy and dilatation. Note the time (technique: card G8).

**Record:** "Tn by GAT: RE 18 mm Hg, LE 15 mm Hg at 10 am."

Q: Why measure before gonioscopy and dilatation, and why note the time?
A: The pressure is measured **before gonioscopy or dilatation**, preferably by Goldmann applanation. The time is recorded because the pressure varies through the day.

Q: How does the pressure in PXG differ from POAG?
A: It is **higher**, often above 40 mm Hg, and harder to control. Its greater **diurnal variation** means one normal reading does not exclude PXG.

### Step 5 — Gonioscopy, before dilating
**Do:** In a dim room, view each quadrant with a gonioscopy lens (card G9). Name the deepest structure seen. Note the pigment, Sampaolesi's line and flakes. Indent if the angle looks narrow.

**Record:** the cross below, then "Blotchy trabecular pigmentation; Sampaolesi's line present."

Q: What is Sampaolesi's line? Is it specific?
A: **Sampaolesi's line** is a dark, dense, scalloped band of pigment on or anterior to Schwalbe's line. It is not specific: pigment dispersion and chronic inflammation also show it.

Q: How do you tell Sampaolesi's line from a pigmented meshwork?
A: **Sampaolesi's line** is dark, granular and discontinuous ("salt and pepper"). A pigmented meshwork is fine, continuous and "brown sugar" in colour.

Q: Why are 9–18% of PXF angles occludable?
A: **Weak zonules** let the lens–iris diaphragm move forward, and the cataract thickens the lens. So I do gonioscopy before dilating.

### Step 6 — Dilatation and the anterior lens capsule
**Do:** Dilate only after gonioscopy shows the angle is not occludable. Note how wide the pupil goes. Examine the anterior capsule, the lens opacity and the stability of the lens.

**Record:** "RE: pupil dilates poorly to 5 mm; PxF in ALC with three zones; NS grade 2; no phacodonesis."

Q: Describe the three zones.
A: The **three zones** form the target sign. A translucent central disc, sometimes with curled edges, is absent in 20%. A clear intermediate zone is rubbed clean by the moving iris. A peripheral granular zone shows radial striations.

Q: Why must you dilate before you call the capsule clear?
A: The peripheral granular zone is **seen only after dilatation**, and the central disc is absent in 20%. An undilated look can miss PXF.

Q: Why note how well the pupil dilates?
A: **Poor mydriasis** is a sign of PXF, from sphincter atrophy and posterior synechiae. It also warns of a difficult cataract operation needing hooks or a ring.

### Step 7 — Disc
**Do:** Examine the disc through the dilated pupil with a +90 D lens, in the department's disc order. If a mature cataract hides it, order a B-scan.

**Record:** "RE: CDR 0.8, diffuse NRR thinning, bayoneting present. LE: CDR 0.3, healthy NRR."

Q: How does the disc in PXG differ from POAG?
A: Cupping is usually unilateral, with **diffuse neuroretinal rim** loss; POAG prefers a sector. PXG also shows more glaucomatous optic atrophy.

Q: What other fundus findings do you look for?
A: **Central retinal vein occlusion** and retinal detachment, both complications of PXF.

### Step 8 — Fields
**Do:** Test by confrontation at 60 cm, one eye at a time, bringing a finger in from each quadrant. Then order automated perimetry (card G10).

**Record:** "Fields by confrontation: RE superior defect; LE full."

Q: Why is confrontation not enough?
A: **Confrontation** gives only a rough assessment. I need baseline automated perimetry, because PXG causes greater field loss than POAG.

Q: How do the cataract and small pupil of PXF affect the Humphrey field?
A: They cause a **uniform generalized depression**. The pattern deviation probability plot stays normal in it, and reveals any scotoma masked under it.

## How I will manage
### Aim
I aim for a **target pressure**: the level at which further glaucomatous damage is unlikely. I set it with the higher diurnal variation in mind. I treat aggressively and review often, because PXG responds less well to drops than POAG.

### The ladder
1. **Drops.** I start latanoprost 0.005% at bedtime. A prostaglandin analogue is preferred to a beta-blocker, which has systemic side effects. I review after 2 months.
2. **Add early.** One drug is usually not enough. If the target is not reached, I add timolol 0.5% twice daily, or brimonidine or dorzolamide if timolol is contraindicated. A fixed combination improves adherence.
3. **Laser trabeculoplasty.** If the target is still not reached, or drops cannot be used reliably, I offer selective or argon laser trabeculoplasty.
4. **Trabeculectomy with mitomycin C.** If drops and laser fail to reach the target, or the glaucoma progresses (card G2).
5. **Cataract with glaucoma.** Intractable pressure or significant field loss: combined phacoemulsification and trabeculectomy. Pressure controlled on one or two drugs without major field loss: cataract surgery alone. Both need the PXF precautions.
6. **General.** I lower a high homocysteine and advise antioxidants. A physician checks the heart and blood pressure.

## What you must know
### Definition
**Pseudoexfoliation syndrome** (PXF or PEX) "is an age-related systemic syndrome where mainly ocular tissues are affected through the gradual deposition of fibrillary white flaky material from the lens, mainly on the lens capsule, ciliary body, zonules, corneal endothelium, iris and pupillary margin resulting in secondary glaucoma."

In plain words: with age, a white, dandruff-like material coats the front of the eye and clogs its drain. The diagnosis rests on **flakes on the pupillary margin**, the **three zones** and **Sampaolesi's line**.

**Pseudoexfoliation glaucoma** is the secondary open-angle glaucoma caused by PXF: a high pressure at onset, faster progression and a poor response to medical therapy.

- **Glaucoma capsulare:** glaucoma in an eye with PXF; usually unilateral.
- **True exfoliation:** lamellar delamination of the anterior capsule from heat (glass blowing) or infrared. Clear scrolls split off and may float in the chamber (glassblower's cataract). Goggles prevent it. Trauma, inflammation and old age also cause delamination.
- **Iridodonesis and phacodonesis:** quivering of the iris and of the lens, from weak zonules.
- **Target pressure:** the pressure at which further glaucomatous optic neuropathy is unlikely.

**Who gets it:** people over 50, mostly in the 7th decade; men and women equally; commonest in Scandinavians.

### Pathogenesis
- **The material** is a grey-white fibrillogranular extracellular matrix: fibrillin, α-elastin and laminin, coated with hyaluronic acid. Abnormal basement membranes of ageing epithelial cells make it.
- **Open angle:** material made in the meshwork swells the juxtacanalicular meshwork and narrows Schlemm's canal. Liberated iris pigment adds to the block. Trabecular cells fail.
- **Angle closure:** weak zonules let the lens move forward. The cataract thickens the lens, and the iris sticks to it.
- **Iris:** the sphincter atrophies, so the pupil dilates poorly. The iris rubs on the capsule and sheds pigment.

### Systemic associations
- Myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular events, systemic hypertension and aortic aneurysm.
- Deposits in the skin, heart, lungs, liver, kidneys, gall bladder and cerebral meninges; a raised homocysteine.

### Why PXG is more aggressive than POAG
- The pressure is higher at onset, fluctuates more and is harder to control.
- Drops work less well. Damage progresses faster, with diffuse rim loss and greater field loss.
- The meshwork is blocked from within, by material made in it and by shed pigment. Surgery is often needed.

### Cataract surgery in pseudoexfoliation
**Risks:** a poorly dilating pupil; zonular dialysis and vitreous loss; a friable posterior capsule that tears; IOL decentration or late dislocation; corneal decompensation; more inflammation; a pressure rise after surgery.

### Complications
- **Angle and lens:** open-angle and angle-closure glaucoma, acute pressure rises; nuclear cataract, phacodonesis, zonular dialysis, subluxation.
- **Iris and cornea:** posterior synechiae, uveitis, sphincter degeneration, poor mydriasis; early endothelial decompensation.
- **Retina:** central retinal vein occlusion, retinal detachment.

### Prognosis
- **Better:** target pressure reached early, with aggressive treatment and close follow-up; a good first response to laser.
- **Worse:** high, fluctuating pressure; poor response to drops; advanced damage; weak zonules and endothelium at surgery.

### Recent advances
- *(extra)* **LOXL1 gene:** a polymorphism in exon 1 is associated with exfoliation syndrome.
- *(extra)* **Trabecular aspiration:** a surgical method to remove intertrabecular and pretrabecular debris.
- Nothing beyond these in your practical books.

## Viva questions
### Why did you ask…?
Q: Why did you ask about heart attack, stroke and blood pressure?
A: **PXF is systemic.** It is linked with myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular events, systemic hypertension and aortic aneurysm.

Q: Why did you ask about glass blowing?
A: To exclude **true exfoliation**. Heat or infrared exposure splits the anterior capsule into clear scrolls. Glaucoma is infrequent with it.

Q: Why did you ask about cataract surgery in the other eye?
A: **Zonular weakness** makes surgery riskier: zonular dialysis, vitreous loss and late IOL decentration. A complicated first operation tells me to plan the second with every precaution.

Q: Why did you ask about asthma and sulfa allergy?
A: **Asthma** rules out beta-blockers, which cause bronchospasm. **Sulfa allergy** rules out carbonic anhydrase inhibitors.

Q: Why did you ask about the family?
A: A **genetic predisposition** is often present in PXF.

### Diagnosis and differentials
Q: How common is PXF, and how often does glaucoma follow?
A: The incidence is **1.5–27%** and rises with age. **40%** develop glaucoma or ocular hypertension, and PXG is seen in up to 50% of eyes with PXF.

Q: What are the risk factors for PXF?
A: **Age above 70**, Scandinavian origin, a genetic predisposition and a high blood homocysteine.

Q: What are the corneal signs of PXF?
A: **Flakes and pigment on the endothelium**, sometimes a Krukenberg spindle, and guttata. Specular microscopy shows reduced cell density and polymegathism. Early endothelial decompensation is common.

Q: What is the differential diagnosis of PXF?
A: **True exfoliation**, pigment dispersion, primary amyloidosis, uveitis, and melanosis or melanoma. Transillumination defects from trauma or herpes also mimic it. POAG and angle closure are the glaucoma differentials.

Q: What are the causes of trabecular meshwork pigmentation?
A: **PIGMENT**: Pseudoexfoliation and Pigment dispersion, Iritis, Glaucoma after angle closure, Melanosis, Endocrine (diabetes, Addison's), Naevus (Cogan–Reese), Trauma. Also pseudophakic pigment dispersion and YAG iridotomy.

Q: How do you tell PXF from pigment dispersion syndrome?
A: **Pigment dispersion** affects young myopic men in both eyes, with mid-peripheral defects and a uniform dark band. PXF affects older people, with flakes, the target sign and **blotchy pigmentation**.

Q: Why are the zonules weak in PXF?
A: **PXF material** invades the zonules at their origin and insertion, and its proteolytic enzymes help them disintegrate. PXF zonules are also modified fibres.

Q: What are the theories of its pathogenesis? *(extra)*
A: The **elastic microfibril** theory, the basement membrane theory, the glycosaminoglycan theory and the amyloid theory.

Q: What does iris fluorescein angiography show? *(extra)*
A: **Hypoperfusion** and peripupillary leakage, at the sites of pupillary ruff defects.

### Investigations
Q: Why do you do diurnal variation of pressure in PXG?
A: PXG shows a **greater diurnal variation**. Phasing shows the true size of the pressure fall, and guides the target pressure.

Q: What does specular microscopy show?
A: **Reduced endothelial cell density**, with altered cell size and shape. It warns of corneal decompensation after cataract surgery.

Q: When will you order ultrasound biomicroscopy?
A: When there is **phacodonesis** or unequal chamber depth before cataract surgery. It confirms the extent of zonular dialysis.

Q: Why check blood homocysteine?
A: A **high homocysteine** is a risk factor for PXF. If it is raised, I lower it.

### Management — drugs
Q: What do you keep in mind when you treat PXG medically?
A: It responds less well than POAG, so I **treat aggressively** with frequent follow-up. I allow for the higher diurnal variation, and consider laser trabeculoplasty early.

Q: Which drug will you start, and why?
A: A **prostaglandin analogue**, latanoprost 0.005% at bedtime. It is preferred to a beta-blocker, which can cause bronchospasm and bradycardia.

Q: Why use a fixed combination, and what is its drawback?
A: **Fixed combinations** simplify dosing, improve adherence and cut preservative toxicity. But the drugs cannot be timed apart: timolol works best in the morning, a prostaglandin at bedtime.

### Management — laser and surgery
Q: Is laser trabeculoplasty useful in PXG, and how does it work?
A: Yes. **Laser trabeculoplasty** succeeds more often in PXG than in POAG, but wears off sooner. Burns shrink the meshwork and stretch the areas between them. Released cytokines raise matrix metalloproteinases, which improve outflow.

Q: When will you do a trabeculectomy?
A: When drops and laser fail to reach the **target pressure**, or the glaucoma progresses. I use mitomycin C, especially after more than 3 years of drops.

Q: When will you choose combined surgery over cataract surgery alone?
A: **Combined surgery** when the pressure needs many drugs, field loss is significant, or exfoliation is present. Cataract alone when one or two drugs control the pressure without major field loss.

Q: What will you check before cataract surgery?
A: The **endothelium**; zonular dialysis (phacodonesis, unequal chamber depth, ultrasound biomicroscopy); pupil dilatation; posterior synechiae; and subluxation.

Q: How does a capsular tension ring help?
A: A **capsular tension ring** in the bag spreads any force over the whole zonular apparatus, not the weak part. It manages zonular dialysis and stabilises the bag.

Q: How will you enlarge a small pupil at surgery?
A: I stretch it, or use **iris hooks**, Kuglen hooks or a pupil expansion ring. The **Malyugin ring** is a square expander with four coils. Mini-sphincterotomies or a sector iridectomy are alternatives.

Q: Why the soft-shell technique?
A: The **endothelium** is weak in PXF. In the soft-shell method a dispersive viscoelastic goes in first and coats the endothelium; a cohesive one follows.

Q: How will you operate on a subluxated lens?
A: By its **extent in clock hours**. Up to 3: phacoemulsification. 3–5: add a capsular tension ring. 5–7: a Cionni ring. Beyond 7: intracapsular extraction, anterior vitrectomy and a scleral-fixated or anterior chamber IOL.

### Complications
Q: What complications do you expect during and after cataract surgery?
A: **Poor dilatation**, zonular dialysis and vitreous loss, IOL decentration, corneal decompensation, heavier inflammation and a pressure rise. Late IOL prolapse into the posterior segment can follow.

Q: Why is inflammation heavier after surgery in PXF?
A: PXF disrupts the **blood–aqueous barrier**, shown by increased flare. So I give steroids aggressively and longer, and may use a heparin-surface-modified IOL.

### Recent advances
Q: Which gene is linked with exfoliation syndrome? *(extra)*
A: The **LOXL1** gene: a polymorphism in exon 1.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Dilating before showing the pupillary margin and doing gonioscopy: the hallmark and an occludable angle are missed.
- Calling the capsule clear through an undilated pupil.
- Confusing pseudoexfoliation with true exfoliation (capsular delamination from heat or infrared).
- Swapping the angles: **blotchy pigment with Sampaolesi's line = PXF**; **uniform dark band = pigment dispersion**.
- Treating PXG like POAG: one drug, one pressure reading, no phasing, no early laser.
- Offering cataract surgery without checking phacodonesis, the chamber depth of both eyes, dilatation and the endothelium.
- Forgetting the other eye, and forgetting that PXF is systemic.
:::

## Short-case version
### Pseudoexfoliation
**The instruction you may get:** "Examine the anterior segment of this patient." Or: "Look at this patient's pupil and lens."

**Spot diagnosis:** White, flaky, dandruff-like material on the pupillary margin and the anterior lens capsule — suggestive of pseudoexfoliation.

**Focused examination:**
1. Before any drop: the pupillary margin at high magnification for tufts of flakes and a moth-eaten margin; pupil size and reactions.
2. Retro-illumination of the iris: transillumination defects at the margin.
3. Iridodonesis, phacodonesis, and Van Herick depth in both eyes.
4. Ask to measure the pressure and do gonioscopy before dilating: blotchy pigment, Sampaolesi's line.
5. After dilatation: how far the pupil dilates; the three zones; the cataract. Offer to see the disc and the other eye.

:::say
On slit-lamp examination of the right eye before dilatation, the colour of the material is white. Its nature is flaky, dandruff-like. It is located on the pupillary margin as tufts and on the anterior lens capsule as a disc-shaped deposit. There is no associated iris atrophy.

The pupillary margin looks moth-eaten, with transillumination defects at the margin. The pupil dilates poorly. After dilatation, the anterior capsule shows three zones: a central disc, a clear zone and a peripheral granular zone. There is no phacodonesis. There is nuclear sclerosis. Gonioscopy shows open angles with blotchy pigmentation and Sampaolesi's line.

These findings are suggestive of pseudoexfoliation. My diagnosis is right eye pseudoexfoliation syndrome with immature senile cataract.
:::

**Three signs to show:**
1. White flecks on the pupillary margin (the hallmark), before dilatation.
2. The three-zone target sign on the anterior capsule, after dilatation.
3. Sampaolesi's line and blotchy pigment on gonioscopy.

Q: Why do you look at the pupillary margin before dilating?
A: The **flecks on the pupillary margin** are the hallmark, and a dilated pupil hides the margin. The angle must also be checked before any dilatation.

Q: How will you show the iris transillumination defects?
A: By **retro-illumination**: the beam is set coaxial, then moved to the pupil edge. The fundus glow shines through the defects at the margin.

Q: What does "with or without iris atrophy" mean in your description?
A: **Sphincter atrophy** at the pupil edge gives the moth-eaten margin of PXF. In pigment dispersion the atrophy lies in the iris periphery.

Q: Where else can you find PXF material?
A: On the **corneal endothelium**, the iris crypts, the trabecular meshwork as dandruff-like deposits, and the conjunctiva as a whitish powder.

Q: You have found PXF. What else will you examine?
A: The **pressure**, gonioscopy, the disc and the fields, because 40% develop glaucoma or ocular hypertension. I also examine the other eye.

## Say-it script
### Opening (about 1 minute)
:::say
Mr X, a sixty-eight-year-old man, has come for regular follow-up of glaucoma in the right eye.

He was apparently normal three years ago. Then he noticed defective vision in the right eye, gradual in onset and progressive in nature, without pain or headache. An ophthalmologist at his place found raised pressure in the right eye and started eye drops. He then came to our institution for follow-up. Over the past year the vision has fallen further.

There is no history of headache with vomiting, coloured haloes, redness, watering or pain. There is no history of injury, herpes, glass blowing, short sight or frequent change of glasses.

He is a known hypertensive for ten years, on regular treatment. He has no asthma, heart disease, stroke or sulfa allergy, and has not used steroids. He has had no eye surgery or laser.

For three years he has used latanoprost 0.005 percent at bedtime and timolol 0.5 percent twice daily in the right eye. He uses them regularly, without side effects. There is no family history of glaucoma.
:::

### Closing (2 minutes)
:::say
To summarise, Mr X, a sixty-eight-year-old man, has glaucoma in the right eye, on two drugs for three years, with falling vision.

Visual acuity is 6/24 in the right eye, 6/12 with pinhole, and 6/9 in the left. The right pupillary margin shows white, flaky, dandruff-like tufts, with a moth-eaten margin and transillumination defects. The pupil dilates poorly, and the anterior capsule shows three zones. There is an immature senile cataract, without phacodonesis. Intraocular pressure by Goldmann applanation is 18 millimetres of mercury in the right eye and 15 in the left, at ten in the morning. Gonioscopy shows open angles, with blotchy pigmentation and Sampaolesi's line on the right. The right cup–disc ratio is 0.8, with diffuse neuroretinal rim loss. The left eye shows flakes on the pupillary margin and a healthy disc.

My diagnosis is right eye pseudoexfoliation glaucoma with immature senile cataract, controlled on two medications. The left eye has pseudoexfoliation syndrome.

I will proceed with diurnal variation of pressure, central corneal thickness, Humphrey perimetry and optical coherence tomography. Before surgery I will do specular microscopy, ultrasound biomicroscopy and biometry. I will continue both drugs, and offer laser trabeculoplasty if the target is not reached. I will plan phacoemulsification with precautions for poor dilatation and weak zonules, with a capsular tension ring ready. If the field loss is significant, I will combine it with trabeculectomy. I will review him every three months.
:::

## Quick recall
:::recall
- **Four points:** colour (white) · nature (flaky, dandruff-like) · location (tufts on the margin; a disc on the capsule) · with or without iris atrophy.
- **Order:** pupils and margin before any drop → tonometry → gonioscopy → dilate → capsule → disc → fields.
- **Hallmark:** flecks on the pupillary margin. **Most consistent:** the whitish ring on the anterior capsule.
- **Three zones:** central disc (absent in 20%) · clear zone · peripheral granular zone (only after dilatation).
- **Defects:** at the pupillary margin in PXF; in the mid-periphery in pigment dispersion.
- **Sampaolesi's line:** dark, scalloped, on or in front of Schwalbe's line; not specific.
- **Numbers:** incidence 1.5–27% · glaucoma or ocular hypertension in 40% · occludable angle in 9–18%.
- **PXG:** over 60, usually unilateral, pressure often above 40 mm Hg, wide swings, diffuse rim loss, poor drug response.
- **Laser trabeculoplasty:** works better than in POAG but wears off sooner.
- **Cataract surgery:** hooks or ring · dispersive viscoelastic · chop · capsular tension ring · 3-piece IOL · longer steroids.

**Mnemonic:** **PIGMENT** (causes of trabecular pigmentation): Pseudoexfoliation and Pigment dispersion · Iritis · Glaucoma after angle closure · Melanosis of the angle · Endocrine (diabetes, Addison's) · Naevus (Cogan–Reese) · Trauma. **PART B** (before dilating): Pupillary reflex · Angle · Rubeosis · Tension · BCVA (best-corrected visual acuity).
:::
