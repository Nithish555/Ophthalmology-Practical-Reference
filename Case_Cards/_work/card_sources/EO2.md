## At a glance
- **Microcornea**: horizontal corneal diameter **less than 10 mm in an adult** (less than 9 mm at birth). **Corectopia**: a pupil displaced from its normal, almost central position. Both are developmental anomalies and travel with **coloboma, glaucoma and cataract**.
- **Epibulbar choristomas**: the **limbal (solid) dermoid** sits astride the **inferotemporal limbus**; the **lipodermoid (dermolipoma)** lies under the conjunctiva in the **superotemporal fornix**. Either may be part of **Goldenhar syndrome**.
- Kept because the signs are stable and the viva can range over embryology, anterior segment dysgenesis, glaucoma and amblyopia.
- **S.No 6 ★**: "Right eye microcornea with corectopia; left eye typical iris coloboma — bilateral congenital anterior segment anomalies, probably of the coloboma spectrum; to rule out microphthalmos, fundal coloboma and glaucoma." The iris coloboma is covered in full on card ER5.
- **S.No 49**: "Right (or left) eye epibulbar lipodermoid in the superotemporal fornix with optic disc coloboma; Goldenhar (oculo-auriculo-vertebral) spectrum to be excluded."
- Examiners want **measured** corneal diameters, the pupil position, intraocular pressure (IOP), gonioscopy, the fundus, refraction and amblyopia, and a systemic check (ears, face, spine, teeth, heart).

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
Mr X, a 20-year-old man, has had poor vision in the right eye since early childhood, with no watering or photophobia in infancy, no injury or surgery, and no similar illness in the family. The right cornea measures 9 mm horizontally against 11.5 mm on the left; the right anterior chamber is of normal depth and the pupil is displaced superotemporally about 2 mm from the centre, round and reacting. The left eye has a keyhole-shaped full-thickness iris defect reaching the iris root inferonasally, with a flat notch in the lens edge in the same meridian. Intraocular pressure is 14 and 16 millimetres of mercury (mmHg) by applanation, gonioscopy shows open angles, and the discs are healthy; dilated fundus examination shows a retinochoroidal coloboma below the left disc. Right best-corrected vision is 6/24 with a hypermetropic correction, suggesting anisometropic amblyopia; systemic examination shows no ear, facial, dental or spinal anomaly.

**Diagnosis (S.No 6):** Right eye microcornea with corectopia and amblyopia; left eye typical iris coloboma with retinochoroidal coloboma — bilateral congenital anomalies of the coloboma spectrum, with no glaucoma at present.

**Diagnosis (S.No 49):** Right (or left) eye epibulbar lipodermoid in the superotemporal fornix with optic disc coloboma; Goldenhar syndrome to be excluded.
:::

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Define microcornea. What are the normal corneal diameters?
A: Horizontal diameter below 10 mm in an adult (below 9 mm in a term newborn). Normal adult cornea is 11.5–12 mm horizontally and 10.5–11 mm vertically; the newborn cornea is about 9.5–10 mm and reaches adult size by about 2 years. Megalocornea is 13 mm or more in an adult.

Q: How do microphthalmos, nanophthalmos and isolated microcornea differ?
A: Isolated microcornea: small cornea, normal axial length. Microphthalmos: the whole eye is small; simple, or complex (with coloboma, cataract, persistent fetal vasculature). Nanophthalmos: simple microphthalmos with axial length below about 20.5 mm, thick sclera, crowded anterior segment, hypermetropia of +8 to +20 dioptres (D), and angle closure and uveal effusion after surgery.

Q: What are the associations of microcornea?
A: Ocular: hypermetropia; angle closure, and later open-angle glaucoma (about 20%); congenital cataract; uveal coloboma; persistent fetal vasculature; Axenfeld–Rieger, Peters anomaly; amblyopia. Systemic: rubella, fetal alcohol syndrome, CHARGE, Nance–Horan, Weill–Marchesani, Ehlers–Danlos.

Q: How do you tell megalocornea from buphthalmos?
A: Megalocornea: bilateral, clear, non-progressive cornea of 13 mm or more, X-linked recessive (CHRDL1) so mostly males, normal IOP and disc, deep chamber, iridodonesis, later lens subluxation and early cataract. Buphthalmos: raised IOP, photophobia, watering, corneal oedema, horizontal or concentric Haab's striae, an enlarging eye, cupping and axial myopia.

Q: What is corectopia? Give its causes.
A: Displacement of the pupil from its normal position (normally very slightly inferonasal). Congenital: isolated, ectopia lentis et pupillae, Axenfeld–Rieger, iris coloboma, partial aniridia, Peters anomaly, persistent pupillary membrane. Acquired: trauma, surgery (iris or vitreous in the wound, peaked pupil), synechiae, adherent leucoma, ICE syndrome, iris tumour, iris ischaemia after angle closure.

Q: What is ectopia lentis et pupillae?
A: Autosomal recessive (ADAMTSL4), bilateral; the pupil and lens are displaced in **opposite** directions. The pupil is small, oval and dilates poorly; a persistent pupillary membrane is seen in about 87%, with iridodonesis, transillumination defects, cataract and risk of retinal detachment; there are no systemic features.

Q: Describe the Axenfeld–Rieger spectrum.
A: Autosomal dominant, bilateral anterior segment dysgenesis (PITX2 on 4q25, FOXC1 on 6p25). **Axenfeld anomaly**: posterior embryotoxon (prominent, anteriorly placed Schwalbe's line) with iris strands to it. **Rieger anomaly**: adds iris hypoplasia, corectopia, polycoria and ectropion uveae. **Rieger syndrome**: adds hypodontia, maxillary hypoplasia, redundant periumbilical skin, hypospadias. Glaucoma in about 50%.

Q: How is ICE syndrome different from Axenfeld–Rieger?
A: ICE is acquired and unilateral, in women aged 20–50: abnormal endothelium (ICE cells on specular microscopy) grows over angle and iris, giving PAS beyond Schwalbe's line that pull the pupil towards them; variants are progressive iris atrophy, Chandler and Cogan–Reese. Axenfeld–Rieger is congenital, bilateral and familial with normal endothelium.

### Examination and signs
Q: How do you measure the corneal diameter at the bedside?
A: Callipers or a clear ruler against the cornea, or the slit-beam length on the slit lamp, limbus to limbus horizontally and vertically in both eyes; in infants, callipers during examination under anaesthesia (EUA).

Q: What is a persistent pupillary membrane, and how do you tell it from posterior synechiae?
A: A remnant of the anterior tunica vasculosa lentis, which normally regresses in the last trimester. Its strands arise from the **collarette** and cross the pupil to the opposite collarette, the lens (pigment stars on the capsule) or the cornea; synechiae arise from the **pupil margin** after inflammation. Most need nothing; a dense membrane may need mydriatics, laser or excision to prevent amblyopia.

Q: Describe aniridia.
A: Bilateral near-total absence of iris (a stump on gonioscopy); PAX6 on 11p13; two-thirds autosomal dominant. Nystagmus, foveal and optic nerve hypoplasia, aniridic keratopathy (limbal stem cell deficiency), glaucoma (50–75%), cataract, ectopia lentis. Sporadic cases may have WAGR (Wilms tumour, aniridia, genitourinary anomalies, retardation): genetic testing and 3-monthly renal ultrasound in early childhood.

Q: What is Peters anomaly?
A: A central corneal leucoma over a defect in posterior stroma, Descemet's membrane and endothelium. Type 1: iris strands from the collarette to the edge of the opacity, clear lens; type 2: keratolenticular adhesion or cataract, more severe. About 80% bilateral; glaucoma in 50–70%. Peters plus: autosomal recessive, short stature, cleft lip or palate, developmental delay.

Q: How do you tell a limbal dermoid from a lipodermoid?
A: Limbal dermoid: inferotemporal limbus, half on cornea and half on sclera, firm, white-yellow, dome-shaped, keratinised, fine hairs, fixed, sometimes with a lipid arc in the cornea ahead of it. Lipodermoid: superotemporal fornix between the superior and lateral recti, soft, yellow, mobile with the conjunctiva, its posterior edge merging with orbital fat and never seen.

Q: How do you tell a lipodermoid from prolapsed orbital fat?
A: Prolapsed orbital fat appears in the elderly, is often bilateral, yellow, smooth, without hairs, and can be pushed back into the orbit. A lipodermoid is congenital, may bear fine hairs, has a whitish keratinised surface and cannot be reposited.

Q: What is Goldenhar syndrome?
A: Oculo-auriculo-vertebral spectrum (first and second branchial arch), mostly sporadic. Triad: epibulbar dermoid or lipodermoid (bilateral in about 25%), preauricular appendages, pretragal fistulae; also upper-lid coloboma, Duane syndrome, microtia, deafness, hemifacial microsomia, vertebral, cardiac and renal defects. Differential: Treacher Collins (bilateral, lower-lid coloboma, no dermoid).

Q: Why does a limbal dermoid reduce vision?
A: It flattens the cornea in the meridian of the lesion, giving large, often oblique astigmatism, with irregular astigmatism from its edge and lipid; it may encroach on the visual axis; the result is anisometropic amblyopia, sometimes with strabismus.

### Investigations
Q: What investigations would you do for S.No 6?
A: Cycloplegic refraction, keratometry and A-scan axial length; applanation IOP, central corneal thickness and gonioscopy; dilated fundus with optical coherence tomography (OCT) of disc and macula; fields if glaucoma is suspected; UBM of angle and ciliary body; genetic testing if syndromic.

Q: Which investigations help before excising a dermoid?
A: UBM and AS-OCT to show the depth of corneal and scleral involvement and whether Descemet's membrane is involved; MRI of the orbit for posterior extension of a lipodermoid into fat, muscle or lacrimal gland. For Goldenhar: spine X-ray, audiometry, echocardiography, renal ultrasound. Biopsy is rarely needed.

Q: What is the histology of a dermoid and a lipodermoid?
A: Both are choristomas (normal tissue at an abnormal site). Dermoid: keratinised stratified squamous epithelium over dense collagen with hair follicles, sebaceous and sweat glands, sometimes cartilage, bone or lacrimal tissue (complex choristoma). Lipodermoid: mostly adipose tissue with few adnexal structures.

Q: How are corneal dermoids graded (Mann)?
A: Grade I: superficial limbal (epibulbar) dermoid, the commonest. Grade II: larger, covering part or all of the central cornea, extending into stroma but sparing Descemet's membrane and endothelium. Grade III: whole anterior segment involved, often with microphthalmos.

### Management
Q: How would you manage S.No 6?
A: Full cycloplegic refraction and glasses; occlusion or atropine penalisation if still in the amblyopic age; tinted or painted-iris contact lens for glare from the coloboma; protective glasses; lifelong IOP and disc checks; watch for cataract; regular fundus review for retinal detachment (card ER5); genetic counselling.

Q: When and how would you excise a limbal dermoid?
A: Indications: visual axis involvement, amblyopia not responding to glasses and occlusion, dellen, irritation, rapid growth, cosmesis. Lamellar sclerokeratectomy (shave excision) from the corneal side in a clean plane, not chasing full depth; cover with a lamellar patch graft or amniotic membrane; mitomycin C reduces recurrence and pseudopterygium; send for histopathology.

Q: Will excision cure the astigmatism?
A: Usually not: the astigmatism often persists or even increases, so surgery is mainly for cosmesis, comfort or a lesion entering the visual axis, and amblyopia treatment must continue afterwards. Tell the parents this before surgery.

Q: How do you manage a lipodermoid?
A: Usually leave it alone. If it is cosmetically troublesome, excise only the visible anterior part under the conjunctiva and preserve the conjunctiva. Deep dissection risks damage to the lacrimal ductules (dry eye), lateral rectus and levator (restriction, ptosis), symblepharon and orbital fat prolapse.

Q: What are the complications of dermoid surgery?
A: Corneal scarring and vascularisation, persistent epithelial defect, pseudopterygium, perforation with deep lesions, recurrence, limbal stem cell deficiency, increased astigmatism.

Q: How is glaucoma managed in anterior segment dysgenesis?
A: Medical first: beta-blockers, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, prostaglandin analogues (no brimonidine under 2 years). Angle surgery works less well than in primary congenital glaucoma, so trabeculectomy with mitomycin C or a drainage device is often needed; cyclophotocoagulation for refractory eyes.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: What must be watched for in an eye with microcornea?
A: Angle-closure and later open-angle glaucoma, cataract, amblyopia, and retinal detachment if coloboma coexists. Cataract surgery is harder: little working space, shallow chamber, zonular weakness, high and less predictable lens power, and uveal effusion in nanophthalmos.

Q: What is the visual prognosis?
A: Isolated microcornea or corectopia with corrected refraction and treated amblyopia gives useful vision; macular or disc coloboma, foveal hypoplasia, nystagmus or glaucoma limit it. Limbal dermoids do well if amblyopia is treated early; grade III lesions do poorly.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: Which genes are linked with these anomalies?
A: PAX6 (aniridia, some Peters anomaly and coloboma); PITX2 and FOXC1 (Axenfeld–Rieger, some Peters); CYP1B1 (primary congenital glaucoma, some Peters); ADAMTSL4 (ectopia lentis et pupillae); SOX2 and OTX2 (severe microphthalmos); MFRP (nanophthalmos); CHRDL1 (X-linked megalocornea); CHD7 (CHARGE).

Q: Give the key points of the left iris coloboma in S.No 6 (details on card ER5).
A: Inferonasal keyhole pupil, from failure of closure of the embryonic fissure; complete (full thickness, to the root) or partial (margin only); incomplete forms show only on transillumination. Look for a lens notch, ciliary body, retinochoroidal and disc coloboma, and CHARGE. Tinted or iris-printed contact lens for glare; screen for retinal detachment (23–40% with retinochoroidal coloboma).

Q: What is CHARGE?
A: Coloboma, Heart defects, choanal Atresia, Retarded growth and development, Genital hypoplasia, Ear anomalies with deafness; CHD7 gene.

Q: What are the causes of congenital corneal opacity?
A: STUMPED: Sclerocornea; Tears in Descemet's membrane (congenital glaucoma, forceps); Ulcer (herpes, bacterial, neurotrophic); Metabolic (mucopolysaccharidoses); Peters anomaly (posterior corneal defect); Endothelial dystrophy (congenital hereditary endothelial dystrophy, posterior polymorphous dystrophy); Dermoid.

Q: Where does microcornea sit in Hoskins' classification of developmental glaucoma?
A: Under corneotrabeculodysgenesis, with the other corneal defects: peripheral (Axenfeld), mid-peripheral (Rieger), central (Peters) and abnormal corneal size (microcornea, megalocornea). The other groups are isolated trabeculodysgenesis and iridotrabeculodysgenesis (for example aniridia).


## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Saying "microcornea" without a number: give the horizontal diameter of **both** corneas in millimetres.
- Skipping IOP and gonioscopy: small eyes and dysgenesis carry a glaucoma risk.
- Missing the fundus: microcornea, corectopia or iris coloboma oblige you to look for retinochoroidal and disc coloboma.
- Promising that dermoid excision will correct the astigmatism.
- Advising complete removal of a lipodermoid: it risks ptosis, dry eye and restriction.
- Forgetting the systemic examination: ears and spine (Goldenhar), teeth and umbilicus (Rieger), kidneys (sporadic aniridia).
- Calling an acquired unilateral corectopia in an adult "congenital": think of ICE syndrome, trauma or surgery.
:::
