## Case as presented
**Mr X, 49.** Examiners said this is usually a short case.
- **Complaints:** redness, pain and blurring of the right eye for 1 week. The redness was sudden and constant, with watering and photophobia.
- **Past episodes:** similar episodes in the right eye about every 6 months for 5 years, each settling with treatment. Periorbital swelling of the right eye last year, which settled on antibiotics.
- **Ocular history:** glasses since age 12; "LASIK in the right eye 25 years ago".
- **Medical history:** diabetes and hypertension for 18 years; ischaemic heart disease for 2 years.

## Think like this
1. **Which pathology explains the most?** Here it is bilateral axial high myopia, with lengths of 31 mm and corrections of −12 and −21 D. Then come the right recurrent anterior uveitis, the complicated cataracts and the claimed refractive surgery.
2. **Corroborate every history with an examination finding.** Records are often missing, so:
   - claimed LASIK → flap edge, keratometry, topography, and ask whether he was ever spectacle-free afterwards;
   - claimed periorbital inflammation → lacrimal gland, orbit.
3. **If he still needs −12 D after "LASIK"**, think of regression, partial correction or no surgery at all. **Keratometry** settles it: an ablated cornea is flat, while 44–45 D suggests no ablation.
4. **In a uveitis case, show you can read the anterior segment:**
   - KP type and age;
   - signs of previous attacks (pigment on the lens, posterior synechiae, iris atrophy);
   - granulomatous or non-granulomatous.

## Viva: questions and expected answers
Q: How do you make a definite diagnosis of pseudoproptosis? (2:58:06)
A: Proptosis is forward displacement of a normal globe. Pseudoproptosis is apparent prominence from a **large globe (axial myopia)**, a shallow orbit, lid retraction or enophthalmos of the other eye. **Compare the axial lengths** and the facial and orbital symmetry.
Q: He says he had LASIK. What will you look for? (2:59:56)
A: The flap edge at the slit lamp, which becomes visible when you press gently near it. **Keratometry**: a flat cornea after an ablation; with 44–45 D, refractive surgery is unlikely. Topography if available. History of seeing without glasses after the surgery.
Q: You have no topography in a peripheral centre. Which simple test tells you the cornea was operated on? (3:05:32)
A: **Keratometry.**
Q: What is your primary diagnosis? (3:03:10)
A: Bilateral high axial (possibly pathological) myopia. Secondary: right recurrent acute non-granulomatous anterior uveitis. Tertiary: complicated cataract in both eyes, claimed refractive surgery in the right eye.
Q: What type of cataract is it at 49 with high myopia and uveitis? (3:04:52)
A: **Complicated cataract**, not senile.
Q: How do you describe the hyperaemia in anterior uveitis? (3:07:24)
A: Circumcorneal (ciliary) congestion.
Q: What would you describe in this high-myopic fundus? (3:08:22)
A: Disc size, colour and CDR; tilted disc; peripapillary atrophy or temporal crescent; chorioretinal degeneration around the disc and at the macula; **posterior staphyloma**; vessel course; tessellation; (added) lacquer cracks and Foster–Fuchs spot.
Q: How do you tell fresh KPs from old ones? (3:09:34)
A: (added) Fresh KPs are white, round and fluffy. Old KPs are shrunken, pigmented and crenated (glassy). Mutton-fat KPs mean granulomatous inflammation; fine KPs mean non-granulomatous.

:::say
Mr X, a 49-year-old man with diabetes, hypertension and ischaemic heart disease, has had redness, pain, photophobia and blurring of the right eye for one week. He has had similar episodes about every six months for five years. He has worn glasses since childhood and reports refractive surgery in the right eye 25 years ago, but he still needs −12 D in that eye and −21 D in the left; axial lengths are about 31 mm in both. The right eye shows circumcorneal congestion, fine keratic precipitates and 2+ cells with 1+ flare. Both eyes have posterior subcapsular cataract and myopic fundi with large discs, extensive peripapillary atrophy and chorioretinal degeneration. My diagnosis is bilateral high axial (probably pathological) myopia, with right recurrent acute non-granulomatous anterior uveitis and complicated cataract in both eyes. I would confirm any earlier refractive surgery with keratometry.
:::

## Images and their points

:::recall
- **Primary → secondary → tertiary** diagnosis. Here: high axial myopia → recurrent anterior uveitis → complicated cataract.
- Pseudoproptosis needs proof: **axial length**, orbit symmetry.
- Claimed LASIK → flap edge, **keratometry**, topography.
- Not "senile" below 45 in India; myopia + uveitis = **complicated cataract**.
- Few findings → describe each one fully (myopic fundus, staphyloma).
:::
