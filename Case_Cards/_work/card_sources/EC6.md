## At a glance
- **What they are:** acquired, age-related or secondary changes in a previously normal cornea. Two are on the list: **climatic droplet keratopathy (CDK)**, an ultraviolet (UV)-induced protein deposit, and **band-shaped keratopathy (BSK)**, calcium in Bowman layer.
- **Why examiners keep them:** quick cases that test slit-lamp description (amber oil droplets against a chalky plaque with holes), causes, systemic work-up, and one procedure you must describe step by step: ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) chelation.
- **Both lie in the interpalpebral strip**, the cornea exposed between the lids, and start at 3 and 9 o'clock.
- **Diagnosis line for S.No 35:** "Both eyes (BE) climatic droplet keratopathy (spheroidal degeneration), primary corneal type, grade …, with BE pseudophakia (posterior chamber intraocular lens, PCIOL)".
- **Diagnosis line for S.No 40:** "Right eye (RE) calcific band-shaped keratopathy over a leucomatous corneal opacity, secondary to … (state the cause: chronic uveitis, phthisis, old keratitis); vision perception of light with accurate projection".
- In S.No 40 the leucoma usually limits vision more than the band: decide whether the eye has visual potential before planning surgery.

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
Mr X, a 68-year-old farmer who has worked in the sun all his life, had cataract surgery in both eyes three years ago; vision has stayed slightly hazy, with glare in bright light, but there is no pain. Best-corrected vision is 6/12 in the right eye and 6/18 in the left; intraocular pressure is 16 mm Hg in each eye. Both eyes have pingueculae and many small, translucent, golden-yellow oil-like droplets beneath the epithelium and in the superficial stroma, in a horizontal band running from the nasal and temporal limbus towards the centre; the band crosses the visual axis in the left eye only. There are no elevated nodules and no epithelial defects. Both eyes have a well-centred posterior chamber intraocular lens in the bag, with fine pseudoexfoliation material at the pupil margin; the discs are healthy and the maculae show a few dry drusen.

**Diagnosis: Both eyes climatic droplet keratopathy, primary corneal type, grade 1 in the right eye and grade 3 in the left, with both eyes pseudophakia (posterior chamber intraocular lens in the bag) and pseudoexfoliation.**

For S.No 40: **Right eye calcific band-shaped keratopathy over a leucomatous corneal opacity, secondary to (cause), with perception of light and accurate projection.**
:::

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Define a corneal degeneration. How does it differ from a dystrophy?
A: Acquired deterioration of previously normal corneal tissue from age, environment or disease. Unlike a dystrophy it is not inherited, starts later, is often unilateral or asymmetric, peripheral or interpalpebral rather than central, may be vascularised, and is linked to ocular or systemic disease.

Q: What is CDK, and what are its other names?
A: Accumulation of translucent golden globules beneath the epithelium, in Bowman layer and anterior stroma of the exposed cornea, sometimes the conjunctiva. Also called spheroidal degeneration, Labrador keratopathy, Bietti nodular degeneration, chronic actinic keratopathy, keratinoid degeneration, fisherman's keratitis and corneal elastosis.

Q: What causes CDK?
A: Chronic UV light, direct and reflected from snow, sand or water, alters plasma proteins diffusing from limbal vessels into the exposed cornea; age, wind, sand and dust microtrauma, low humidity and dry eye add to it. Commonest in elderly men working outdoors.

Q: What are the droplets made of?
A: Extracellular protein with elastotic features, rich in photo-oxidised tryptophan, tyrosine, cystine and cysteine; eosinophilic on haematoxylin and eosin; not calcium. They darken from yellow to brown with age.

Q: Classify CDK.
A: Type 1, primary corneal: bilateral, interpalpebral, age and UV related, cornea otherwise normal. Type 2, secondary corneal: after scars, keratitis, trachoma, lattice dystrophy or glaucoma; may be unilateral or central. Type 3, conjunctival: at 3 and 9 o'clock with pinguecula, alone or with type 1.

Q: How is CDK graded?
A: Johnson and Ghosh: trace, a few lesions at one end of the strip; grade 1, interpalpebral periphery only; grade 2, centre involved without visual loss; grade 3, centre involved with visual loss; grade 4, grade 3 plus elevated nodules.

Q: What is band-shaped keratopathy?
A: Deposition of calcium hydroxyapatite in the epithelial basement membrane, Bowman layer and anterior stroma, forming a horizontal interpalpebral band.

Q: Why is calcium deposited in the exposed strip?
A: Evaporation concentrates calcium and phosphate in the tear film there; loss of carbon dioxide raises the local pH, which makes calcium phosphate less soluble; inflammation adds to the alkalinity. Raised serum calcium or phosphate makes it worse.

Q: List the causes of band keratopathy.
A: Ocular: chronic uveitis (especially JIA), phthisis bulbi, long-standing glaucoma, chronic corneal oedema, interstitial keratitis, silicone oil in the anterior chamber, dry eye. Systemic: hypercalcaemia (hyperparathyroidism, vitamin D excess, sarcoidosis, milk–alkali syndrome, myeloma, bone metastases), renal failure, gout. Chemical: mercury, phosphate-containing drops. Familial; idiopathic.

Q: Why is JIA important?
A: The chronic anterior uveitis of oligoarticular, antinuclear antibody-positive young girls is white and painless, so band keratopathy, complicated cataract and glaucoma (the triad taught with Still disease) may be the first signs; such children need slit-lamp screening every 3–6 months.

### Examination and signs
Q: How do you tell CDK from band keratopathy at the slit lamp?
A: CDK: amber, translucent, oil-like droplets of varying size, raised, starting at the limbus, often with pinguecula. Band keratopathy: grey-white chalky flat plaque with a clear zone at the limbus and small holes, later white nodules, and gritty to a blade.

Q: Why is there a clear zone at the limbus, and holes in the band?
A: Limbal vessels buffer the pH and Bowman layer ends near the limbus, so calcium does not settle there. The holes (the "Swiss-cheese" look) are where corneal nerves pierce Bowman layer.

Q: Where does band keratopathy start?
A: Usually peripherally at 3 and 9 o'clock, the two ends spreading to meet centrally; in chronic uveitis and phthisis it may begin centrally.

Q: What is a leucomatous opacity?
A: A dense white scar hiding iris details. A nebula is faint (iris seen); a macula is denser (iris margins seen); a leucoma is total; with iris incarcerated it is an adherent leucoma.

Q: What else must you examine in S.No 40?
A: Light perception and projection, IOP, globe size (phthisis), signs of uveitis or silicone oil, the other eye, and B-scan if the fundus is hidden: this decides between optical and comfort surgery.

Q: What is the differential diagnosis of an interpalpebral corneal band?
A: Calcific band keratopathy; CDK; Vogt limbal girdle; urate (gout) keratopathy, brown and non-calcific; Salzmann nodules; calcareous degeneration; familial band keratopathy.

Q: Why look for pseudoexfoliation in a CDK patient?
A: Both are age and UV related and are reported together in sunny, outdoor populations, alongside cataract and pterygium. Pseudoexfoliation means weak zonules and glaucoma risk, so check the pupil margin, intraocular lens centration and IOP.

### Investigations
Q: What systemic work-up do you order for band keratopathy?
A: When no ocular cause is obvious: serum calcium and phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, uric acid, urea and creatinine, parathyroid hormone, vitamin D; angiotensin-converting enzyme, lysozyme and chest X-ray for sarcoidosis; serum protein electrophoresis for myeloma; antinuclear antibody in a child with uveitis.

Q: How is calcium confirmed in tissue?
A: Basophilic granules on haematoxylin and eosin in the basement membrane and Bowman layer; black with von Kossa stain, red with alizarin red.

Q: Which imaging helps plan treatment?
A: Anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) shows the depth of calcium or droplets: anterior disease suits chelation, keratectomy or phototherapeutic keratectomy (PTK); deep scarring needs lamellar keratoplasty. B-scan assesses the posterior segment behind an opaque cornea.

### Management
Q: How do you manage CDK?
A: Most need nothing beyond UV protection (wraparound sunglasses, hat) and lubricants; bandage contact lens for erosions. Central visual loss: superficial keratectomy with a no. 15 blade, with or without amniotic membrane; excimer PTK for lesions within about 100 µm or residual irregularity; lamellar keratoplasty for deep scars.

Q: Why is penetrating keratoplasty risky in CDK?
A: Grafts fail often: these eyes have dry, UV-damaged surfaces, poor goblet cell function, lid problems and continuing exposure.

Q: CDK and cataract: which first?
A: Central droplets distort keratometry and limit post-operative vision. Either PTK first and cataract surgery once keratometry is stable, or cataract surgery first and PTK about three months later; counsel that the result depends on the cornea.

Q: When and how do you treat band keratopathy?
A: Treat the cause first. Indications: reduced vision, irritation, epithelial breakdown, cosmesis. Mild: lubricants, bandage contact lens. Definitive: EDTA chelation, superficial keratectomy, PTK to smooth, amniotic membrane, lamellar keratoplasty for deep deposits.

Q: Describe EDTA chelation step by step.
A: Topical anaesthesia and speculum; scrape the epithelium off the plaque with a no. 15 blade; apply disodium EDTA 1.5–3% (about 0.05 mol/L) on a soaked cellulose sponge or in a corneal trephine well for 5–30 minutes, renewing it; scrape away the softened calcium with a blunt spatula; irrigate; smooth with a diamond burr if needed; bandage contact lens.

Q: How do you make 3% EDTA in theatre?
A: Dilute 2 mL of 15% disodium EDTA with 8 mL of normal saline. Dipotassium EDTA is a cheaper alternative.

Q: What is the care after chelation?
A: Bandage contact lens until healed, topical antibiotic, cycloplegic, lubricants, and a non-steroidal or acetate-steroid drop; avoid phosphate-containing steroid drops, which re-precipitate calcium; review for recurrence.

Q: What is the role of amniotic membrane?
A: As a graft or patch after removal it provides a basement membrane, speeds epithelial healing and reduces inflammation, scarring and recurrence; useful when the surface is poor.

Q: Why not PTK alone for band keratopathy?
A: Calcium ablates at a different rate from stroma, so PTK alone leaves an irregular surface; use it after chelation, with a masking fluid, to smooth what remains.

Q: How do you manage S.No 40 if the eye has no visual potential?
A: Treat for comfort and appearance: lubricants, bandage contact lens, chelation for painful nodules, cosmetic tinted contact lens or prosthetic shell; conjunctival flap for persistent pain; evisceration for a blind, painful eye.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: What are the complications of CDK?
A: Epithelial breakdown over nodules, recurrent erosion, sterile ulceration, microbial keratitis, thinning, rarely perforation, and visual loss when central.

Q: What is the outlook after EDTA chelation?
A: Vision and comfort improve in most otherwise healthy eyes; recurrence over years is common if the cause persists (uveitis, silicone oil, hypercalcaemia), and chelation can be repeated.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: Arcus senilis: what is it and when does it matter?
A: Lipid in the peripheral stroma, first above and below, later a ring with a sharp outer edge, a clear zone at the limbus and a hazy inner edge. Under about 50 (arcus juvenilis): check fasting lipids for familial hypercholesterolaemia. Unilateral arcus: carotid disease or hypotony on the side without arcus.

Q: What is Vogt limbal girdle?
A: Chalky white crescents at the nasal and temporal limbus in older people. Type 1 has a clear zone from the limbus and holes (an early calcific band); type 2 has no clear zone (elastotic, like pinguecula). Harmless.

Q: What is Salzmann nodular degeneration?
A: Grey-white or bluish raised nodules in the mid-periphery after chronic keratitis (trachoma, phlyctenulosis, vernal keratoconjunctivitis), contact lens wear, or in middle-aged women; collagen and hyaline beneath the epithelium. Lubricants; superficial keratectomy peels them off a cleavage plane; PTK; mitomycin C reduces recurrence.

Q: How do you tell Terrien marginal degeneration from Mooren ulcer?
A: Terrien: painless superior peripheral thinning, mostly men, bilateral, intact epithelium, vessels crossing the gutter, lipid line at the steep central edge, against-the-rule astigmatism, rare perforation. Mooren: painful, ulcerated, overhanging edge, no clear zone, interpalpebral, needs immunosuppression.

Q: What is pellucid marginal degeneration?
A: Bilateral inferior band of thinning 1–2 mm wide from 4 to 8 o'clock, 1–2 mm from the limbus, with protrusion above the band (a "beer-belly" profile); no vessels, lipid or inflammation; high against-the-rule astigmatism; "crab-claw" or "kissing doves" topography. Rigid or scleral lenses, cross-linking, crescentic lamellar graft.

Q: How does calcareous degeneration differ from band keratopathy?
A: Calcareous degeneration affects all layers of a badly damaged, often phthisical and scarred cornea; band keratopathy is superficial, at Bowman layer, and confined to the exposed strip.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Calling amber oily droplets "band keratopathy": calcium is chalky white with holes and a clear limbal zone; CDK is golden and translucent.
- Forgetting occupation and sun exposure in CDK, and the systemic causes (calcium, kidneys, sarcoidosis, JIA) in band keratopathy.
- Not stating light perception and projection, IOP and B-scan in S.No 40: an eye with a leucoma, band and phthisis may have no visual potential.
- Describing chelation vaguely: examiners expect the strength (1.5–3%), the epithelium removed first, the time (5–30 minutes) and a bandage lens.
- Prescribing phosphate-containing steroid drops after chelation.
- Offering penetrating keratoplasty first for CDK: superficial keratectomy or PTK usually suffices and grafts fare badly.
- Missing pseudoexfoliation at the pupil margin or the intraocular lens in a CDK patient.
:::
