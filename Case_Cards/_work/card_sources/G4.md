## At a glance
- **Primary angle closure**: the peripheral iris closes the drainage angle, usually from **pupillary block**. The ISGEO classification stages it as primary angle-closure suspect (**PACS**), primary angle closure (**PAC**) or primary angle-closure glaucoma (**PACG**).
- Why it is kept: common, bilateral, stable and full of signs. Gonioscopy was the task in all four cases last year.
- Examiners test: the Van Herick grade, gonioscopy with indentation, the stage, the mechanism, imaging, laser iridotomy and the acute attack.

**Keywords the examiner listens for:** pupillary block · iridotrabecular contact · peripheral anterior synechiae · Van Herick · indentation gonioscopy · occludable angle · plateau iris · glaukomflecken · laser peripheral iridotomy · ultrasound biomicroscopy · anterior segment optical coherence tomography · mannitol · Fincham test · combined mechanism

### Step 1 — Visual acuity and glasses
**Do:** Snellen acuity in each eye, then with pinhole and with her glasses. Note a plus (hypermetropic) prescription.

**Record:** "RE 6/12, 6/9 with +2.00 D sphere; LE 6/9."

Q: Why does vision blur in an attack?
A: **Corneal changes.** The pressure first distorts the corneal lamellae. Later the epithelium becomes oedematous, which also gives the haloes.
Q: Why note the glasses?
A: **Hypermetropia** means a short eye, small cornea and thick lens. This crowded front segment is a risk factor for PACG.

### Step 2 — Pupils, before any drop
**Do:** Note size and shape. Test direct and consensual reactions, then the swinging torch-light test.

**Record:** "Pupils round, 3 mm, reacting to light, no RAPD." After an attack: "RE pupil mid-dilated, vertically oval, sluggish."

Q: Why is the pupil mid-dilated, vertically oval and non-reacting in an acute attack?
A: **Sphincter ischaemia.** Above 40 mm Hg the sphincter is ischaemic and cannot constrict. Severe pain also drives sympathetic overactivity, which dilates the pupil vertically.
Q: What does a relative afferent pupillary defect mean here?
A: **Advanced glaucoma** in that eye. It points to PACG rather than PAC.

### Step 3 — Anterior chamber depth: pen-torch, then Van Herick
**Do:** Shine a pen-torch from the temporal side and note how much iris is lit. At the slit lamp, put a slit-thin beam 1 mm inside the temporal limbus at 60°. Compare the peripheral depth with the corneal thickness, and note the central depth.

**Record:** "Pen-torch from the temporal side: two-thirds of iris lit, grade 2, shallow chamber. Van Herick ratio 1:1/4, grade 2: shallow chamber with occludable angles."

Q: How do you grade the chamber by Van Herick?
A: **Van Herick** compares peripheral depth with corneal thickness. Grade 4: one thickness or more (normal). Grade 3: ¼ to ½. Grade 2: ¼. Grade 1: under ¼. Grade 0: iridocorneal contact. Grades 0–2 are suspicious of angle closure.
Q: Can Van Herick replace gonioscopy?
A: **No.** It is a guide to who needs gonioscopy. Peripheral depth under ¼ corneal thickness means the angle may be occludable.
Q: The centre is deep but the periphery shallow. What do you suspect?
A: **Plateau iris**: a deep axial chamber, a narrow peripheral chamber and a flat iris. Pupillary block gives a shallow chamber with a forward-bowed iris.

### Step 4 — Slit lamp: signs of a past attack
**Do:** Before any drop, examine cornea, chamber, iris and lens in order for signs of an old attack.

**Record:** "RE: sectoral iris atrophy superiorly, glaukomflecken, Krukenberg spindle. LE: no signs of a previous attack."

Q: What are the signs of a previous attack?
A: **Iris pigment** on the endothelium with endothelial loss, and PAS. The iris shows sectoral atrophy, or whorling from sectoral infarction of the sphincter. Also look for posterior synechiae, a mid-dilated sluggish pupil and glaukomflecken. Field loss and cupping may follow.
Q: What are glaukomflecken?
A: **Glaukomflecken** are small grey-white anterior subcapsular or capsular opacities in the pupillary zone. They come from infarction of lens fibres in an attack.
Q: What is Vogt's triad?
A: **Glaukomflecken, sectoral iris atrophy and a Krukenberg spindle.** Together they are characteristic of PACG after an attack.

### Step 5 — The peripheral iridotomy
**Do:** Find the opening with diffuse light. Describe, in the department's order: margin, site, surrounding iris, then patency by retro-illumination.

**Record:** "Iridotomy at 11 o'clock, ragged irregular edges, hypopigmented surrounding iris. Light reflex seen on retro-illumination: suggestive of a patent laser peripheral iridotomy."

Q: How do you tell a laser iridotomy from a surgical iridectomy?
A: **Laser**: ragged edges, any clock hour, pigment dispersion around it; it can close. **Surgical**: clean-cut triangular edges near the limbal incision, usually at 12 o'clock; it cannot close.
Q: Does a light reflex on retro-illumination prove patency?
A: **Not on its own.** Seeing the **anterior lens capsule** through the opening confirms it.
Q: Why at 11–1 o'clock?
A: **The upper lid covers it.** Some prefer the upper nasal iris, to avoid diplopia and macular burns.

### Step 6 — Applanation tonometry
**Do:** Topical anaesthetic and fluorescein. Goldmann applanation before gonioscopy and before any dilatation. Note the time.

**Record:** "Tn by GAT: RE 16 mm Hg, LE 18 mm Hg at 10 am."

Q: Why record the time?
A: **Diurnal variation.** Pressure is higher in the morning. The normal swing is 3–6 mm Hg, and larger in glaucoma.
Q: When will you measure central corneal thickness?
A: **After any attack settles.** Goldmann applanation is calibrated for a mean central corneal thickness of 520 µm. A thinner cornea reads falsely low, a thicker one falsely high, by about 0.7 mm Hg per 10 µm. Thin is under 500 µm; thick is over 570 µm.

### Step 7 — Gonioscopy: static, then indentation
**Do:** Both eyes, before dilatation. First a Goldmann two-mirror lens, without pressure, which would distort the angle. Ask the patient to look towards the mirror to see into a narrow angle. Name the structures from behind forward: ciliary body band, scleral spur, trabecular meshwork, Schwalbe's line. Count the quadrants where the posterior meshwork is hidden. Then press with a Zeiss four-mirror lens (indentation or dynamic gonioscopy). Clear an oedematous cornea with 1–2 drops of anhydrous glycerin. Write the deepest structure seen in each quadrant (Shaffer grades under Classification).

**Record:**

:::gonio
RE | S=Closed | T=SL | I=TM | N=Closed
LE | S=SL | T=SL | I=TM | N=SL
caption: Deepest structure before indentation (SL Schwalbe's line, TM trabecular meshwork). ITC in 3 quadrants per eye
:::

"RE: ITC in 3 quadrants; indentation opens the temporal angle to the scleral spur; PAS superiorly and nasally. LE: ITC in 3 quadrants, all opening on indentation."

Q: What is an occludable angle?
A: An **occludable angle** shows no pigmented trabecular meshwork in at least three quadrants without indentation. That is Shaffer grade 1 or 0.
Q: What does indentation add?
A: **Indentation gonioscopy** forces aqueous into the angle. If it opens, closure is appositional; if not, there are PAS, whose extent I note. A double hump means plateau iris. Indentation can even break an attack.
Q: How do you tell PAS from iris processes, and what causes PAS?
A: **Iris processes** are lacy and fenestrated; the structures behind stay visible. **PAS** are solid and hide them. Causes of PAS: primary angle closure, anterior uveitis, iridocorneal endothelial syndrome, intraocular surgery (a wound leak, for example) and trauma.
Q: What do you expect in acute and chronic closure?
A: **Acute**: an occludable angle. **Chronic**: PAS, which appear late. After an attack the meshwork may be pigmented.

### Step 8 — Fundus, when it is safe
**Do:** No dilatation until iridotomies are done. View the undilated disc with a direct ophthalmoscope or a +78 D or +90 D lens. Dilate after a patent iridotomy, usually about 2 weeks after laser.

**Record:** "Undilated, +90 D: disc vertically oval, CDR 0.7, inferior rim thinning."

Q: Why did you not dilate?
A: **Dilatation is contraindicated** until iridotomies are done; it can start an attack. In plateau iris syndrome it can do so even after a patent iridotomy.
Q: What does the disc look like after an attack?
A: **Hyperaemic and oedematous**, sometimes with haemorrhages. It becomes pale and cupped after 9–10 days. Chronic closure gives cupping like open-angle glaucoma.

### Step 9 — Fields by confrontation
**Do:** Confrontation in each eye, all four quadrants, before the diagnosis. Confirm with automated perimetry.

**Record:** "Fields full to confrontation in both eyes."

Q: What happens to the field during an attack?
A: **Generalised constriction.**
Q: Why do the disc and fields decide the stage?
A: **Optic neuropathy** is the only difference between PAC and PACG.

## Differential diagnosis
@widths 20 22 24 34
| Condition | Points for | Points against | How to tell apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary open-angle glaucoma | Raised IOP, cupping | Shallow chambers; haloes in dim light | **Gonioscopy**: open angle in all quadrants |
| Plateau iris syndrome | Narrow angle, attacks | Shallow central chamber, convex iris | Deep central chamber, flat iris; indentation: **double hump**; stays occludable after a patent iridotomy |
| Phacomorphic glaucoma | Shallow chamber, high IOP | Both chambers shallow; clear lenses; hypermetrope | **Asymmetric** shallowing with a swollen (intumescent) cataract in one eye |
| Malignant glaucoma | Shallow chamber, high IOP | No recent intraocular surgery | **Diffuse** shallowing; ultrasound shows forward-rotated ciliary processes |
| Neovascular glaucoma | Closed angle, high IOP | No diabetes or vein occlusion | **New vessels** on iris or angle, ectropion uveae |
| Drug-induced ciliary effusion | Shallow chamber, high IOP | No topiramate or sulpha drug | Drug history; ultrasound shows ciliary body engorgement |

**Exclude first:** secondary closure — look at the fellow eye. Primary closure tends to be bilateral; a wide-open fellow angle suggests another diagnosis.

**Open-angle mimics of an attack:** glaucomatocyclitic crisis, herpes simplex keratouveitis, herpes zoster ophthalmicus, and pigmentary, exfoliative and phacolytic glaucoma. Gonioscopy shows an open angle.

## How I will manage
### Aim
To relieve pupillary block, open the angle and protect the optic nerve in both eyes. Management of angle closure is essentially surgical; drugs control the pressure first.

### The ladder
1. **Acute attack, IOP above 50 mm Hg:** miotics fail on an ischaemic iris. I give **mannitol 20% intravenously, 1–2 g/kg over 20–30 minutes**. Without nausea or vomiting, I add oral **acetazolamide** 250–500 mg stat, then 250 mg four times a day. Oral **glycerol 50%, 1–1.5 g/kg**, suits non-diabetics. **Apraclonidine 0.5%** lowers the pressure quickly.
2. **Trigger — IOP below 40 mm Hg:** **pilocarpine 2%** four times over 30 minutes, then 6-hourly. I add **timolol 0.5%** twice daily, **brimonidine 0.2%** three times daily and **prednisolone acetate 1%** four times daily. Ibuprofen 500 mg twice daily and an antiemetic help. I indent the central cornea, review hourly, and do gonioscopy of both eyes once the cornea clears.
3. **Trigger — quiet eye, clear cornea:** **Nd:YAG (neodymium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet) iridotomy in both eyes**. Hazy cornea and closure under two-thirds: **surgical iridectomy**, with YAG iridotomy in the fellow eye. Closure over two-thirds, or persistent inflammation with high IOP: **primary trabeculectomy**.
4. **PACS:** LPI, or observation with IOP, gonioscopy and disc checks. I advise LPI with frequent dilatation (diabetes, hypertension, macular degeneration) or an attack in the other eye. Hypermetropia, poor follow-up, intermittent symptoms and predisposing drugs also favour it.
5. **PAC and PACG:** LPI, plus drugs or surgery to control the IOP.
6. **Trigger — gonioscopy 1 week after LPI:** still occludable despite a patent iridotomy means **plateau iris syndrome**. I then do **argon laser peripheral iridoplasty**. An open angle with high IOP means a **combined mechanism**: timolol plus a prostaglandin, dorzolamide, brimonidine or pilocarpine.
7. **Trigger — failure of laser and drugs:** **trabeculectomy with mitomycin C or 5-fluorouracil**. Indications: PACG not controlled by iridotomy and/or iridoplasty, progression on maximal medical therapy, or synechial closure over 270°.
8. **Lens extraction**, when the lens drives the closure (approved passage below).

**Lens extraction**
- **Phacoemulsification with intraocular lens implantation** corrects hypermetropia, deepens the anterior chamber and opens the angle: IOP control in almost all patients with normal preoperative IOP, and in up to **80%** with raised preoperative IOP. *(Kanski — not in your practical books)*
- **EAGLE study**: clear lens extraction is more effective and more cost-effective than LPI in PAC with IOP **> 29 mm Hg**, or in PACG. *(Kanski — not in your practical books)*
- **PACS — ZAP trial**: LPI has a modest prophylactic effect; offer it only to those at highest risk of PACG. If significant ITC persists after LPI and there is symptomatic cataract, lens extraction usually opens the angle. *(Kanski — not in your practical books)*

### Follow-up and counselling
- After laser: IOP at 1 hour. A rise of 8 mm Hg or more, or IOP over 30 mm Hg, is a spike. With advanced damage or a high IOP, I also give oral acetazolamide. Prednisolone acetate 1% four times daily for about a week.
- At 1 week: IOP, patency, inflammation and gonioscopy. Then gonioscopy at every review, because the lens keeps thickening.
- Counsel: no dilating drops, antihistamines or antidepressants without telling the doctor. Come at once with haloes, pain or redness.
- After intravenous mannitol, do not get up at once: hypotension.
- Bring first-degree relatives for examination.

## What you must know
### Definition
"**Primary angle closure** (PAC) is appositional or synechial closure of the anterior-chamber angle caused by pupillary block. The angle closure may or may not be associated with elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) or glaucomatous optic neuropathy, and may occur in either an acute or chronic form. This entity does not include secondary forms of angle closure induced by other causes, for example subluxed lens."

In plain words: aqueous is held back at the pupil, and the peripheral iris blocks the angle. **Gonioscopy**, **IOP**, **disc and field** make the diagnosis.

- **Relative pupillary block**: lens–iris touch blocks flow through the pupil. Aqueous collects behind the iris and bows it forward.
- **Plateau iris configuration**: the plateau iris shape without angle closure.
- **Plateau iris syndrome**: an anteriorly placed ciliary body pushes the peripheral iris forward. The angle stays occludable despite a patent iridotomy.
- **Combined mechanism**: angle-closure and open-angle elements together.
- **Acute congestive attack**: circumcorneal congestion, corneal oedema, pain and vomiting. The chamber is shallow, the pupil vertically oval, the IOP very high.

### Classification
**The ISGEO classification** uses IOP, gonioscopy, disc and fields:

@widths 14 32 26 28
| Stage | Gonioscopy | IOP and PAS | Disc and field |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PACS** | **ITC in 3 or more quadrants** (almost 270°) | Normal IOP, **no PAS** | Normal |
| **PAC** | ITC in 3 or more quadrants | **Raised IOP and/or PAS** | Normal |
| **PACG** | ITC in 3 or more quadrants | Raised IOP and/or PAS | **Glaucomatous optic neuropathy**: cupping with field loss |

**By mechanism**
- **Primary**: with pupillary block (acute, subacute or chronic closure); without block (plateau iris).
- **Secondary with block**: swollen lens, mobile lens (ectopia lentis, microspherophakia), miotics. In spherophakia, miotics slacken the zonules and the lens moves forward: **inverse glaucoma**.
- **Secondary without block**: anterior "pulling" (neovascular glaucoma, iridocorneal endothelial syndrome). Posterior "pushing" (ciliary block, cysts, nanophthalmos, tumours, suprachoroidal haemorrhage).
- **Four sites**: iris (pupillary block), ciliary body (plateau iris), lens (phacomorphic), behind the iris (malignant glaucoma).

**Clinical stages:** prodromal (haloes, white eye, IOP 40–60 mm Hg). Then constant instability, then the acute congestive attack. Chronic closed-angle: PAS, high IOP between attacks. Absolute glaucoma: no light perception, stony hard.

- **Scheie**, by structures hidden: wide open, all seen. Grade 1: iris root hard to see. Grade 2: ciliary body band hidden. Grade 3: posterior meshwork hidden. Grade 4: only Schwalbe's line seen.
- **Spaeth**, four parameters: iris-root insertion **A–E** and angle width **10–40°**. Iris contour is **S** (steep, convex), **R** (regular, flat) or **Q** (queer, deeply concave). Meshwork pigmentation is graded **0–4**.

### Pathogenesis
- **Pupillary block** traps aqueous behind the iris, and the peripheral iris bows onto the meshwork.
- Contact is first **appositional**, then **synechial**; outflow falls and IOP rises.
- **Age** thickens the lens, moves it forward and narrows the pupil.
- **Risk factors**: older age, women, family history, Asian or Inuit descent. Also hypermetropia, short axial length, small cornea, shallow chamber and a thick lens. An anteriorly placed ciliary body adds risk.

### Complications
- **Of an attack**: glaukomflecken, iris atrophy, PAS, posterior synechiae, endothelial loss, cupping; finally **absolute glaucoma**.
- **Of trabeculectomy in angle closure**: a shallow chamber and malignant glaucoma.

### Prognosis
- PACS progresses to acute closure in **6–10%**, and to PAC or PACG in **17–35%**. The fellow eye closes in about **50% within 5 years**.
- **Good**: appositional closure that opens on indentation; early iridotomy.
- **Poor**: synechial closure over 270°, plateau iris, a combined mechanism, advanced cupping.

- *(extra)* **AS-OCT** may detect occludability better than gonioscopy, but gonioscopy remains the **gold standard**.

## Viva questions
### Why did you ask…?
Q: Why did you ask about coloured haloes?
A: **Corneal epithelial oedema** acts as a diffraction grating: blue-green in the centre, yellow-red outside. Haloes point to intermittent closure.
Q: Why did you ask about diabetes and hypertension?
A: **Frequent dilated examinations** can start an attack in a shallow chamber. This is an indication for LPI in a suspect.
Q: Why did you ask about drugs?
A: **Anticholinergic and adrenergic drugs** dilate the pupil and precipitate closure. Topiramate and sulpha drugs cause ciliary effusion, which closes the angle without block.

### Diagnosis and differentials
Q: Why does a mid-dilated pupil precipitate an attack?
A: **Maximum pupillary block.** At mid-dilatation the iris–lens contact is greatest, and the slack peripheral iris bunches onto the meshwork. Full dilatation lifts the iris off the lens.
Q: Why is pain worse than in open-angle glaucoma at the same pressure?
A: **Rate of rise.** Pain follows how fast the pressure rises, not the level.
Q: What is the Fincham test?
A: The **Fincham test** moves a stenopaeic slit across the pupil. A glaucoma halo stays intact; a cataract halo breaks into colours.
Q: How do you diagnose and treat plateau iris syndrome?
A: **Plateau iris**: deep axial chamber, shallow periphery, flat iris. Indentation shows a sine-wave, double-hump iris, because the iris hangs over the anterior ciliary processes. Ultrasound biomicroscopy confirms it. The angle stays occludable after a patent iridotomy. Most eyes also have pupillary block, so iridotomy comes first, then argon laser iridoplasty.
Q: What is the combined mechanism?
A: **Combined mechanism**: after iridotomy for an attack, the angle opens but the IOP stays high. I treat the open-angle component.

### Investigations
Q: What is ultrasound biomicroscopy?
A: **Ultrasound biomicroscopy** is 50–100 MHz ultrasound of structures in front of the pars plana. Axial resolution is 25 µm, lateral 50 µm. Penetration is about 4 mm.
Q: What does ultrasound biomicroscopy show in each mechanism?
A: **Pupillary block**: forward iris bowing. **Plateau iris**: double hump on indentation. **Malignant glaucoma**: everything pressed against the cornea, with or without supraciliary fluid.
Q: How does UBM differ from AS-OCT?
A: **UBM** needs contact, fluid, a skilled operator and lying down; the supine position may falsely widen the chamber. It sees behind the iris pigment epithelium and through opaque media. **AS-OCT** works by low-coherence interferometry. It is non-contact and faster, with higher axial resolution and a wider field, but needs a clear cornea.
Q: What angle measurements can UBM give?
A: **Angle width** as a linear distance or an angle, and the iris–meshwork area. Also iris thickness and contour, chamber depth and the iris–ciliary body relation, followed after treatment.
Q: What are the provocative tests?
A: **Mydriatic, dark room, prone, prone dark room, phenylephrine–pilocarpine and triple tests.** In the dark-room test the patient sits awake in the dark for 90 minutes. A rise over 8 mm Hg, or closure, is positive. Done with ultrasound biomicroscopy, dark-room testing gives objective results.

### Management — drugs
Q: Why mannitol first in an attack?
A: **Mannitol** lowers the pressure fast by drawing water from the vitreous. Above 50 mm Hg miotics fail. It is the intravenous drug of choice: safe in diabetes and renal failure.
Q: How does pilocarpine work here, and when do you start it?
A: **Pilocarpine** constricts the pupil and pulls the peripheral iris off the meshwork, relieving pupillary block. I start it once IOP is below 40 mm Hg.
Q: Why give apraclonidine or brimonidine with laser iridotomy?
A: **To prevent the pressure spike.** One drop goes before and one after laser. Apraclonidine 1% brings down short-term rises after iridotomy quickly.

### Management — laser and surgery
Q: Why does iridotomy work?
A: **Laser peripheral iridotomy** lets aqueous bypass the pupil. The pressure gradient goes, the iris flattens and the chamber deepens.
Q: What are the indications for laser iridotomy?
A: **Angle closure** at any stage, and the fellow eye. Also pseudophakic pupillary block, malignant glaucoma, combined mechanism, microspherophakia and phacomorphic glaucoma.
Q: What are the contraindications?
A: **Corneal oedema** or opacity, a flat chamber and a totally synechial angle. Also uveitis, neovascular glaucoma and iridocorneal endothelial syndrome.
Q: Why Nd:YAG rather than argon?
A: **Nd:YAG** (1064 nm) uses photodisruption and works on all iris colours. Argon is thermal, needs pigment and more energy, and closes more often. Argon suits anticoagulated patients.
Q: What does the Abraham lens do?
A: **The Abraham lens** (+55 D button) steadies the eye and keeps the lids apart. It magnifies the periphery and concentrates energy. Its pressure stops bleeding.
Q: What are the signs of penetration?
A: **A sudden gush of aqueous** with a plume of pigment, and sudden deepening of the chamber.
Q: What if the iridotomy does not penetrate?
A: **Retreat** after a few days, or choose another site. Thick dark irides often need thermal (argon) pre-treatment first.
Q: Why does lens extraction help in angle closure?
A: The lens thickens and moves forward with age. Phacoemulsification with intraocular lens implantation corrects hypermetropia, deepens the anterior chamber and opens the angle; in the EAGLE study, clear lens extraction was more effective than LPI in PAC with IOP > 29 mm Hg, or PACG. *(Kanski — not in your practical books)*
Q: What problems do you expect at phacoemulsification in this short, crowded eye?
A: **A shallow anterior chamber** raises the risk of a posterior capsule rent. A hypermetropic eye with a shallow chamber is also a cause of corneal oedema after cataract surgery. I choose the lens-power formula by axial length. Hoffer Q suits 20–22 mm; Holladay II or Hoffer Q suits under 20 mm.
Q: When would you do trabeculectomy in angle closure?
A: **Trabeculectomy**: PACG uncontrolled by iridotomy and drugs, progression, or synechial closure over 270°. Non-penetrating surgery fails, because the meshwork lies close to the iris root.

### Complications
Q: What are the complications of LPI?
A: **Common**: IOP spike, uveitis, hyphaema, focal cataract, posterior synechiae, glare, corneal damage. **Rare**: aqueous misdirection, herpetic keratouveitis, retinal haemorrhage, macular hole.
Q: How do you recognise and treat malignant glaucoma?
A: **Malignant glaucoma** follows surgery, with diffuse shallowing and high or normal IOP. Ultrasound shows forward-rotated ciliary processes. Hyperosmotics help; Nd:YAG hyaloidotomy opens the anterior hyaloid.
Q: How do you manage absolute glaucoma? *(extra)*
A: **Comfort first**: antiglaucoma drops, steroids and atropine. Then cyclodestruction or retrobulbar alcohol, which lasts 3–6 months. Last, evisceration or enucleation.

### Recent advances
Q: Can imaging replace gonioscopy?
A: **No.** Anterior segment optical coherence tomography may detect occludability better. But gonioscopy remains the gold standard; it shows PAS directly.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Dilating before gonioscopy and iridotomy.
- Calling an iridotomy patent on retro-illumination alone.
- Pressing during static grading, or skipping indentation.
- Staging without the disc and fields.
- Calling every attack PACG: without glaucomatous optic neuropathy, it is primary angle closure.
- Pilocarpine while the pressure is above 40 mm Hg.
- Forgetting the fellow eye and the relatives.
- Saying imaging replaces gonioscopy.
:::

## Say-it script
### Opening (about 1 minute)
:::say
My patient, Mrs X, is a fifty-eight-year-old woman. She has come for regular follow-up for glaucoma, after laser to both eyes three years ago.

She was apparently normal four years ago. Then she had repeated episodes of blurred vision with coloured haloes around lights and brow ache, in both eyes. These came on in dim light, while watching television in a dark room, and while reading. She has worn plus glasses for distance since the age of forty. An ophthalmologist found raised pressure and narrow angles. Laser was done to both eyes, and she was started on timolol 0.5% eye drops twice daily. Since then she has had no haloes.

There is no history of a sudden painful red eye with headache and vomiting. There is no trauma or eye surgery. She has not used dilating drops, antihistamines or antidepressants. She is not diabetic or asthmatic and has no sulpha allergy. Her mother was treated for glaucoma. She uses timolol regularly, without side effects.
:::

### Closing (2 minutes)
:::say
To summarise, Mrs X is a fifty-eight-year-old hypermetropic woman with intermittent haloes in dim light. Laser was done to both eyes three years ago, and she uses timolol twice daily.

Best-corrected visual acuity is 6/9 in each eye. The anterior chambers are shallow, Van Herick grade two. Each eye has an iridotomy at eleven o'clock with ragged edges, hypopigmented surrounding iris and a light reflex on retro-illumination. These are suggestive of patent laser peripheral iridotomies. Intraocular pressure by Goldmann applanation is 16 and 18 millimetres of mercury at ten in the morning. Gonioscopy shows iridotrabecular contact in three quadrants in each eye. On indentation gonioscopy the angles open, except superiorly and nasally in the right eye, where there are peripheral anterior synechiae. The undilated discs show cup–disc ratios of 0.7 and 0.6, with inferior rim thinning. Confrontation fields are full.

My diagnosis is both eyes primary angle-closure glaucoma, status post laser peripheral iridotomy with patent iridotomies. Intraocular pressure is under control on one medication.

I would confirm the stage with automated perimetry and nerve fibre layer scans. I would look for plateau iris with ultrasound biomicroscopy and anterior segment optical coherence tomography. I will continue timolol, repeat gonioscopy at every visit, and consider trabeculectomy if the disease progresses.
:::

## Quick recall
:::recall
- ISGEO: ITC in 3 or more quadrants (almost 270°). PAC adds raised IOP and/or PAS. PACG adds optic neuropathy.
- Van Herick grade 2 = ¼ corneal thickness; under ¼ may be occludable.
- Attack IOP 50–100 mm Hg. Miotics fail above 50; pilocarpine 2% only below 40.
- Mannitol 20% 1–2 g/kg intravenously over 20–30 min. Glycerol 50% 1–1.5 g/kg, not in diabetes.
- Nd:YAG LPI: 2–5 mJ, 1–3 pulses, 150–200 µm, 11–1 o'clock.
- Spike after LPI: rise of 8 mm Hg or more, or IOP over 30 mm Hg.
- Trabeculectomy: synechial closure over 270°; after an attack, closure over two-thirds.
- PACS: acute closure 6–10%, PAC or PACG 17–35%. Fellow eye 50% in 5 years.
- Vogt's triad: glaukomflecken, sectoral iris atrophy, Krukenberg spindle.

**Mnemonic:** **I CAN SEE TILL SCHWALBE'S LINE**: Iris root, Ciliary body, Scleral spur, Trabecular meshwork, Schwalbe's line. These are the angle structures from the iris root forwards.
:::
