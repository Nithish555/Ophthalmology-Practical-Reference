## At a glance
- **Primary angle closure**: the peripheral iris closes the angle, usually by **pupillary block**; staged as primary angle-closure suspect (**PACS**), primary angle closure (**PAC**) or primary angle-closure glaucoma (**PACG**); often seen after laser peripheral iridotomy (LPI).
- Kept: bilateral, stable, full of signs — shallow anterior chamber (AC), iridotomies, glaukomflecken, iris atrophy. Gonioscopy was the listed task in all 4.
- Examiners test: Van Herick grade, gonioscopy **with indentation**, the stage, the mechanism, imaging, LPI, the acute attack.

## Case format — history
**Chief complaint:** "Mrs X, a 58-year-old woman, has come for regular follow-up for glaucoma, after laser to both eyes 3 years ago."

*House wording:* "Patient was apparently normal ___ years ago, after which she had episodes of coloured haloes and brow ache in dim light; laser was done to both eyes and she was started on timolol 0.5% eye drops twice daily."

@widths 50 50
| What to ask | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **History of present illness:** haloes, brow ache, "smoke-filled room" blurring — in the dark, reading, after dilating drops, with stress | **Intermittent closure**: a mid-dilated pupil gives maximum pupillary block |
| Sudden painful loss of vision with redness, headache, vomiting — or gradual painless blurring | **Acute attack** (now or past) versus **chronic** closure, mostly without symptoms |
| Laser and surgery (which eye, when); drops, compliance; old intraocular pressure (IOP) and field records | Status after LPI; control; progression |
| Use of glasses | **Hypermetropia**: short eye, thick lens |
| **Negative history:** no coloured haloes | Rules out **intermittent closure**; haloes also occur in incipient cataract (**Fincham test**) |
| No painful red, watering eye with headache and vomiting | Rules out an **acute congestive attack** and its mimics: **glaucomatocyclitic crisis**, uveitic glaucoma, herpetic keratouveitis |
| No trauma, no recent eye surgery | Rules out **traumatic** angle closure and **malignant glaucoma** |
| No diabetic eye disease, vein occlusion or retinal laser | Rules out **neovascular glaucoma** and ciliary body engorgement |
| No topiramate or sulpha drugs | Rules out drug-induced **ciliary effusion** |
| **Past, drug and surgical:** diabetes, hypertension | Repeated dilatation can start an attack |
| Asthma, heart or kidney disease; drug allergy (sulpha) | Choice of drugs (asthma: no beta-blocker) |
| Anticholinergic or adrenergic drugs (antihistamines, antidepressants, phenylephrine) | Precipitate angle closure |
| **Personal and family:** age, sex; glaucoma in the family | Middle-aged and older **women**; **first-degree relatives** at high risk |

### Special tests
**Anterior chamber depth** (department script)
1. Pen-torch held **temporally**: whole iris lit = **grade 4**, normal depth; **2/3** lit = **grade 2**, shallow.
2. Van Herick: **slit-thin beam, 1 mm inside the temporal limbus, at 60°**; ratio of corneal thickness to the cornea–iris gap.
3. Grades (peripheral depth ÷ corneal thickness): **4** ≥ 1 (1:1, normal depth); **3** ¼ to ½; **2** ¼ (1:1/4); **1** < ¼; **0** iridocorneal contact. Grades 0–2: suspicious of angle closure. Depth **< ¼** corneal thickness: the angle may be occludable.

Record: "Slit-thin slit 1 mm inside the temporal limbus at 60°; ratio 1:1/4; Van Herick grade 2, suggestive of shallow anterior chamber with occludable angles."

**Gonioscopy** (technique: card G9)
1. Both eyes, before dilatation. Corneal oedema: wait, or clear it with **anhydrous glycerin**.
2. **Static**: Goldmann **two-mirror** lens without pressure (pressure distorts the angle). Posterior trabecular meshwork (TM) not seen = **ITC**; count the quadrants.
3. **Indentation**: Zeiss **four-mirror** lens, press: angle opens = **appositional**; stays shut = **synechial** (PAS; note extent). **Double hump** = plateau iris.

Record: "Right eye (RE): ITC in 3 quadrants; indentation opens temporal to scleral spur (appositional); superior and nasal PAS. Left eye (LE): ITC in 3 quadrants, all opening on indentation."

**Iridotomy** (slide order)
1. **Margin**: ragged, irregular = laser (surgical iridectomy: sharp, well-defined).
2. **Site**: clock hour.
3. **Surrounding iris**: hypopigmented, with pigment dispersion.
4. **Retro-illumination test for patency**: light reflex seen = patent.

Record: "Iridotomy at 11 o'clock, ragged irregular edges, hypopigmented surrounding iris, light reflex on retro-illumination — suggestive of a patent laser peripheral iridotomy."

## Differential diagnosis
@widths 24 26 50
| Condition | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Primary open-angle glaucoma | Raised IOP, cupping | **Open angle**; normal AC depth |
| Plateau iris syndrome | Narrow angle, attacks | **Deep central AC**; flat iris; **double hump**; still occludable after a patent LPI; younger |
| Lens-induced closure | Shallow AC, high IOP | Intumescent or subluxated lens |
| Malignant glaucoma | Shallow AC, high IOP | After surgery; **diffuse** shallowing; ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM): ciliary processes rotated forward |
| Neovascular glaucoma | Closed angle, high IOP | **New vessels** on iris or angle |

Primary angle closure tends to be bilateral: a **wide-open angle in the fellow eye** points to another diagnosis.

## What you must know
### Definition
**Primary angle closure**: **appositional or synechial closure** of the angle caused by **pupillary block**, with or without raised IOP or optic neuropathy, acute or chronic; secondary closure is excluded.

### Classification
**The ISGEO classification**, based on IOP, gonioscopy, disc and fields:

@widths 14 30 26 30
| Stage | Gonioscopy | IOP and PAS | Disc and field |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PACS** | **ITC in ≥ 3 quadrants** (almost 270°) | Normal IOP, **no PAS** | Normal |
| **PAC** | ITC in ≥ 3 quadrants | **Raised IOP and/or PAS** | Normal |
| **PACG** | ITC in ≥ 3 quadrants | Raised IOP and/or PAS | **Glaucomatous optic neuropathy**: cupping, field loss |

**Mechanism — four sites**: iris (**pupillary block**, most common) · ciliary body (**plateau iris**) · lens (thick, intumescent or subluxated) · behind the iris ("pushing": **malignant glaucoma**, cysts). Secondary "pulling": neovascular glaucoma, iridocorneal endothelial syndrome.

**Gonioscopic grading — Shaffer**:

@widths 24 14 40 22
| Grade | Width | Deepest structure seen | Closure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **4** wide open | 35–40° | Ciliary body | Impossible |
| **3** open | 25–35° | Ciliary body | Impossible |
| **2** moderately narrow | 20° | Pigmented TM | Possible but unlikely |
| **1** extremely narrow | 10° | Only Schwalbe's line; maybe anterior TM | Possible |
| **Slit** | < 10° | None; no obvious iridocorneal contact | Portions appear closed |
| **0** closed | — | Iridocorneal contact | Complete |

**Occludable angle**: pigmented TM not visible (Shaffer 1 or 0) without indentation in at least **three quadrants**.

**Spaeth** — four parameters: iris-root insertion **A–E** · angle width **10–40°** · peripheral iris contour **S, R, Q** · TM pigmentation **0–4**.

### Pathogenesis
- **Relative pupillary block**: lens–iris touch traps aqueous in the posterior chamber, bowing the peripheral iris onto the TM; worst at **mid-dilatation** (dim light).
- **Anatomy**: short axial length, small cornea, thick lens and anteriorly placed iris–lens diaphragm; the lens thickens and moves forward with age.
- Contact is first **appositional**, then **synechial**; outflow falls and IOP rises. Above **40 mm Hg** the sphincter is ischaemic; infarcted lens fibres form glaukomflecken.

### Investigations
@widths 20 36 44
| Test | Why | Expected finding |
|---|---|---|
| Gonioscopy with indentation | **Gold standard**; stage; appositional versus synechial | ITC ≥ 3 quadrants; PAS |
| Anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) | Non-contact, quick, 360°; lens vault | Occludable angle; **cannot see behind the pigmented iris epithelium** |
| UBM (50–100 MHz) | Mechanism: ciliary body, sulcus, zonules; works through corneal oedema | Iris bowing (block); double hump on indentation (plateau iris); cysts |
| Perimetry; central corneal thickness (after an attack) | Stage (PAC versus PACG); applanation accuracy | Glaucomatous field loss |

### Management
**By stage**: PACS — **LPI**, or observation with serial gonioscopy; PAC and PACG — **LPI plus medical or surgical** IOP control.

**LPI in PACS** if: frequent dilatation (diabetes, hypertension, age-related macular degeneration), an attack in the other eye, hypermetropia, intermittent symptoms, predisposing drugs, unreliable follow-up.

**Neodymium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet (Nd:YAG) iridotomy** (photodisruption; all iris colours)
1. **Pilocarpine 1%**; brimonidine or apraclonidine half an hour before; **proparacaine 0.5%**.
2. **Abraham lens** (+55 D button): steadies the eye, magnifies, concentrates energy.
3. **Site**: peripheral third, in a **crypt**, **11–1 o'clock** under the upper lid.
4. **2–5 mJ, 1–3 pulses**; optimal size **150–200 µm**.
5. **End point**: sudden gush of aqueous or pigment; the chamber **deepens**.
6. **After**: IOP at **1 hour** (spike: rise **≥ 8 mm Hg** or IOP **> 30 mm Hg**); **prednisolone acetate 1% four times a day for 5–7 days**; **gonioscopy at 1 week**.

**Argon** (thermal): anticoagulated patients; pre-treatment of thick dark irides. No LPI with corneal oedema, a flat AC, a totally synechial angle, uveitis or neovascular glaucoma.

**Acute attack — step by step**
1. **Mannitol 20% intravenously, 1–2 g/kg over 20–30 minutes**: miotics fail while IOP is very high.
2. If no nausea or vomiting: **acetazolamide 250–500 mg orally stat, then 250 mg four times a day**, or **glycerol 50% orally, 1–1.5 g/kg** (not in diabetes); **apraclonidine 0.5%**.
3. IOP **below 40 mm Hg**: **pilocarpine 2%** four times in 30 minutes, then 6-hourly.
4. **Timolol 0.5% twice daily** with **brimonidine 0.2% three times daily**.
5. **Prednisolone acetate 1% four times a day**; **ibuprofen 500 mg twice daily**; antiemetic.
6. Review **hourly**; gonioscopy of both eyes once the cornea clears. Indenting the central cornea may open the angle.
7. Quiet eye, clear cornea: **Nd:YAG iridotomy in both eyes**.
8. Hazy cornea, closure **< 2/3**: **surgical iridectomy** (YAG in the fellow eye). Closure **> 2/3** or persistent inflammation: **primary trabeculectomy**.

**Plateau iris syndrome**: LPI first; if still occludable, **argon laser iridoplasty**.

**Medical after LPI**: as in primary open-angle glaucoma (card G1).

**Trabeculectomy** (5-fluorouracil or mitomycin C): IOP uncontrolled despite LPI and drugs; progression on maximal tolerated therapy; **synechial closure > 270°**. Non-penetrating surgery is relatively contraindicated.

**Lens extraction**
- **Phacoemulsification with intraocular lens implantation** corrects hypermetropia, deepens the anterior chamber and opens the angle: IOP control in almost all patients with normal preoperative IOP, and in up to **80%** with raised preoperative IOP. *(Kanski — not in your practical books)*
- **EAGLE study**: clear lens extraction is more effective and more cost-effective than LPI in PAC with IOP **> 29 mm Hg**, or in PACG. *(Kanski — not in your practical books)*
- **PACS — ZAP trial**: LPI has a modest prophylactic effect; offer it only to those at highest risk of PACG. If significant ITC persists after LPI and there is symptomatic cataract, lens extraction usually opens the angle. *(Kanski — not in your practical books)*

**Fellow eye**: prophylactic LPI early. **Follow-up**: serial gonioscopy; high IOP with an open angle after LPI = **combined mechanism**: treat the open-angle part.

### Complications
- **Of an attack**: glaukomflecken, iris atrophy, PAS, posterior synechiae, endothelial loss, cupping; **absolute glaucoma**.
- **Of LPI**: IOP spike, uveitis, hyphaema, focal cataract, glare; rarely **malignant glaucoma** (treated by Nd:YAG **hyaloidotomy**).

### Prognosis and counselling
- PACS → acute closure **6–10%**; PAC or PACG **17–35%**. Fellow eye: angle closure in about **50% within 5 years**.
- A patent LPI is not a cure (plateau iris, PAS, combined mechanism): lifelong review.
- Counsel: no dilating or anticholinergic drugs before iridotomy; report haloes or pain at once; examine relatives.

### Recent advances
- AS-OCT may detect occludability better than gonioscopy, but gonioscopy remains the **gold standard**; imaging is complementary, and UBM is still needed behind the iris.

## Viva questions
### Why did you ask / examine…?
Q: Why did you ask about coloured haloes?
A: Corneal epithelial oedema acts as a diffraction grating (blue-green centre, yellow-red outside). Also: incipient cataract, mucus, vitreous opacities, tilted intraocular lens.
Q: Why did you ask about diabetes and hypertension?
A: Repeated dilatation can start an attack: an indication for LPI.
Q: Why did you ask about drugs?
A: Anticholinergics and adrenergics precipitate angle closure; topiramate and sulpha drugs cause ciliary effusion.
Q: Why did you not dilate the pupil?
A: Contraindicated until iridotomies are done; I used a +90 D lens.
Q: Why did you look for glaukomflecken?
A: A sign of a previous attack. **Vogt's triad**: glaukomflecken, sectoral iris atrophy, Krukenberg spindle.

### Diagnosis
Q: Define PACS, PAC and PACG.
A: All: ITC in ≥ 3 quadrants. PACS: normal IOP, disc and fields, no PAS. PAC: raised IOP and/or PAS, normal disc. PACG: plus glaucomatous optic neuropathy.
Q: Plateau iris configuration versus syndrome?
A: Configuration: anterior ciliary processes hold the peripheral iris forward, without closure. Syndrome: the angle stays occludable despite a patent LPI.
Q: Why is the pupil mid-dilated and fixed in an attack?
A: Above 40 mm Hg the sphincter is ischaemic; pain adds sympathetic overactivity.
Q: Why is pain worse than in open-angle glaucoma?
A: Pain follows the speed of the rise, not the level.
Q: What is the Fincham test?
A: A stenopaeic slit moved across the pupil: the glaucoma halo stays intact; the cataract halo breaks into colours.

### Investigations
Q: AS-OCT or UBM?
A: AS-OCT: non-contact, quick, 360°; cannot see behind the pigmented iris epithelium. UBM: 50–100 MHz, axial resolution 25 µm, 4–5 mm deep; sees the ciliary body, even through corneal oedema.
Q: How are UBM and AS-OCT findings described?
A: UBM: echogenic, hypoechoic, echolucent; radial scan for the angle, transverse for clock-hour extent. AS-OCT: hyperreflective, hyporeflective.
Q: Name the provocative tests.
A: Mydriatic, dark room (90 minutes), prone (45–60 minutes), prone dark room, phenylephrine–pilocarpine, triple. Mydriatic or dark room positive: rise > 8 mm Hg or closure on gonioscopy.

### Management and surgery
Q: Why does iridotomy work?
A: Aqueous bypasses the pupil; the gradient goes, the iris flattens and the chamber deepens.
Q: Why Nd:YAG rather than argon?
A: Photodisruption works on all iris colours, with fewer late closures; argon needs pigment.
Q: Why place the iridotomy at 11–1 o'clock?
A: The upper lid covers it; the upper nasal iris avoids diplopia and macular burns.
Q: Why does lens extraction help in angle closure?
A: The lens thickens and moves forward with age. Phacoemulsification with intraocular lens implantation corrects hypermetropia, deepens the anterior chamber and opens the angle; in the EAGLE study, clear lens extraction was more effective than LPI in PAC with IOP > 29 mm Hg, or PACG. *(Kanski — not in your practical books)*
Q: When would you do trabeculectomy?
A: IOP uncontrolled despite LPI and drugs, progression, synechial closure > 270°, or an attack with closure > 2/3.
Q: Why repeat gonioscopy after LPI?
A: To prove the block is relieved (lens capsule seen through the iridotomy), map PAS and unmask plateau iris.

### Complications
Q: Complications of LPI?
A: IOP spike, uveitis, hyphaema, focal cataract, posterior synechiae, glare; rarely malignant glaucoma.

### Recent advances
Q: Newer imaging for angle closure?
A: AS-OCT and UBM. AS-OCT may detect occludability better than gonioscopy, but gonioscopy remains the gold standard.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Dilating before gonioscopy and iridotomy.
- "Patent" on retro-illumination alone: the lens capsule must be seen through it.
- Pressing during static grading, or skipping indentation.
- Staging without disc and fields: PAC and PACG differ only by optic neuropathy.
- Pilocarpine first when IOP is above 40 mm Hg.
- Forgetting the fellow eye.
:::

## Say-it script
:::say
My patient, a fifty-eight-year-old hypermetropic woman, has come for regular follow-up. Three years ago she had evening haloes and brow ache with raised pressure; laser was done to both eyes, and she uses timolol twice daily. She has no history of a painful red eye with vomiting, trauma, eye surgery, sulpha drugs or asthma.

Best-corrected visual acuity is 6/9 in each eye. The anterior chambers are shallow, Van Herick grade two. Each eye has an iridotomy at eleven o'clock with ragged edges, hypopigmented surrounding iris and a clear light reflex on retro-illumination, suggestive of patent laser peripheral iridotomies. Intraocular pressure by Goldmann applanation is 16 and 18 millimetres of mercury at ten in the morning. Gonioscopy shows iridotrabecular contact in three quadrants in each eye; on indentation the angles open, except superiorly and nasally in the right eye, where there are peripheral anterior synechiae. The undilated discs show cup–disc ratios of 0.7 and 0.6 with inferior rim thinning. Confrontation fields are full.

My diagnosis is both eyes primary angle-closure glaucoma, status post laser peripheral iridotomy with patent iridotomies, with intraocular pressure under control on one medication.

I would confirm with automated perimetry, look for plateau iris with ultrasound biomicroscopy, continue timolol, repeat gonioscopy at each review, and consider trabeculectomy if the disease progresses.
:::

## Quick recall
:::recall
- ISGEO: **ITC in ≥ 3 quadrants**; PAC adds raised IOP and/or PAS; PACG adds optic neuropathy.

**Mnemonic:** **I CAN SEE TILL SCHWALBE'S LINE** — Iris root, Ciliary body, Scleral spur, Trabecular meshwork, Schwalbe's line: the angle structures from the iris root forwards.
