## At a glance
- **Staphyloma**: a localised bulge (ectasia) of a thinned, weakened outer coat of the eye — cornea or sclera — **lined by uvea**, which shows through as a bluish-black colour. An ectasia without uveal lining (keratoconus) is not a staphyloma.
- **Ciliary staphyloma**: the bulge lies over the ciliary body, about **2–8 mm behind the limbus**. It is usually the end result of **long-standing raised intraocular pressure (IOP)** — absolute or secondary glaucoma, or buphthalmos — or of **scleritis** or **trauma**.
- Kept because the sign is striking, and the viva covers the types of staphyloma, glaucoma, the blind painful eye and the differential from **ciliary body melanoma**.
- **S.No 30**: "Both eyes ciliary staphyloma (give the clock hours), secondary to long-standing uncontrolled glaucoma (or buphthalmos, or old scleritis — state the cause), with absolute glaucoma (state vision)."
- A **bilateral** staphyloma points to a bilateral cause: late buphthalmos (primary congenital glaucoma), bilateral chronic glaucoma, or necrotising scleritis in rheumatoid arthritis.

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
Mr X, a 40-year-old man, has had no useful vision in either eye for several years, with bouts of pain and redness; he was told in childhood that his eyes were large and watery and has used drops only irregularly. Both eyes show bluish-black, lobulated scleral bulges about 2–8 mm behind the limbus, from 9 to 3 o'clock superiorly in the right eye and 10 to 2 o'clock in the left; they transmit light, and no nodule or sentinel vessel is seen. The corneas measure 14 mm with horizontal Haab's striae and mild oedema, the anterior chambers are deep, and the pupils are mid-dilated and fixed with iris atrophy. Vision is no perception of light in the right eye and perception of light with inaccurate projection in the left; IOP is 42 and 38 millimetres of mercury (mmHg) by rebound tonometry. B-scan shows total disc cupping and no intraocular mass in either eye.

**Diagnosis (S.No 30):** Both eyes ciliary staphyloma secondary to long-standing uncontrolled glaucoma from late buphthalmos (primary congenital glaucoma), with absolute glaucoma in the right eye and near-absolute glaucoma in the left.

**If due to scleritis:** Both eyes ciliary staphyloma after necrotising anterior scleritis (scleromalacia perforans) in rheumatoid arthritis, with normal IOP.
:::

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Define staphyloma. How does it differ from ectasia?
A: A staphyloma is a localised bulge of a thinned, weakened outer coat (cornea or sclera) lined by uveal tissue, which shows through as bluish-black. An ectasia is a bulge of the outer coat without uveal lining, such as keratoconus or keratoglobus. Both form when a weak wall yields to normal or raised IOP.

Q: Classify staphylomas by site.
A: Anterior (corneal): an ectatic corneal scar lined by iris after a perforated ulcer. Intercalary: at the limbus, lined by the iris root. Ciliary: 2–8 mm behind the limbus, lined by ciliary body. Equatorial: at the equator, lined by choroid, where the vortex veins leave the eye. Posterior: behind the equator, usually at the posterior pole in pathological myopia.

Q: Why does a staphyloma look blue?
A: The thin, stretched sclera lets the dark uveal pigment show through, and light scattered by the thin collagen adds a bluish tint.

Q: What causes ciliary staphyloma?
A: Long-standing raised IOP: absolute glaucoma after chronic angle closure, neovascular, uveitic or traumatic glaucoma; buphthalmos. Scleritis: necrotising scleritis, scleromalacia perforans. Trauma: perforating injury, scleral scar. Surgery: surgically induced necrotising scleritis, a thin scleral flap. Rarely herpes zoster or tuberculous scleritis.

Q: Why does the ciliary region give way?
A: The sclera is thinnest (about 0.3 mm) just behind the rectus insertions, close to this zone; the anterior ciliary vessels pierce it here, leaving weak canals; and this zone is the usual site of anterior scleritis. In an infant the whole sclera is distensible.

Q: Where does the ciliary body lie on the surface?
A: From the scleral spur, just behind the limbus, to the ora serrata about 6 mm (nasal) to 7 mm (temporal) behind the limbus: the pars plicata in front (about 2 mm) and the pars plana behind (about 4 mm). A bulge 2–8 mm behind the limbus therefore overlies it.

Q: How do ciliary and intercalary staphylomas differ?
A: An intercalary staphyloma sits at the limbus, lined by the iris root, usually after a peripheral corneal perforation or limbal wound. A ciliary staphyloma lies further back over the ciliary body, usually from raised IOP or scleritis.

### Examination and signs
Q: How would you describe a staphyloma?
A: Eye; site in clock hours; distance from the limbus; size; shape (single, multiple, lobulated, ring-shaped); colour; surface vessels; consistency on gentle touch; transillumination; relation to the rectus insertions; and the associated corneal, IOP and fundus findings.

Q: How do you measure IOP in such an eye?
A: Applanation if the cornea is regular; otherwise a rebound tonometer, Tono-Pen, or digital estimate. Avoid Schiotz indentation and pressure on the thin area: it risks rupture, and low scleral rigidity makes indentation readings falsely low.

Q: What does transillumination show?
A: In a dark room a bright light on the globe or through the pupil passes through a thin staphyloma, which glows. A ciliary body melanoma or other solid pigmented mass blocks it and casts a shadow, often larger than the visible lesion.

Q: What are the signs of absolute glaucoma?
A: No perception of light; stony-hard eye; dilated circumcorneal vessels; corneal oedema with reduced sensation; iris atrophy and ectropion uveae; fixed, mid-dilated pupil; glaucomatous cataract; total cupping. Late results: ciliary and equatorial staphyloma, bullous keratopathy, corneal ulcer, endophthalmitis, phthisis.

Q: Which signs point to buphthalmos as the cause?
A: Corneal diameter over 12 mm in infancy (often 14 mm or more), horizontal or concentric Haab's striae, deep anterior chamber, thin bluish sclera, axial myopia, subluxated lens, cupped disc, and a history of watering and photophobia in infancy.

### Investigations
Q: Which investigations would you do?
A: B-scan (cupping, detachment, intraocular mass, axial length); UBM (scleral thickness, uveal lining, ciliary body, angle); gonioscopy if possible; fields if vision allows. For scleritis: blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, rheumatoid factor, anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), syphilis serology, chest X-ray. Magnetic resonance imaging if a tumour is suspected.

Q: How do ultrasound and UBM separate a staphyloma from a ciliary body melanoma?
A: Staphyloma: thin sclera bowing outward with a thin uveal lining and an echo-free cavity continuous with the eye; no mass. Melanoma: a solid dome or mushroom-shaped mass with low-to-medium internal reflectivity, acoustic hollowness and choroidal excavation, sometimes with extrascleral extension.

Q: What is the differential diagnosis of a bluish-black scleral bulge or patch?
A: Ciliary body melanoma with extrascleral spread (sentinel vessels, sector cataract); scleral thinning after scleritis or scleromalacia perforans (flat unless IOP is high); uveal prolapse after injury or surgery; intercalary or equatorial staphyloma; ocular melanocytosis (flat, slate-grey); Axenfeld nerve loop; senile scleral plaque (flat, grey, near the horizontal recti); blue sclera (diffuse); conjunctival naevus or melanoma (moves with the conjunctiva).

Q: How do you tell scleromalacia perforans from a staphyloma?
A: Scleromalacia perforans is a painless necrotising scleritis in older women with long-standing rheumatoid arthritis: a yellow necrotic plaque separates and leaves bare, dark uvea, usually flat. It bulges only if IOP rises — then it has become a staphyloma.

### Management
Q: What are the principles of management?
A: Find and treat the cause; control IOP to stop further stretching; protect the eye from rupture with polycarbonate glasses; relieve pain; reinforce the sclera if perforation threatens; give cosmetic rehabilitation; remove the eye only if it is blind and painful, or if a tumour cannot be excluded.

Q: How would you lower the IOP?
A: Aqueous suppressants: timolol 0.5% twice daily, dorzolamide 2% three times daily, brimonidine 0.2% three times daily (not under 2 years); oral acetazolamide 250 mg four times daily for short periods; intravenous mannitol 20% 1–2 g/kg for an acute rise; a prostaglandin analogue (latanoprost 0.005% at night). Topical atropine 1% and prednisolone acetate 1% for comfort in an inflamed blind eye.

Q: Which glaucoma surgery suits an eye with a ciliary staphyloma?
A: In a blind or near-blind eye, cyclodestruction. Trabeculectomy over thin sclera risks perforation and bleb failure, and pressure swings may enlarge the staphyloma. If the eye still has useful vision, a glaucoma drainage device placed away from the thin zone, with a patch graft over the tube, is safer.

Q: How is transscleral cyclophotocoagulation done here?
A: Diode laser (810 nm), contact probe about 1.2 mm behind the limbus; 1.5–2 s at 1,500–2,000 mW; 12–24 burns, sparing 3 and 9 o'clock (long posterior ciliary vessels and nerves). Find the ciliary body by transillumination because the anatomy is distorted, and do not fire over the thinned bulge. Steroid and cycloplegic drops afterwards.

Q: What about cyclocryotherapy?
A: A 3.5 mm probe at about −60 to −80 °C placed over the ciliary body, usually 180° at a time sparing 3 and 9 o'clock; effective but painful, with more inflammation, hypotony and phthisis than laser. It is now used mainly when laser is unavailable.

Q: When is a scleral patch graft indicated?
A: Impending or actual perforation, or a thin, enlarging staphyloma in a seeing eye: donor sclera, processed pericardium, fascia lata or cornea is sutured over the thin area after IOP control. In scleritis, control the inflammation with systemic immunosuppression first.

Q: How do you manage a blind painful eye with a staphyloma?
A: Atropine, steroid and aqueous suppressant drops; cyclodestruction; retrobulbar injection of absolute alcohol (about 1 ml, after local anaesthetic) or chlorpromazine for pain; finally evisceration or enucleation with an orbital implant and a prosthesis.

Q: Evisceration or enucleation?
A: Evisceration (contents removed, sclera and muscles kept) gives better motility and is simpler, but must never be done if an intraocular tumour has not been excluded, and a very thin sclera may need partial excision. Enucleation is chosen when a tumour is possible or tissue is needed; place an implant (silicone, polymethyl methacrylate, hydroxyapatite or porous polyethylene) and fit the prosthesis at about 6 weeks.

Q: What is a cosmetic shell?
A: A thin, custom-painted scleral shell worn over a blind, disfigured but comfortable eye to match the fellow eye. A large staphylomatous globe may not fit one, and the shell may irritate; such eyes often need evisceration first.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: What complications can follow?
A: Rupture after trivial trauma, with uveal prolapse or expulsive haemorrhage; sympathetic ophthalmia after rupture or surgery; enlargement; pain; exposure keratopathy and corneal ulcer; endophthalmitis; phthisis bulbi; disfigurement.

Q: What is the prognosis?
A: Visual prognosis is poor: a staphyloma signals years of uncontrolled IOP with optic atrophy, so the aim is a comfortable, safe and acceptable-looking eye. After scleritis, the prognosis depends on control of the systemic vasculitis, which can be life-threatening if untreated.

Q: What is sympathetic ophthalmia, and why does it matter here?
A: A bilateral granulomatous panuveitis following penetrating injury or surgery to one eye, from autoimmunity to uveal antigens; a ruptured staphyloma with uveal prolapse can trigger it. Treat with systemic steroids and immunosuppressants; enucleating a hopeless injured eye within about 2 weeks of injury is the classic preventive step.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: What is micropulse transscleral cyclophotocoagulation?
A: Diode energy delivered in short repeated "on" pulses (duty cycle about 31%) with a sweeping probe, so tissue cools between pulses; it causes less inflammation, hypotony and phthisis than continuous-wave treatment and suits eyes with some vision.

Q: What is endoscopic cyclophotocoagulation?
A: Laser ablation of the ciliary processes under direct endoscopic view, through a limbal or pars plana route, often combined with cataract surgery; it spares the overlying sclera.

Q: What is the treatment of an anterior (corneal) staphyloma?
A: In a blind eye, evisceration or enucleation with a prosthesis; staphylectomy (excision with closure) is historical. In an eye with potential, keratoplasty after IOP control.

Q: Where else in the course do staphylomas appear?
A: Posterior staphyloma in pathological myopia (with lacquer cracks and myopic maculopathy); peripapillary staphyloma among excavated disc anomalies; staphyloma with limbal dermoids and in congenital corneal opacity; and as a complication of absolute glaucoma and necrotising scleritis.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Calling it "scleral thinning" without describing the bulge: a staphyloma is an ectasia **lined by uvea**.
- Not excluding ciliary body melanoma: transilluminate, and do B-scan or UBM before any surgery; never eviscerate an eye whose contents are unknown.
- Pressing on the globe or using a Schiotz tonometer.
- Recommending trabeculectomy over thin sclera, or firing laser over the bulge.
- Missing the cause: measure the cornea and look for Haab's striae; ask about rheumatoid arthritis and scleritis.
- Forgetting protective glasses and the fellow eye.
:::

:::recall
- Staphyloma: bulge of a thinned coat lined by uvea; ectasia has no uveal lining.
- Sites from front to back: anterior, intercalary, ciliary (2–8 mm, ciliary body), equatorial (vortex veins), posterior (myopia).
- Causes: glaucoma (absolute, buphthalmos), scleritis, trauma, surgery.
- Transillumination: a staphyloma glows; a melanoma casts a shadow.
- Treat: lower IOP, cyclodestruction, patch graft, protect; blind and painful: retrobulbar alcohol, evisceration or enucleation, shell.
- Mnemonic for the sites: **"All Ink Comes Evenly Packed"** — Anterior, Intercalary, Ciliary, Equatorial, Posterior.
