## At a glance
- A **corneal opacity** is loss of corneal transparency from a scar, most often after healed keratitis or trauma. By density: **nebula** (faint; iris detail seen through it), **macula** (moderate; iris pattern lost but pupil margin seen), **leucoma** (dense white; iris and pupil hidden).
- **Adherent leucoma**: a leucoma with iris incarcerated in it after a perforation. **Anterior staphyloma**: an ectatic scar lined by iris.
- Why kept: quick to examine, stable signs; tests how you describe an opacity, judge visual potential and choose between optical iridectomy, deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK), penetrating keratoplasty (PK) and cosmetic options.
- Diagnosis lines:
  - S.No 19: "Right eye central leucomatous corneal opacity (size, depth, vessels), non-adherent; left eye adherent leucoma with pupil drawn towards the scar, intraocular pressure (IOP) __, projection __; probably healed corneal ulcers, perforated on the left."
  - S.No 40: "Right eye leucomatous cornea with secondary band-shaped keratopathy; globe size, IOP and projection __." Band keratopathy in detail: card EC6.
- Opacities in both eyes from childhood: ask about keratomalacia (vitamin A deficiency), measles, trachoma and repeated ulcers.

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
Model for S.No 19: "Mr X, a 35-year-old man, has had poor vision in both eyes since childhood after repeated eye infections, and recalls a sudden gush of fluid from the left eye during one of them. The right eye has a central 5 × 4 mm dense white leucoma involving the anterior two-thirds of the stroma, with deep vessels in two quadrants; the iris and pupil are hidden behind it, the AC is of normal depth and the periphery is clear. The left eye has a 4 mm paracentral leucoma with iris incarcerated in it, the pupil drawn up towards the scar and the AC shallow at that site; the eye is firm on digital tonometry. Projection of rays is accurate in all quadrants in the right eye and defective temporally in the left; the B-scan is normal in both eyes."

**Diagnosis: Right eye central leucomatous corneal opacity with deep vascularisation; left eye adherent leucoma with secondary glaucoma; probably healed corneal ulcers, perforated on the left. The right eye has the better prognosis for keratoplasty.**

**S.No 40: "Right eye leucomatous cornea with secondary band-shaped keratopathy; globe normal or phthisical, projection accurate or absent."**
:::

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Define nebula, macula and leucoma.
A: Grades of density of a corneal scar. Nebula: a faint haze through which iris detail is seen. Macula: moderately dense; iris pattern is lost but the pupil margin can be made out. Leucoma: dense and white; iris and pupil cannot be seen through it.

Q: Why can a nebula blur vision more than a leucoma?
A: A nebula over the pupil lets light through but scatters it irregularly, causing glare and irregular astigmatism, so the image is degraded; a dense leucoma blocks light completely, and if clear cornea remains beside it a sharp, though dimmer, image can still form.

Q: Why does a healed ulcer leave an opacity?
A: Bowman's layer does not regenerate, and activated keratocytes (myofibroblasts) lay down new collagen of irregular diameter and spacing, which scatters light; the deeper and wider the stromal loss, the denser the scar. Superficial epithelial lesions heal without a scar.

Q: What is an adherent leucoma and how does it form?
A: A leucoma with iris incarcerated in it. A weak ulcer floor gives way (often on coughing or straining), aqueous escapes, the iris–lens diaphragm moves forward and the iris plugs the hole; scar tissue then incorporates the iris. Signs: iris tissue in the scar, brown pigment on its back, pupil drawn towards it, irregular AC.

Q: What are pseudocornea, anterior staphyloma and kerectasia?
A: Pseudocornea: the fibrous scar that forms over prolapsed iris after a large perforation. Anterior staphyloma: an ectatic pseudocornea lined by iris that bulges under raised IOP, looking bluish because uvea shows through; partial or total. Kerectasia: bulging of a thinned scar without iris in it.

Q: How do anterior synechia, leucoma, adherent leucoma and anterior staphyloma differ?
A: Anterior synechia: iris stuck to the back of the cornea (perforated ulcer, angle closure), surface flat, vision may be normal. Leucoma: dense scar, flat surface, normal AC and pupil. Adherent leucoma: flat scar with iris in it, pupil drawn up, AC irregular, IOP normal or raised. Anterior staphyloma: ectatic, iris-lined scar, AC very shallow or absent, IOP raised.

Q: What causes corneal opacity?
A: Acquired: healed bacterial, fungal, herpetic or Acanthamoeba keratitis; trauma and surgery; chemical injury; trachoma; keratomalacia; interstitial keratitis; degenerations (band keratopathy, spheroidal degeneration); dystrophies; failed graft; long-standing oedema. Congenital, mnemonic STUMPED: Sclerocornea, Tears in Descemet's membrane (congenital glaucoma, forceps), Ulcer, Metabolic (mucopolysaccharidosis), Peters' anomaly, Endothelial dystrophy, Dermoid.

### Examination and signs
Q: How do you describe a corneal opacity?
A: Eye, number, site (central, paracentral, peripheral; relation to the pupil), size in two meridians, shape, density, depth on slit section, margins, surface (flat, facet, ectatic), vessels, calcium or pigment, iris adherence, sensation, AC depth and IOP.

Q: How do you tell superficial from deep corneal vessels?
A: Superficial: bright red, wavy and branching, continuous with conjunctival vessels across the limbus. Deep: straight, parallel, brush-like, ending abruptly at the limbus (from anterior ciliary vessels), dull and seen in the stroma. Ghost vessels are empty deep vessels left after inflammation settles.

Q: What does iris incarceration imply?
A: A past perforation; a high risk of secondary glaucoma; and a harder keratoplasty (synechiolysis needed, more peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS) and rejection). Confirm with the slit lamp, and with anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) or ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) when the view is poor.

Q: Why is IOP important in adherent leucoma?
A: Incarcerated iris drags the iris root forward and PAS close the angle, so secondary angle-closure glaucoma is common; raised IOP stretches the scar into an ectasia or anterior staphyloma and damages the optic nerve. Measure digitally or with a Tono-Pen (Goldmann is unreliable on a scar) and control IOP before any keratoplasty.

Q: How do you assess visual potential when the fundus cannot be seen?
A: Perception and projection of light in four quadrants, colour perception, Maddox rod (an unbroken line), two-point light discrimination, the Purkinje vascular entoptic test, a potential acuity meter or laser interferometer through a clear area, and B-scan for retinal detachment, vitreous disease and globe length; visual evoked potentials and electroretinography when in doubt or in children.

Q: What is a facet?
A: A depressed scar where stroma was lost but the epithelium has healed over it; fluorescein pools in the dip without staining, and it causes irregular astigmatism.

Q: How do you perform the Seidel test?
A: Paint a moistened fluorescein strip (or 2% drops) over the suspected leak and watch under cobalt blue light: aqueous dilutes the dye into a bright green stream (positive). Used for a thin or ectatic scar or a suspected fistula.

### Investigations
Q: Which investigations help plan surgery?
A: AS-OCT for scar depth (DALK if Descemet's membrane and endothelium are spared, PK if full thickness), thinning, iris adhesion and angle; UBM through dense scars; B-scan; specular microscopy of a clear area or the fellow eye; gonioscopy where possible; examination under anaesthesia in children.

Q: What can a B-scan show in these eyes?
A: Retinal or choroidal detachment, vitreous exudates or haemorrhage, a disc cup, axial length (a long eye in staphyloma; a short eye with thick choroid and calcification in phthisis bulbi), and any intraocular tumour in a blind eye.

Q: When are systemic tests needed?
A: In band keratopathy without an ocular cause: serum calcium, phosphate, uric acid, creatinine, parathyroid hormone, vitamin D and angiotensin-converting enzyme (sarcoidosis). In a child with bilateral scars: nutrition and vitamin A status.

### Management
Q: Outline the management of a corneal opacity.
A: It depends on site, depth, visual potential and the patient's need. Peripheral nebula or macula: spectacles or rigid gas-permeable (RGP) contact lens for irregular astigmatism. Superficial scar: phototherapeutic keratectomy (PTK). Central opacity with good potential: DALK if Descemet's membrane and endothelium are healthy, PK if full thickness or adherent. Small central opacity with clear periphery: optical iridectomy. Blind eye: cosmetic contact lens, prosthetic shell or tattooing; blind painful eye: evisceration.

Q: What is PTK and what are its limits?
A: Excimer laser (193 nm) ablation of the anterior stroma to remove superficial scars, band keratopathy remnants and dystrophies, and to smooth the surface. Useful for opacities within about the anterior 100 µm; deeper scars need DALK. Risks: hyperopic shift, haze, recurrence and thinning, so check pachymetry first.

Q: What is an optical iridectomy and when is it done?
A: A sector iridectomy behind clear cornea to make a new pupil, usually inferonasal, for a small central opacity with a clear periphery and a clear lens when keratoplasty is unavailable or risky (for example Peters' anomaly in infants). First check that vision improves when the pupil is dilated; phenylephrine can bridge the wait.

Q: What is the role of keratoplasty in adherent leucoma?
A: PK with synechiolysis, pupil reconstruction and IOP control, with cataract extraction and intraocular lens implantation if the lens is opaque (triple procedure). The prognosis is only fair because PAS, vessels and glaucoma increase rejection and failure.

Q: How is corneal tattooing done?
A: For a disfiguring leucoma in a blind, quiet, non-ectatic eye. Under topical anaesthesia the epithelium over the scar is scraped off; filter paper soaked in 2% gold chloride (brown) or 2% platinum chloride (black) is applied for 2–3 minutes; then 2% hydrazine hydrate turns the salt to dark metal; wash, antibiotic and pad. Alternatives: India ink, keratopigmentation, a coloured cosmetic contact lens or prosthetic shell. Avoid in a thin or ectatic scar or a painful glaucomatous eye.

Q: How do you manage an anterior staphyloma?
A: Control IOP. If vision is possible, PK (often large) with glaucoma surgery; if the eye is blind and painful or disfiguring, staphylectomy, evisceration or enucleation with a prosthesis.

Q: How would you manage band keratopathy over a leucomatous cornea (S.No 40)?
A: Find the cause (chronic uveitis, phthisis, silicone oil, long-standing glaucoma or oedema). A blind, comfortable eye needs nothing more than a cosmetic shell. For irritation or before a graft: remove the epithelium and chelate the calcium with disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) about 3%, then a bandage contact lens. Details on card EC6.

Q: When is a keratoprosthesis considered?
A: For bilateral corneal blindness where a donor graft is bound to fail (multiple failures, heavy vascularisation, chemical injury, Stevens–Johnson syndrome): Boston type I, or osteo-odonto-keratoprosthesis for very dry eyes, usually in the better-potential eye only.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: What are the complications of an adherent leucoma?
A: Secondary glaucoma, ectatic scar and anterior staphyloma, corneal fistula, recurrent ulceration in a degenerated scar (atheromatous ulcer), endophthalmitis through a thin scar, cataract and phthisis bulbi.

Q: What is the prognosis of keratoplasty for corneal opacity?
A: By Brightbill's grading: excellent for avascular central and traumatic scars (grade I), good for small vascularised scars (grade II), fair for heavily vascularised corneas (grade III), guarded for staphyloma and paediatric grafts (grade IV). Adherent leucoma with PAS and glaucoma does worse.

Q: What is special about corneal opacity in children?
A: An opacity in the visual axis in the first years causes deprivation amblyopia and nystagmus, so treat early: optical iridectomy or PK (by 2–12 months in Peters' anomaly), then refraction and patching of the better eye. Paediatric grafts reject more, sutures loosen early (all out by about 6 months) and follow-up needs examinations under anaesthesia.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: Which eye would you operate first at S.No 19?
A: The eye with better visual potential and graft prognosis, usually the non-adherent leucoma with normal IOP and accurate projection (here the right). The adherent leucoma with raised IOP needs glaucoma control first and has a guarded prognosis.

Q: What is Peters' anomaly?
A: A congenital central leucoma from a defect of posterior stroma, Descemet's membrane and endothelium, with a clear periphery. Type I: iris strands from the collarette to the scar; type II: lens adhesion or cataract, usually bilateral. Glaucoma in 50–70%. Genes include PAX6, PITX2 and FOXC1; Peters' plus syndrome adds short stature, cleft lip and developmental delay.

Q: What newer options exist for corneal scars?
A: DALK by big-bubble (no endothelial rejection), femtosecond laser-assisted keratoplasty, PTK for superficial scars, AS-OCT-guided planning, keratopigmentation for cosmesis, keratoprosthesis; and regression of vessels before grafting by fine-needle diathermy or bevacizumab (subconjunctival or topical).

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Describing only "a white opacity" without size, depth, vessels, iris adherence and IOP.
- Missing iris incarceration: look for a peaked pupil and brown pigment on the back of the scar.
- Forgetting IOP in adherent leucoma, and using Goldmann on a scar.
- Proposing keratoplasty without checking projection of rays and B-scan.
- Calling an ectatic iris-lined scar a leucoma: it is an anterior staphyloma.
- In a child, forgetting amblyopia and the need for early surgery.
- At S.No 40, offering a graft to a phthisical, blind eye: treat for comfort and cosmesis.
:::
