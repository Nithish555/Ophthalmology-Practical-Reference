## History — the examiner's reasoning questions
Q: In a 60-year-old with gradual loss of vision in both eyes, what is the differential before you examine? (V2)
A: Senile cataract, chronic simple glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy or maculopathy, age-related macular degeneration.
Q: Intermittent one-sided eye pain and headache that began at 58 — causes? (V2)
A: Uncorrected hypermetropia, systemic hypertension, intermittent angle closure, lens-induced glaucoma, absolute glaucoma, corneal disease. Migraine is unlikely at this age.
Q: Why ask about redness, diplopia, nausea and vomiting, trauma, and asthma in a glaucoma suspect? (V2)
A:
- Redness → uveitis, acute congestive attack.
- Diplopia → intracranial space-occupying lesion.
- Nausea and vomiting → migraine or an acute attack.
- Trauma → traumatic cataract, angle recession, subluxated lens.
- Asthma → steroid use; beta-blockers contraindicated.
Q: How should a symptom like haloes be written in the history? (V2)
A: In the patient's words — "coloured rings around lights" — not in the language of signs.
Q: A patient says he had LASIK 25 years ago and still needs −12 D. How do you check? (V7)
A: Look for the flap edge (press gently with a blunt instrument); keratometry (flat after ablation); topography; ask whether he was ever spectacle-free.

## Glaucoma
Q: How do you assess a shallow anterior chamber clinically? (V2)
A: Torch test (iris shadow on the nasal side) and the Van Herick slit-lamp grading:
- grade 4: chamber ≥ 1 corneal thickness;
- grade 3: ¼–½;
- grade 2: ¼;
- grade 1: < ¼;
- grade 0: iris touching the cornea.

Grades 2, 1 and 0 need gonioscopy.
Q: PACG with a significant cataract — what plan? (V2)
A: Control the IOP medically first; judge the lens contribution (lens thickness). If controlled → cataract surgery alone and watch the IOP; sequential glaucoma surgery if needed. Combined surgery when uncontrolled with extensive synechial closure.
Q: Target IOP for moderate glaucomatous damage? (V2)
A: Low to mid teens.
Q: How do you tell synechial from appositional angle closure? (V3)
A: Indentation (dynamic) gonioscopy — an appositional angle opens, a synechial one does not.
Q: Which antiglaucoma drugs do you avoid in neovascular glaucoma, and why? (V3)
A: Prostaglandin analogues (macular oedema, inflammation) and miotics (inflammation, synechiae). Use acetazolamide, timolol, brimonidine, dorzolamide, plus topical steroid and atropine.
Q: How are secondary glaucomas classified? (V3)
A: Secondary open-angle and secondary angle-closure. NVG with 360° PAS is secondary angle-closure glaucoma.
Q: What is the newest class of glaucoma drug, and how does it work? (V10)
A: Rho kinase inhibitors (ripasudil, netarsudil). They increase outflow through the trabecular meshwork; netarsudil also lowers episcleral venous pressure.

## Retina and vascular
Q: Risk factors for CRVO, in categories? (V3)
A:
- Systemic vascular: age, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia.
- Blood: hyperviscosity (polycythaemia, myeloma), thrombophilia (antiphospholipid syndrome, protein C or S deficiency, hyperhomocysteinaemia).
- Inflammatory: sarcoidosis, tuberculosis.
- Local: glaucoma.
Q: Signs of an ischaemic CRVO? (V3)
A: Vision worse than 6/60, RAPD, NVI or NVA, many cotton-wool spots (and non-perfusion on FFA).
Q: Why NVI rather than NVD in a CRVO? (V3)
A: VEGF from widely ischaemic retina that cannot itself support new vessels acts on the anterior segment; lesser ischaemia gives NVD or NVE.
Q: Collaterals versus NVD? (V3)
A: Collaterals are looped, do not branch and do not leak on FFA.
Q: Why is superotemporal BRVO the commonest? (V3)
A: Most arteriovenous crossings are in that quadrant. (added) Temporal occlusions affect the macula and are noticed; nasal ones often go undetected.
Q: Normal central macular thickness on OCT, and the oedema threshold? (V3)
A: About 250 µm; more than 270–280 µm suggests oedema — (added) the exact values depend on the OCT machine.
Q: Dose of intravitreal ranibizumab? (V3)
A: (added) 0.5 mg in 0.05 mL. Dexamethasone implant 0.7 mg; triamcinolone 4 mg/0.1 mL.
Q: When do you do PRP in a CRVO? (V3)
A: (added) Promptly when 2 clock hours of NVI or any NVA appear (Central Vein Occlusion Study), not as prophylaxis.
Q: In what order do you describe a fundus? (V3)
A: Disc → macula → vessels → background.

## Retinal detachment and myopia
Q: Classify retinal detachment. (V8)
A: Rhegmatogenous, tractional, exudative, combined.
Q: Slit-lamp clues to a rhegmatogenous RD? (V8)
A: Shafer's sign (pigment in the anterior vitreous — only in unoperated eyes) and a lower IOP than the other eye (about 4–5 mm Hg).
Q: Lincoff's rules — the gist? (V8)
A: The shape of the detachment points to the break. An inferior RD with equal sides → break near 6 o'clock; one side higher → break on that side; superior RD on one side → break near its highest border; crossing 12 o'clock → break near 12.
Q: Degenerations that need prophylaxis versus those that do not? (V8)
A: Treat lattice with holes (barrage laser) in at-risk eyes. Paving-stone (cobblestone), microcystoid and honeycomb degeneration and peripheral drusen need no treatment.
Q: Indications for an encircling band? (V8)
A: Breaks in several quadrants, extensive detachment or degeneration, (added) proliferative vitreoretinopathy.
Q: Definition and signs of pathological myopia? (V7, V8)
A: More than −6 D or axial length more than 26 mm, with degenerative changes: peripapillary atrophy, tilted disc, lacquer cracks, Foster–Fuchs spot, choroidal neovascularisation, posterior staphyloma, chorioretinal atrophy.
Q: Refractive options for high myopia; when is refractive lens exchange done? (V8)
A: Phakic IOL (ICL), refractive lens exchange, bioptics. RLE usually after about 45, because of the risk of RD in younger myopes.
Q: Father of modern retinal surgery? (V8)
A: Jules Gonin — find the break and seal it.

## Orbit and neuro-ophthalmology
Q: Commonest cause of unilateral and bilateral proptosis in adults? (V4)
A: Thyroid eye disease, for both.
Q: Name the TED signs. (V4)
A: Dalrymple (lid retraction), von Graefe (lid lag on downgaze), Stellwag (infrequent blinking), Kocher (staring look), Möbius (weak convergence), Ballet (muscle restriction), Braley (IOP rises on upgaze). (added) The commonest and earliest sign is lid retraction.
Q: CT in TED? If the tendon is involved? (V4)
A: Fusiform enlargement of the muscle belly sparing the tendon. Tendon involved → orbital myositis.
Q: Which exophthalmometer after a lateral orbitotomy? (V4)
A: One resting on the superior and inferior rims — (added) Naugle. Also know Hertel and Luedde.
Q: How do you test optic nerve function in proptosis? (V4)
A: Vision, RAPD, colour vision **with red desaturation**, fields.
Q: Pseudoproptosis — causes? Pseudo-Graefe sign? (V4, V7)
A: Axial myopia (commonest), lid retraction, enophthalmos of the other eye, shallow orbit. Pseudo-Graefe sign = aberrant regeneration of the third nerve.
Q: Localise a third nerve lesion from the orbit to the nucleus. (V5)
A:
- Orbit — one division.
- Fissure — III, IV, V1, VI; cavernous sinus — also V2.
- Subarachnoid space — PCOM aneurysm (pupil, pain), meningitis.
- Fascicle — Nothnagel, Claude, Benedikt, Weber.
- Nucleus — bilateral ptosis and opposite superior rectus weakness.
Q: Why is the pupil spared in a microvascular third nerve palsy? (V5)
A: Pupillary fibres are superficial and pial-fed. Ischaemia affects the core; compression affects the surface.
Q: When is a third nerve palsy an emergency? (V5)
A: Pupil involvement, sudden severe headache, meningeal signs, other neurological deficits ("third nerve plus") → neurosurgeon at once.
Q: Grade ptosis. (V5)
A: (added) Mild ≤ 2 mm, moderate 3 mm, severe ≥ 4 mm of droop. In a third nerve palsy it is neurogenic ptosis.

## Cornea
Q: Bacterial versus fungal keratitis? (V6)
A: **Bacterial:** fast, sharp margins, dense infiltrate, sterile mobile hypopyon. **Fungal:** slow, vegetable trauma, dry raised ulcer, feathery margins, satellite lesions, immobile convex-topped hypopyon.
Q: Where do you take a corneal scraping from? (V6)
A: The leading edge and base, after removing debris; avoid thin areas.
Q: Mooren's ulcer — diagnostic features, and Watson's classification? (V6)
A: Diagnosis: crescentic peripheral ulcer with an undermined edge, no scleritis, no detectable systemic disease. (added) Watson's types: 1 unilateral elderly painful; 2 bilateral aggressive young; 3 bilateral indolent elderly.
Q: Goal of treating infectious keratitis? (V6)
A: Eliminate the infection and prevent complications.
Q: When is glue suitable for a corneal perforation? (V6)
A: Small perforations (under 2 mm) without uveal prolapse. Otherwise use a therapeutic keratoplasty.
Q: How do you follow impending corneal perforation? (V6)
A: Serial anterior segment OCT.
Q: Why syringe in infectious keratitis even when ROPLAS is negative? (V6)
A: A hidden lacrimal block keeps reinfecting the ulcer; treating it improves the prognosis.

## Uveitis and lens
Q: How do you describe the congestion of anterior uveitis? (V7)
A: Circumcorneal (ciliary) congestion.
Q: Cataract at 49 with high myopia and uveitis — what type? (V7)
A: Complicated cataract (not senile; 45 is the presenile cut-off).
Q: Fresh versus old keratic precipitates? (V7)
A: (added) Fresh: white, round, fluffy. Old: shrunken, pigmented, crenated. Mutton-fat = granulomatous.
