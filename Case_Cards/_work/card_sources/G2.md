## At a glance
- **Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG)** after **trabeculectomy**: the bleb has **failed** and the intraocular pressure (IOP) is above target. For primary open-angle glaucoma itself, see card G1.
- A checklist long case: the bleb, the surgical iridectomy and the cupped disc last for years.
- Tested: the **bleb description in the department's order**, Seidel's test, iridectomy, ostium, antimetabolites, the shallow chamber.

**Keywords the examiner listens for:** filtering bleb · failed bleb · microcysts · subconjunctival fibrosis · encapsulated bleb (Tenon's cyst) · Seidel's test · peripheral iridectomy · internal ostium · mitomycin C · 5-fluorouracil · digital pressure · laser suture lysis · shallow anterior chamber · malignant glaucoma · bleb-related endophthalmitis

## History — positive and negative, with reasons
**Chief complaint:** "Mr X, a 56-year-old man, came with defective vision in both eyes. Right eye (RE) for 1 year, left eye (LE) for 6 months. The right eye was operated for raised pressure 6 months ago."

**History of present illness (house wording):** "Patient was apparently normal __ years before. Then he had defective vision in both eyes, gradual in onset and progressive. He was found to have raised pressure and started on eye drops (__). The __ eye underwent trabeculectomy (with / without __) __ months ago at __. He is now on __."

### Step 1 — Visual acuity
**Do:** Distance acuity with and without pinhole, each eye; then near vision.

**Record:** "RE 6/60, pinhole 6/12; LE 6/24, pinhole 6/9."

Q: Why can vision fall after trabeculectomy?
A: **Cataract** is a late complication, also caused by a shallow chamber after surgery. Early on, hypotony maculopathy and loss of central vision can occur.

Q: What is snuff-out?
A: **Snuff-out** is sudden loss of vision after trabeculectomy in advanced glaucoma.

### Step 2 — Pupils
**Do:** Before any drop: size, shape and the swinging-flashlight test.

**Record:** "RE 4 mm, round, sluggish, grade 3 RAPD; LE normal."

Q: Why check the pupils before any drop?
A: A **relative afferent pupillary defect** shows more optic-nerve damage in one eye. Dilating drops would hide it.

Q: What does a grade 3 RAPD tell you here?
A: A **grade 3 RAPD** is seen in advanced glaucoma. It fits the 0.9 cup, so this eye has little reserve.

### Step 3 — Bleb examination in the department's order
**Do:** Ask the patient to look down; lift the upper lid gently. Use diffuse light, then high magnification. Describe in order:
1. **Height** (flat, medium) and **clarity** (translucent or opaque).
2. **Horizontal extent** (clock hours) and **location** over the limbus.
3. **Vascularity** (avascular, or highly vascular with corkscrew vessels) and **microcysts**.
4. **Subconjunctival fibrosis** and **sutures**.

**Record:** "Flat, opaque bleb from 11 to 2 o'clock, superiorly above the limbus. Highly vascular with corkscrew vessels, no microcysts, subconjunctival fibrosis present, no sutures — suggestive of a failed bleb."

| Feature | Good functioning bleb | Failing or failed bleb |
|---|---|---|
| Height | Medium: low and diffuse, or circumscribed and elevated | **Flat**; reduced height |
| Clarity and wall | **Translucent**, thin | **Opaque**; thicker wall |
| Vascularity | **Avascular** | **Highly vascular, corkscrew** vessels |
| Microcysts | **Numerous** | **Absent** |
| Subconjunctival fibrosis | None | **Present** |

Q: What are the features of a good filtering bleb?
A: A **good filtering bleb** is avascular, with numerous epithelial **microcysts**. It is low and diffuse, or circumscribed and elevated. Such blebs control the IOP.

Q: What are the signs of a failing bleb?
A: A **failing bleb** shows reduced height, a thicker wall, vascularisation and loss of microcysts. The IOP rises.

Q: What do the microcysts mean?
A: **Microcysts** come from aqueous flowing across the conjunctiva. They mean the bleb is filtering.

Q: Why do you note the scleral flap sutures?
A: **Visible flap sutures** can be cut by **laser suture lysis** when the target pressure is not reached. This is done from a few days to 6 months after surgery. Gentle pressure on the conjunctiva shows them better. A tenonectomy at surgery also makes them easier to see.

### Step 4 — Seidel's test
**Do:** Put fluorescein over the bleb: 0.25% shows many leaks, 2% the subtle ones. With cobalt blue light, look for aqueous leaking through the dye.

**Record:** "Seidel's test negative."

Q: Why do Seidel's test on every bleb?
A: A **leak** and **overfiltration** both give a low pressure. **Seidel's test** is positive only in the leak.

Q: How is a late leaking bleb managed?
A: A **late leak** is treated conservatively when minimal, with a deep chamber and a pale, elevated bleb. I use aqueous suppressants, gentamicin drops and patching, and a shell if the chamber is flat. Otherwise I repair it.

### Step 5 — Anterior chamber depth
**Do:** Van Herick: a thin slit beam 1 mm inside the temporal limbus at 60°. Compare the peripheral chamber with corneal thickness. Then judge central depth, cells and hyphaema.

**Record:** "VH grade IV (ND); AC quiet."

Q: What is the commonest problem after trabeculectomy?
A: A **shallow anterior chamber**. With low IOP: leak, overfiltration or choroidal detachment. With high IOP: malignant glaucoma, pupillary block or delayed suprachoroidal haemorrhage.

Q: Why does a shallow chamber matter?
A: A **shallow chamber** causes peripheral anterior synechiae, corneal endothelial damage and cataract.

### Step 6 — Peripheral iridectomy and its patency
**Do:** On the slit lamp describe **margin** → **site** → **surrounding iris**. Then **retro-illumination**: a light reflex seen through the defect means patency.

**Record:** "Sharp, well-defined margin at 12 o'clock near the limbus; iris normal; retro-illumination positive — patent surgical iridectomy."

Q: Why is a peripheral iridectomy done with trabeculectomy?
A: The **peripheral iridectomy** stops the iris blocking the internal ostium and prevents **pupillary block**. It is basal and wider than the ostium.

Q: How do you tell a surgical iridectomy from a laser iridotomy?
A: A **surgical iridectomy** has clean-cut triangular margins near the incision and cannot close. A laser iridotomy has ragged margins at any site, may close, and may show pigment dispersion.

### Step 7 — Tonometry
**Do:** Goldmann applanation after the slit-lamp examination, before gonioscopy and dilatation; note the time.

**Record:** "Tn by GAT RE 26 mm Hg, LE 24 mm Hg at 10 am."

Q: At what pressure would you act soon after surgery?
A: **Above 12 mm Hg**, or whenever the target is missed, I apply **digital pressure**. I do not wait for the bleb to fail.

Q: What does a soft eye with a flat bleb mean?
A: A **wound leak**: soft eye, poor bleb, positive Seidel's test. A very low pressure with a good bleb and negative Seidel's test means overfiltration.

### Step 8 — Gonioscopy and the internal ostium
**Do:** After tonometry, before dilatation (technique: card G9). Note the deepest structure in each quadrant; superiorly, the **internal ostium** and iridectomy.

**Record:** "Scleral spur in all quadrants; patent internal ostium and PI superiorly."

Q: Why do gonioscopy in an operated eye?
A: The **internal ostium** can be blocked by clot, iris, ciliary body, lens or vitreous. Gonioscopy shows whether it is open.

Q: How do you treat a blocked ostium?
A: **Argon laser** at low energy around the obstruction, or internal bleb revision. For fibrin or clot, intracameral tissue plasminogen activator **6–12.5 µg**.

### Step 9 — Disc and macula
**Do:** After dilatation, with a +90 D lens: disc, rim, vessels, nerve fibre layer, macula, then the periphery.

**Record:** "RE CDR 0.9, diffuse rim loss, bayoneting, laminar dot sign. LE CDR 0.75, superior notch."

Q: What do you look for in the fundus after trabeculectomy?
A: The **optic disc** for progression, the **macula** for hypotony maculopathy, and the periphery for choroidal detachment or suprachoroidal haemorrhage.

Q: How is hypotony maculopathy prevented?
A: **Hypotony maculopathy** is prevented by a partial-thickness flap, a tighter flap after MMC, and releasable sutures or laser suture lysis. In eyes at risk, 5-FU replaces MMC.

### Step 10 — Fields
**Do:** Confrontation at the bedside; plan Humphrey 24-2 perimetry.

**Record:** "Fields (confrontation test): ___."

Q: How often will you repeat the fields?
A: **Perimetry** is repeated every **6 months** in mild to moderate disease and every **3 months** in advanced disease. Progression despite surgery means I must lower the pressure further.

Q: How do you stage the damage in this eye?
A: **Severe** damage: marked cupping, a field defect within the central 5° and a mean deviation worse than −12 dB. **End-stage**: gross cupping with a small residual field. The 0.9 cup and grade 3 RAPD point to advanced damage; the field confirms the stage (target formula: card G1).

## How I will manage
### Aim
Bring the operated eye to its target IOP, protect the remaining field and prevent infection. The left eye is managed as on card G1.

### The ladder
1. **Find what has failed.** I examine the bleb, do Seidel's test and look at the ostium by gonioscopy. A blocked ostium gets argon laser or internal revision; fibrin or clot, tissue plasminogen activator.
2. **Early failing bleb** (trigger: IOP above 12 mm Hg or target). I increase steroids to hourly and apply **digital pressure**. Then **laser suture lysis**, or I release a releasable suture.
3. **Encapsulated bleb.** It usually settles in **2–4 months**; I control the IOP with drops meanwhile.
4. **Failed bleb** (trigger: IOP above target with a flat vascular bleb). I restart antiglaucoma drops, avoiding beta-blockers because he is asthmatic.
5. **Above target on maximal drops, or progressing.** I advise repeat trabeculectomy with **mitomycin C** at a fresh superotemporal or superonasal site. With scarred conjunctiva, I choose a **glaucoma drainage device** (card G3).
6. **Refractory after failed filtration.** I use transscleral diode cyclophotocoagulation.
7. **Cataract.** With IOP controlled, cataract surgery through a temporal clear cornea spares the conjunctiva. With poor control or a marginal filter, I combine it with trabeculectomy.

## What you must know
### Definition
**Trabeculectomy** is a partial-thickness filtering operation. It has "a partial thickness flap of sclera overlying the opening between the anterior chamber and the subconjunctival space". In plain words: a guarded hole at the limbus drains aqueous under the conjunctiva into a bleb. It works mainly by **filtration**: aqueous leaves by transconjunctival filtration or through lymphatics and vessels.
- **Failing bleb**: reduced height, thicker wall, vascularisation, loss of microcysts, rising IOP.
- **Failed bleb**: no longer filters (types 3 and 4), so the IOP is elevated.
- **Encapsulated bleb (Tenon's cyst)**: a localised, highly elevated, dome-shaped cavity of hypertrophied Tenon's capsule.
- **Malignant (ciliary block) glaucoma**: aqueous is blocked at the ciliary body and forced back into the vitreous.
- **Endophthalmitis**: microbial inflammation of the vitreous and inner eye, with loss of vision.

### Classification
**Filtering operations**: full thickness, with no scleral flap (sclerectomy, trephination, Scheie's thermal sclerostomy). Partial thickness: **trabeculectomy**. Non-penetrating: viscocanalostomy, canaloplasty, deep sclerectomy.

### Trabeculectomy and wound-healing modulation
**Indications in POAG**: as in the viva question below. Also drug side effects and difficult follow-up.

**Relative contraindications**: likely to respond to less invasive treatment; previously failed trabeculectomy; severely scarred conjunctiva. Also neovascular glaucoma with active new vessels, and uveitic glaucoma.

**Key steps**
1. Bridle suture; conjunctival flap based at the limbus or the fornix.
2. Scleral flap based at the limbus, **3 mm radially × 4 mm circumferentially**, 2/3 thickness.
3. MMC now, if used.
4. Dissect the flap into clear cornea. Make a **paracentesis**: an entry for fluid into the AC at any stage of the operation.
5. Enter the AC behind the hinge; Kelly Descemet's punch: **internal ostium 1.5 × 2 mm**.
6. **Peripheral iridectomy**, basal and wider than the ostium.
7. Flap: interrupted **10-0 nylon**; reform the AC with balanced salt solution (BSS) through the paracentesis.
8. Conjunctiva: **8-0 vicryl**, watertight.
9. **Atropine 1%**; subconjunctival betamethasone and gentamicin.

**Antimetabolites** go in the sub-Tenon's space, because subconjunctival fibrosis is the commonest cause of failure.

### Pathogenesis
- Fibroblasts lay down **subconjunctival fibrosis**, the commonest cause of bleb failure.
- Blood under the conjunctival flap scars the bleb, so haemostasis matters.
- Aqueous damps fibroblasts. Older eyes do better: atrophic Tenon's, less fibroblast activity.

### Complications
**During surgery**: conjunctival buttonhole, flap amputation, haemorrhage, lens damage, vitreous loss, choroidal effusion, malignant glaucoma.

**Late**: bleb infection and endophthalmitis, cataract, leaking bleb, Tenon's cyst, failure, malignant glaucoma, upper lid retraction, sympathetic ophthalmia.
- **Bleb infection**: frequent topical antibiotics, empirically; steroids 12–24 hours later.
- **Bleb-related endophthalmitis**: your books give no bleb-specific regimen. I treat it by the general protocol for postoperative endophthalmitis (viva below).

### Prognosis
- Better: older patients (atrophic Tenon's capsule).
- Poorer: age under 40, previous failed filter, aphakia or pseudophakia, neovascular glaucoma, active uveitis, previous eye surgery.
- Plain trabeculectomy lowers IOP by about **41%**.

### Recent advances
*(extra)* **Photodynamic therapy** and **non-penetrating surgery**: see the last two viva questions.

## Viva questions
### Why did you ask…?
Q: Why did you ask whether an antimetabolite was used?
A: **Mitomycin C** causes more complications than **5-fluorouracil**: early or late hypotony, bleb leak and bleb infection.

Q: Why did you ask how long he used drops before surgery?
A: **Long-term drops** increase conjunctival fibroblasts and cut goblet cells, so the bleb fails more. Over 3 years, I would use mitomycin C.

Q: Why did you ask about asthma?
A: **Beta-blockers** such as timolol are contraindicated in asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. When drops restart, I choose another class, such as dorzolamide.

Q: Why did you ask about the other eye?
A: An **encapsulated bleb** (Tenon's cyst) in the fellow eye raises the risk here. If 5-FU surgery failed there, I would use MMC here.

### Diagnosis and differentials
Q: Is "trabeculectomy" a misnomer?
A: **Yes, a misnomer**: the block removed need not contain trabecular meshwork. It is more a peripheral posterior keratectomy.

Q: What are the causes of failure of filtration?
A: **Subconjunctival fibrosis** is the commonest; it is extraocular, like racial and genetic factors. Intraocular causes are a blocked fistula, bleb failure and poor technique.

Q: What are the risk factors for a Tenon's cyst?
A: **Tenon's cyst**: young age, previous conjunctival surgery, secondary glaucoma, laser trabeculoplasty, sympathomimetic drops, and one in the fellow eye. Argon laser trabeculoplasty raises the risk up to 3 times.

### Investigations
Q: What does ultrasound biomicroscopy add after trabeculectomy?
A: **Ultrasound biomicroscopy** shows PI patency and whether the trabeculectomy works. In malignant glaucoma, the anterior structures press against the cornea.

Q: How does B-scan show a choroidal detachment?
A: **Choroidal detachment** is a smooth, dome-shaped, highly reflective elevation that spares the optic nerve. A clear space is serous; echoes mean blood. Large ones meet as "kissing choroids".

### Management — drugs
Q: How do mitomycin C and 5-fluorouracil work?
A: **Mitomycin C** is an alkylating agent acting in all cell-cycle phases, and far more potent. The pyrimidine analogue **5-fluorouracil** blocks DNA synthesis in the S phase. Both stop fibroblast proliferation.

Q: Which eyes need an antimetabolite?
A: **High risk**: neovascular glaucoma, failed trabeculectomy, inflammatory or post-traumatic glaucoma. **Intermediate**: drops for 3 years, previous conjunctival or cataract surgery. **Low**: black patients, age under 40.

Q: How do you prevent antimetabolite toxicity?
A: **Thorough irrigation** after the set time, watertight closure, and careful dissection to avoid a buttonhole.

Q: Why give atropine after trabeculectomy?
A: **Atropine** tightens the lens–iris diaphragm and deepens the chamber. It also keeps the blood–aqueous barrier, relieves ciliary spasm and prevents posterior synechiae.

### Management — laser and surgery
Q: What are the indications for trabeculectomy in POAG?
A: **Failed maximal tolerated medical therapy** with progressive or anticipated damage. Also IOP above target on 3 drugs, baseline IOP over 40 mm Hg, one eye lost, poor compliance.

Q: What is the preoperative care?
A: **Preoperative care**: I lower a very high IOP and treat inflammation. I stop timolol 2 weeks and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors 1–2 days before, to prevent hypotony and let a bleb form. Aspirin stops 5 days before; pilocarpine 1% before surgery prevents iris prolapse.

Q: How will you improve the success of the repeat operation?
A: **Before surgery**, I treat surface infection and stop aqueous suppressants, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors and aspirin. **During surgery**, I constrict the pupil and avoid a buttonhole or flap disinsertion. I make a 1.5 × 2 mm ostium, a larger iridectomy and a tight closure. **Wound healing** is modulated with steroids and an antimetabolite, here mitomycin C.

Q: When would you choose a drainage device or cyclophotocoagulation instead?
A: A **glaucoma drainage device** suits failed surgery with conjunctiva too scarred to dissect. **Transscleral diode cyclophotocoagulation** suits refractory glaucoma after failed filtration. It also suits eyes where filtration is likely to fail: aphakic, pseudophakic or neovascular eyes.

Q: Which structures are removed in trabeculectomy?
A: **Structures removed**, from outside in: a scleral block, Schlemm's canal, trabecular meshwork, Schwalbe's line, peripheral cornea and iris. Removing Tenon's is optional.

Q: Why avoid the 12 o'clock position?
A: **Bleeding**: the anterior ciliary vessel enters the ciliary body 3–4 mm from the limbus. Scarring there also blocks a repeat operation.

Q: Fornix-based or limbus-based conjunctival flap?
A: **Success is equal**. Fornix-based: easier, better exposure, but early limbal leak. Limbus-based: safer early suture lysis, 5-FU and digital pressure, and lower late IOP.

Q: When do you suture the scleral flap tighter?
A: **Angle-closure glaucoma** or past malignant glaucoma, aphakic or highly myopic eyes, nanophthalmos and raised episcleral venous pressure.

Q: What are the advantages of releasable sutures and laser suture lysis?
A: **Releasable sutures** and suture lysis let the eye leave theatre firm, protected from hypotony. The pressure can then be titrated after surgery.

Q: What is the role of digital pressure?
A: **Digital pressure** pushes aqueous through the sclerostomy and expands the bleb. It stops an obstruction to filtration becoming permanent.

Q: When do you combine cataract surgery with trabeculectomy?
A: **Combined surgery** suits a visually significant cataract with poor IOP control, drug intolerance, advanced glaucoma or a marginal filter. Two stages lower the IOP more, with fewer complications.

Q: What will you consider before cataract surgery in this eye?
A: **Prior surgery** has left a bleb or scarring, so a temporal clear-cornea approach spares the conjunctiva. I expect a miotic pupil, posterior synechiae and bleeding from a congested eye. Other risks are an early pressure rise and suprachoroidal haemorrhage; diabetes and hypertension need control. After cataract surgery alone, later filtering surgery is prone to fail.

### Complications
Q: How do you treat malignant glaucoma?
A: **Malignant glaucoma**: strong mydriatics, then intravenous mannitol, then Nd:YAG disruption of the anterior hyaloid face through the iridectomy. If that fails, pars plana vitrectomy.

Q: What is the earliest sign of bleb infection, and how is it treated?
A: **Mild conjunctival hyperaemia** around the bleb. I start frequent topical antibiotics empirically, and steroids after 12–24 hours.

Q: Which organisms cause bleb-related endophthalmitis?
A: **Bleb-related endophthalmitis**: Streptococcus species (most common), Haemophilus influenzae and Staphylococcus.

Q: How do you treat bleb-related endophthalmitis?
A: **Bleb-related endophthalmitis** is treated by the general protocol for postoperative endophthalmitis; your books give no separate regimen for blebs. After a B-scan, I take an aqueous or vitreous sample. I inject intravitreal **vancomycin 1 mg/0.1 ml** with **ceftazidime 2 mg/0.1 ml**. I may repeat the injection after 48 hours. I advise vitrectomy if there is no improvement, or at once if vision is only perception of light.

Q: What is the commonest side effect of mitomycin C?
A: A **cystic bleb with overfiltration**. Others: late leak, corneal decompensation, scleral necrosis, hypotony, endophthalmitis and cataract.

### Recent advances
Q: What is new in wound-healing modulation? *(extra)*
A: **Photodynamic therapy**: diffuse blue light with a photosensitising agent kills fibroblasts and limits the treated area.

Q: What is non-penetrating filtration surgery? *(extra)*
A: **Deep sclerectomy** or **viscocanalostomy**. The AC is not entered, so overfiltration and hypotony are less. It suits POAG, not angle closure.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Calling a high dome "good": an **encapsulated bleb** does not filter.
- "No leak" without **Seidel's test**: a leak and overfiltration both give a low pressure.
- Not retro-illuminating the **iridectomy**: an incomplete one causes pupillary block.
- Gonioscopy without naming the **internal ostium**.
- A **beta-blocker** for this asthmatic.
- Steroids first in a bleb infection: antibiotics come first.
- "Status post trabeculectomy" without the **bleb status, IOP and lens**.
:::

## Say-it script
### Opening (about 1 minute)
:::say
Mr X is a fifty-six-year-old man. He came with defective vision in the right eye for one year and in the left eye for six months.

The vision fell gradually in both eyes. A doctor found raised pressure and started drops twice daily in both eyes. He did not use them regularly. The right eye was operated six months ago to lower the pressure. He has no operation notes, so I do not know whether an antimetabolite was used. He still uses drops in both eyes.

Since surgery he has had no redness, watering or pain in the operated eye. He has had no sudden loss of vision and no change in the upper lid.

He is diabetic for fifteen years on tablets. He is asthmatic and uses an inhaler when needed. He has no sulpha allergy and takes no aspirin or steroids. He has had no other eye surgery. There is no family history of glaucoma.
:::

### Closing (2 minutes)
:::say
To summarise, Mr X, a fifty-six-year-old asthmatic diabetic man, had trabeculectomy in the right eye six months ago.

Vision is 6/60 on the right and 6/24 on the left, improving to 6/12 and 6/9 with pinhole. The right eye has a flat, opaque bleb from eleven to two o'clock superiorly. It is highly vascular, without microcysts, with subconjunctival fibrosis. Seidel's test is negative. A surgical iridectomy at twelve o'clock is patent. The chambers are of normal depth. There is a grade three relative afferent pupillary defect on the right. Both lenses show grade two nuclear sclerosis. Goldmann applanation pressure is twenty-six millimetres of mercury on the right and twenty-four on the left. Gonioscopy shows open angles and a patent internal ostium on the right. The cup–disc ratio is 0.9 on the right and 0.75 on the left.

My diagnosis is right eye primary open-angle glaucoma status post trabeculectomy with failed bleb and grade two nuclear sclerosis. Left eye: primary open-angle glaucoma and grade two nuclear sclerosis.

I will stage the damage with Humphrey fields and optical coherence tomography. I will restart drops, avoiding beta-blockers because of his asthma. If the pressure stays above target, I will advise repeat trabeculectomy with mitomycin C at a fresh site. A glaucoma drainage device is the alternative. He must come at once if the eye becomes red or painful.
:::

## Quick recall
:::recall
- **Good bleb**: avascular, numerous microcysts. **Failing**: lower, thicker, vascular, microcysts lost, IOP up.
- **Types 3 and 4** do not filter; a Tenon's cyst settles in **2–4 months**.
- Commonest cause of failure: **subconjunctival fibrosis**. Commonest problem after surgery: **shallow AC**.
- **Leak**: soft eye, Seidel positive. **Overfiltration**: good bleb. **Malignant glaucoma**: no bleb, IOP high or normal.
- Fluorescein **0.25%** for many leaks, **2%** for subtle ones.
- **MMC 0.2–0.5 mg/ml for 1–5 min**; **5-FU 50 mg/ml for 5 min**, injection **5 mg**, 21 injections.
- Flap **3 × 4 mm**; ostium **1.5 × 2 mm**; PI wider than the ostium.
- Digital pressure above **12 mm Hg**; laser suture lysis up to **6 months**.
- First sign of bleb infection: **hyperaemia around the bleb**; steroids only after **12–24 hours** of antibiotics.
- Bleb-related endophthalmitis: **Streptococcus** most common.

**Mnemonic:** none in your mnemonics file for this case.
:::
