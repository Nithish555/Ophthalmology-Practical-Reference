## At a glance
- **Pseudophakic bullous keratopathy (PBK)**: chronic corneal oedema with epithelial bullae after cataract surgery with an intraocular lens (IOL), because the endothelium can no longer pump the cornea dry. After aphakia it is called aphakic bullous keratopathy.
- A leading indication for keratoplasty; today treated mainly by **endothelial keratoplasty (EK)**.
- Why kept: stable signs; tests the endothelial pump, specular microscopy, the IOL, and the choice between Descemet's stripping endothelial keratoplasty (DSEK), Descemet's membrane endothelial keratoplasty (DMEK) and penetrating keratoplasty (PK).
- Always examine the other eye: guttae there mean **Fuchs' endothelial dystrophy** was the underlying cause.
- Diagnosis lines:
  - S.No 16: "Left eye pseudophakic bullous keratopathy with posterior chamber IOL in the bag (or anterior chamber IOL), diffuse stromal and epithelial oedema with bullae and Descemet's folds, no stromal scarring, intraocular pressure (IOP) normal, projection accurate; right eye __, no guttae."
  - S.No 13: the eye is not stated on the list; record which eye or both.

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
"Mr X, a 68-year-old man, had cataract surgery in the left eye 2 years ago. Vision was good for a year and then became blurred, worst on waking, with bouts of pain and watering for 6 months. The left eye has diffuse stromal oedema with epithelial bullae, one ruptured bulla staining with fluorescein, and Descemet's folds, without stromal scarring or vascularisation. A posterior chamber IOL lies in the bag, the anterior chamber is quiet without vitreous, and IOP is 16 mm Hg by Tono-Pen. Projection of rays is accurate and the B-scan is normal; the right eye is phakic with a few central guttae."

**Diagnosis: Left eye pseudophakic bullous keratopathy with posterior chamber IOL in the bag, no stromal scarring, good visual potential; right eye early Fuchs' dystrophy. Plan: DSEK or DMEK.**

**With an ACIOL: "pseudophakic bullous keratopathy with anterior chamber IOL; plan EK (or PK) with IOL exchange."**

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: How does the endothelium keep the cornea clear?
A: Barrier function (tight junctions limit aqueous entry) and pump function (sodium–potassium ATPase and bicarbonate-linked transport move fluid out) balance the swelling pressure of stromal glycosaminoglycans. Stromal water stays near 78% and central thickness about 540 µm.

Q: What are the important endothelial cell counts?
A: About 5,000–6,000 cells/mm² at birth, 3,000 in young adults and 2,500 in older adults, with loss of about 0.6% a year. Intraocular surgery is risky below 1,000 cells/mm²; oedema usually appears below 400–500. Uncomplicated phacoemulsification loses roughly 5–10%. Cells do not divide in vivo; neighbours enlarge and spread.

Q: What causes PBK?
A: Pre-existing endothelial disease (Fuchs' guttae, low count, herpes); surgical trauma (hard nucleus, phaco energy near the cornea, shallow chamber, long or complicated surgery, DMD); IOL-related (ACIOL, especially closed-loop or iris-fixated, sulcus haptic touching the cornea); vitreous touch; inflammation, raised IOP and TASS after surgery.

Q: Why does an ACIOL damage the endothelium?
A: Intermittent lens–cornea touch, especially on eye rubbing; chronic low-grade inflammation and the uveitis–glaucoma–hyphaema (UGH) syndrome; iris tuck and PAS. Old closed-loop designs were the worst.

Q: Why is vision worst on waking?
A: With the lids closed there is no evaporation, so the tear film becomes less hypertonic and draws less water from the cornea; a weak endothelium cannot clear the overnight load. Vision improves through the day as the surface dries.

Q: Why are bullae painful, and why does the pain later settle?
A: Fluid lifts the epithelium into bullae; when they rupture, exposed nerve endings cause pain, watering and photophobia, and the defect may become infected. With chronic oedema, subepithelial fibrosis forms: pain settles but vision falls further.

### Examination and signs
Q: What are the signs of corneal decompensation?
A: Loss of lustre, epithelial microcysts and bullae, stromal thickening with ground-glass haze, Descemet's folds (striate keratopathy), raised pachymetry, and in long-standing cases subepithelial fibrosis and vascularisation; vision is worst in the morning.

Q: What do you check in the anterior chamber and IOL?
A: ACIOL: type, tilt, haptics in the angle, iris tuck, UGH syndrome, patent iridectomy. Posterior chamber IOL: in bag or sulcus, decentration, haptic touching the cornea. Vitreous strands to the wound (peaked pupil), cells and PAS.

Q: Why examine the fellow eye?
A: Oedema hides the endothelium of the affected eye. Guttae in the other eye point to Fuchs' dystrophy decompensated by surgery; vesicles or bands suggest posterior polymorphous dystrophy; a hammered-silver endothelium with iris atrophy suggests iridocorneal endothelial (ICE) syndrome.

Q: What is the differential diagnosis of oedema after cataract surgery?
A: Transient: striate keratopathy from surgical trauma (clears in a week), raised IOP, DMD, TASS, herpetic endotheliitis (keratic precipitates; responds to antiviral and steroid). Persistent: PBK, Brown–McLean syndrome, vitreous touch, epithelial downgrowth.

Q: What is DMD and how is it managed?
A: Descemet's membrane stripped from the stroma at the incision by blunt instruments or repeated entry; localised oedema with a membrane visible at the slit lamp or on AS-OCT. Small ones reattach; larger ones need air or long-acting gas injected into the anterior chamber (descemetopexy); if that fails, EK.

Q: How do you differentiate PBK from Fuchs' dystrophy and congenital hereditary endothelial dystrophy (CHED)?
A: Fuchs': bilateral, older women, autosomal dominant, central guttae with beaten-metal look, morning blur. CHED: bilateral oedema from birth or early childhood, thick blue-grey cornea of normal diameter, normal IOP, nystagmus in the recessive type (SLC4A11 gene). PBK: unilateral after surgery, fellow eye normal or with guttae.

### Investigations
Q: What does pachymetry tell you?
A: Normal central thickness is about 520–540 µm; rising thickness is the earliest objective sign of endothelial stress; over 640 µm suggests the cornea will not tolerate more intraocular surgery; a difference of over 10% between morning and evening shows early failure. Serial values monitor a graft. Ultrasonic, Scheimpflug or AS-OCT.

Q: What does specular microscopy show?
A: Reflected light images the endothelial mosaic: cell density (cells/mm²); coefficient of variation of cell area (polymegathism; healthy under about 30%, abnormal over 40%); percentage of hexagonal cells (pleomorphism; normal over 60%); guttae as dark spots. Not possible through dense oedema; then use confocal microscopy or examine the fellow eye.

Q: What is the role of confocal microscopy and AS-OCT?
A: Confocal microscopy images the endothelium and stroma through oedema where specular microscopy fails. AS-OCT shows corneal thickness, bullae, stromal scarring (EK or PK), DMD, IOL and angle, and after EK the lenticule position and attachment.

Q: What other tests are needed before surgery?
A: Visual potential (projection of rays, Maddox rod, two-point discrimination, entoptic phenomenon), B-scan for retinal detachment or vitreous disease, AS-OCT for stromal scarring, angle and IOL position, macular optical coherence tomography (OCT) for CMO when the view allows, and gonioscopy where possible.

### Management
Q: Outline the medical treatment.
A: Hypertonic saline (sodium chloride 5%) drops 4–8 times a day and 5% ointment at night; warm air from a hair dryer at arm's length in the morning; lower IOP with timolol or brimonidine, avoiding topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (they impair the pump); BCL for painful bullae; cycloplegic and lubricants; steroid if inflamed. These are temporary measures.

Q: Why does a bandage contact lens help?
A: It splints the bullae so they do not rupture, covers exposed nerve endings and lets the epithelium heal beneath it. Give prophylactic antibiotic and watch for keratitis.

Q: What do you do when visual potential is poor?
A: Aim for a comfortable eye: BCL; anterior stromal puncture (25–26 G needle; the scars anchor the epithelium); phototherapeutic keratectomy (PTK); bullectomy with amniotic membrane graft; Gundersen conjunctival flap; cross-linking (temporary); as last resorts retrobulbar alcohol or evisceration.

Q: What is the definitive treatment?
A: EK when the stroma is clear: DSEK or Descemet's stripping automated endothelial keratoplasty (DSAEK), with donor posterior stroma, Descemet's membrane and endothelium held by an air bubble, or DMEK with Descemet's membrane and endothelium only. PK when there is stromal scarring or heavy vascularisation. Treat the cause at the same sitting: anterior vitrectomy for vitreous touch, IOL exchange for an ACIOL.

Q: How do DSEK and DMEK compare?
A: DMEK gives better and faster vision, no hyperopic shift and the least rejection (1–3%), but the scroll is hard to unfold, rebubbling is commoner and it is difficult with an ACIOL, aphakia, vitrectomy or poor view. DSEK is easier in complex PBK eyes, with a hyperopic shift of about 1 D and rejection of 8–14%. Both beat PK: closed chamber, little astigmatism, stronger globe.

Q: What are the steps of DSEK?
A: Donor lenticule cut on an artificial anterior chamber (or by microkeratome) and trephined at 7.5–8 mm from the endothelial side. Host: side ports, trypan blue, 8–8.5 mm epithelial mark, score and strip Descemet's membrane, inferior peripheral iridectomy. Insert through a 4.5–5 mm incision (folded "taco", glide or injector), fill with air, and keep the patient supine for 24 hours.

Q: When is IOL exchange done and with what?
A: When an ACIOL or a malpositioned lens touches the endothelium or causes UGH syndrome: remove it and place a sulcus IOL on a capsular rim, a retropupillary iris-claw lens or a scleral-fixated lens, combined with EK or PK.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: What are the complications of endothelial keratoplasty?
A: Graft detachment (commonest; rebubble), pupillary block from air (inferior iridectomy, partial air release), primary graft failure, endothelial rejection, interface haze, epithelial ingrowth, raised IOP from steroid or PAS, late endothelial failure.

Q: What happens if PBK is left untreated?
A: Recurrent painful erosions, microbial keratitis, subepithelial fibrosis and vascularisation; once the stroma scars, PK rather than EK is needed and the prognosis falls.

Q: What is the prognosis?
A: Good with EK when the stroma is clear and the macula healthy (Brightbill grade II). Vision is limited by CMO, stromal scarring, glaucoma and macular disease; PBK grafts lose endothelium faster than grafts for Fuchs' dystrophy, and eyes with an ACIOL, a glaucoma tube or vitrectomy do worse.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: What are Rho-kinase inhibitors and what is their role?
A: Ripasudil 0.4% and netarsudil 0.02% drops promote endothelial proliferation, adhesion and migration and reduce apoptosis; tried in oedema after cataract surgery and in Fuchs' dystrophy (after Descemet stripping without a graft), and used with cultured endothelial cells.

Q: Can Descemet stripping without a graft be used in PBK?
A: No. Descemet stripping only (Descemetorhexis without EK) suits Fuchs' dystrophy with central guttae and a healthy periphery (over about 1,000 cells/mm²), from which cells migrate in, helped by a Rho-kinase inhibitor. In PBK the whole endothelium is depleted, so a graft is needed.

Q: What is cultured endothelial cell therapy?
A: Endothelial cells grown from one donor cornea are injected into the anterior chamber with a Rho-kinase inhibitor after the diseased endothelium is scraped off; the patient lies face down for about 3 hours so the cells settle. One donor can treat many eyes; it is already approved for bullous keratopathy in Japan.

Q: How do you prevent PBK at cataract surgery?
A: Specular count when guttae or a low count are suspected; dispersive viscoelastic (soft-shell technique); phaco in the bag, away from the cornea, with low energy; avoid an ACIOL; manage vitreous well; control IOP and inflammation after surgery. With Fuchs' dystrophy: cataract surgery alone if the count is over 1,000 cells/mm² and thickness under 600 µm; combined EK and cataract surgery if the count is under 800 or thickness over 640 µm.

Q: What is TASS and how does it differ from endophthalmitis?
A: Toxic anterior segment syndrome is sterile inflammation within 12–24 hours of surgery from contaminated instruments or solutions (detergent or glutaraldehyde residue): limbus-to-limbus oedema, fibrin, sometimes hypopyon, little pain, no vitritis; it responds to intensive steroid. Endophthalmitis begins after 2–7 days, with pain, vitritis and positive cultures.

Q: What is Brown–McLean syndrome?
A: Peripheral corneal oedema with a clear centre, years after intracapsular cataract extraction, often with orange pigment on the endothelium; it seldom progresses and usually needs no treatment.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Not examining the fellow eye, and so missing Fuchs' dystrophy as the cause.
- Reading Goldmann IOP at face value: it is falsely low on an oedematous cornea.
- Not naming the IOL type and position: an ACIOL changes the plan (IOL exchange).
- Promising vision without checking projection, B-scan and the macula (CMO).
- Prescribing dorzolamide or brinzolamide to a decompensating cornea.
- Saying PK is the treatment of choice: EK is preferred when the stroma is clear.
- Missing an infiltrate under a bandage contact lens.
:::

:::recall
- PBK = endothelial pump failure after IOL surgery; oedema below about 400–500 cells/mm².
- Morning blur, bullae, Descemet's folds, thick cornea; always look for guttae in the fellow eye.
- Medical treatment is temporary: 5% saline, BCL, IOP lowering without topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitors.
- Clear stroma: DSEK or DMEK; scarred stroma: PK; ACIOL: exchange it.
- DMEK: best vision, rejection 1–3%.
- Rho-kinase inhibitors and cultured endothelial cells are the newer options.
- Causes, mnemonic **FAVOURITE**: Fuchs', ACIOL, Vitreous touch, Operative trauma, UGH syndrome, Raised IOP, Inflammation (uveitis, herpes), TASS, Endothelial (Descemet's) detachment.
:::
