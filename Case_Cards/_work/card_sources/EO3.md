## At a glance
- **Aphakia**: absence of the crystalline lens from the pupillary area — surgical (commonest), traumatic, posterior dislocation (functional aphakia) or, rarely, congenital. Optically the eye is **highly hypermetropic (about +10 dioptres (D) at the spectacle plane)** and cannot accommodate.
- **Zonular (lamellar) cataract**: the commonest developmental cataract; a shell of opacity around a clear centre, with clear cortex outside and spoke-like **riders**; usually bilateral and autosomal dominant. Being partial, it often allows fair vision.
- **S.No 29 ★**: "Right eye aphakia, presumably after lens aspiration in childhood for developmental cataract (confirm), with amblyopia; left eye zonular (lamellar) cataract — bilateral developmental cataract." Look for squint, nystagmus and aphakic glaucoma.
- **S.No 48**: "Right eye pseudophakia (posterior chamber intraocular lens, PCIOL) with a superior trabeculectomy bleb and surgical peripheral iridectomy; left eye aphakia; both eyes advanced glaucomatous cupping (cup–disc ratio 0.9)." The bleb is covered on card G2.
- Examiners ask: signs of aphakia, problems with aphakic glasses, correction with and without capsular support, the morphology and causes of lamellar cataract, timing of surgery, and the intraocular lens (IOL) power and posterior capsule in children.

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
Mr X, a 16-year-old boy, had surgery on his right eye at the age of 3 for a "white spot" and wears a +11 D glass for that eye only; his left eye vision has been slowly falling for school work. The right anterior chamber is deep, the iris is tremulous, the pupil is black with a thin posterior capsular remnant, and only two Purkinje images are seen; best-corrected vision is 6/36 with +11.00 D sphere, suggesting amblyopia. The left lens has a 5 mm disc-shaped opacity in the fetal nucleus with a clear centre, clear cortex and spoke-like riders at its edge; vision is 6/18 undilated, improving to 6/12 after dilatation. Intraocular pressure is 16 and 14 mmHg (millimetres of mercury) with open angles, the discs are healthy, and there is a small right exotropia without nystagmus. There are no signs of rickets or hypocalcaemia.

**Diagnosis (S.No 29):** Right eye surgical aphakia after childhood lens aspiration, with amblyopia and sensory exotropia; left eye zonular (lamellar) cataract — bilateral developmental cataract.

**Diagnosis (S.No 48):** Right eye pseudophakia with a superior trabeculectomy bleb and surgical peripheral iridectomy; left eye aphakia; both eyes advanced glaucoma (cup–disc ratio 0.9).

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Define aphakia and give its causes.
A: Absence of the crystalline lens from the pupillary area. Surgical: cataract extraction without IOL, lensectomy in children, nucleus drop. Traumatic: extrusion through a wound, absorption after capsule rupture in a child. Posterior dislocation into the vitreous (functional aphakia). Congenital: primary (lens never formed) or secondary (absorbed in utero, for example rubella).

Q: What is the optical state of an aphakic eye?
A: High hypermetropia, because the lens supplies about +16 to +19 D of the eye's power; at the spectacle plane a previously emmetropic eye needs about +10 to +11 D, plus about +3 D for near. Accommodation is lost.

Q: What is a zonular (lamellar) cataract?
A: An opacity confined to one lamella of the lens, usually in the fetal nucleus around a clearer embryonic nucleus, with clear cortex outside, and short spoke-like opacities (riders) straddling its equator. It is the commonest visually significant developmental cataract, usually bilateral and symmetrical.

Q: Why does it form a shell, and what are riders?
A: An insult acting for a short period of lens growth makes only the fibres laid down then opaque; later fibres are clear and bury the opacity. Its diameter dates the insult (larger means later). Riders are small arcuate cortical opacities at the edge of the zone, like riders astride a horse.

Q: What causes lamellar cataract?
A: Autosomal dominant heredity (commonest; crystallin, connexin and HSF4 genes); hypocalcaemia (neonatal tetany, hypoparathyroidism); vitamin D deficiency (rickets); neonatal hypoglycaemia; malnutrition in late fetal or early infant life; maternal rubella. Look for dental enamel defects formed at the same time.

### Examination and signs
Q: What are the signs of aphakia?
A: Deep anterior chamber; iridodonesis (tremulous iris on eye movement); jet-black pupil; only Purkinje images 1 and 2; surgical scar and peripheral iridectomy; vitreous face in the pupil or vitreous in the chamber; capsular remnants; retinoscopy showing high hypermetropia, with vision improving on +10 D.

Q: Describe the Purkinje–Sanson images.
A: Image 1: anterior cornea, bright, erect, virtual. Image 2: posterior cornea, faint, erect. Image 3: anterior lens surface, largest, erect, moves with the light. Image 4: posterior lens surface, small, inverted, real, moves against the light. Aphakia shows only 1 and 2; in pseudophakia the IOL gives 3 and 4.

Q: How do you tell surgical aphakia from a lens dislocated into the vitreous?
A: Both give a deep chamber, iridodonesis and a black pupil, but a dislocated lens is seen in the vitreous on indirect ophthalmoscopy or B-scan, there is usually no surgical scar, and it may cause uveitis or glaucoma (card EO4).

Q: How do you examine a lamellar cataract?
A: Distant direct ophthalmoscopy: a dark disc with a red glow around it. Slit lamp with dilated pupil: the optical section shows an opaque lamella with clear zones inside and outside; retroillumination shows the riders. Record size, density and vision undilated and dilated.

Q: When is a lamellar cataract visually significant?
A: A central opacity of 3 mm or more, dense, vision worse than about 6/18 or falling school performance, no clear fundus view through it, a poor retinoscopic reflex, squint or nystagmus. Good vision through a dilated pupil means it can wait.

Q: What are the problems of aphakic spectacles?
A: Magnification of about 25–30% (objects look nearer, misjudged distances); ring scotoma from the prism at the lens edge, causing the "jack-in-the-box" phenomenon; pincushion distortion; spherical and chromatic aberration; restricted field; heavy, thick lenses that magnify the eyes; sensitivity to vertex distance; no accommodation.

Q: What is the jack-in-the-box phenomenon?
A: The peripheral prism of a strong plus lens creates a ring-shaped blind zone between the lens field and the peripheral field. It moves with eye movement (roving ring scotoma), so objects suddenly pop out of it or vanish into it.

Q: Why are spectacles unsuitable for unilateral aphakia?
A: With a normal fellow eye, the 25–30% image size difference (aniseikonia) cannot be fused (tolerance is about 5%), causing diplopia. A contact lens (about 7–10%) or an IOL (about 2–3%) is used instead.

### Investigations
Q: What would you do before a secondary IOL?
A: Keratometry and axial length; chamber depth; specular microscopy; gonioscopy (before an anterior chamber IOL, ACIOL); dilated check of capsular support and vitreous; B-scan if media are hazy; macular optical coherence tomography (OCT); peripheral retina for breaks.

Q: How do you investigate bilateral developmental cataract?
A: A familial autosomal dominant lamellar cataract needs nothing more. Otherwise: TORCH serology (toxoplasma, rubella, cytomegalovirus, herpes) and syphilis serology; urine for reducing substances (galactosaemia) and amino acids (Lowe syndrome); serum calcium, phosphorus and glucose; paediatric and genetic review. Ocular: age-appropriate acuity, B-scan, biometry under anaesthesia, visual evoked potentials.

### Management
Q: What are the options for correcting aphakia?
A: Spectacles (bilateral aphakia, elderly); contact lenses (rigid gas-permeable, silicone hydrogel, extended-wear silicone in infants); secondary IOL — sulcus three-piece IOL if there is capsular support, otherwise an ACIOL, iris-claw IOL or scleral-fixated IOL (sutured, or sutureless intrascleral: glued IOL, Yamane flanged technique).

Q: How do you choose a secondary IOL?
A: Capsule or anterior rim present: three-piece IOL in the sulcus, with optic capture if possible. No support: healthy angle, deep chamber, good endothelium and no glaucoma, an open-loop ACIOL; healthy iris, a retropupillary iris-claw IOL; glaucoma, synechiae, a child or damaged iris, a scleral-fixated or glued IOL. Clear the anterior vitreous first.

Q: Which option for the left eye of S.No 48?
A: Advanced glaucoma with a bleb in the other eye: avoid an ACIOL (angle damage, IOP rise, endothelial loss). Use a sulcus IOL if a capsular rim exists, otherwise a scleral-fixated or retropupillary iris-claw IOL through a temporal approach, sparing the superior conjunctiva for filtering surgery; control IOP first. With 0.9 cups the visual gain may be small; a contact lens is an alternative. Glasses fail because of aniseikonia with the pseudophakic right eye.

Q: Describe the glued IOL and the Yamane technique.
A: Glued IOL (Agarwal): two partial-thickness scleral flaps 180° apart about 1.5 mm behind the limbus, sclerotomies beneath, haptics of a three-piece IOL externalised and tucked into scleral tunnels, flaps sealed with fibrin glue, with vitrectomy. Yamane: haptics drawn out through 30-gauge needles and their ends melted into flanges, without flaps or sutures.

Q: What are the complications of ACIOL and iris-claw lenses?
A: ACIOL: corneal decompensation, uveitis–glaucoma–hyphaema syndrome, peripheral anterior synechiae, cystoid macular oedema, pupil block. Iris-claw: oval pupil, iris chafing, uveitis, hyphaema, disenclavation and dislocation. Scleral-fixated: suture erosion or breakage, tilt, vitreous haemorrhage, retinal detachment, endophthalmitis.

Q: How do you manage a lamellar cataract?
A: Partial, with good dilated vision: observe; a mydriatic (phenylephrine 2.5%) may help; correct refraction and treat amblyopia. Visually significant: lens aspiration with IOL. In bilateral cases operate the denser eye first and the second within about a week.

Q: What is the timing of surgery for a dense congenital cataract?
A: Unilateral: before 6 weeks of age; bilateral: before 8 weeks, before deprivation amblyopia becomes fixed. Operating in the first 4 weeks carries a higher glaucoma risk. Lamellar cataracts usually appear later and are partial, so their timing is decided by visual function.

Q: What are the steps of paediatric cataract surgery?
A: Small incisions, sutured because of low scleral rigidity; high-viscosity viscoelastic; anterior continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis (CCC) or vitrectorhexis (elastic capsule); aspiration of the soft lens; primary posterior capsulorhexis with anterior vitrectomy in young children; in-the-bag single-piece hydrophobic acrylic IOL; intensive topical steroid and cycloplegic afterwards.

Q: When do you do a primary posterior capsulotomy and anterior vitrectomy?
A: Visual axis opacification is almost certain in young children if the capsule is left. Under 5 years: posterior capsulorhexis with anterior vitrectomy; 5–8 years: posterior capsulorhexis with or without vitrectomy; over 8 years: keep the capsule and use neodymium-doped yttrium aluminium garnet (Nd:YAG) laser capsulotomy later. The anterior hyaloid otherwise acts as a scaffold for lens epithelial cells.

Q: How do you choose IOL power in a child?
A: Keratometry and immersion A-scan under anaesthesia; SRK/T, Holladay 1 or Hoffer Q for short eyes; implant if axial length is over 17 mm and corneal diameter over 10 mm. Under-correct for the myopic shift: 20% under 2 years, 15% at 2–4, 10% at 4–8, emmetropia after 8. Rough guide (rule of 7): age in years plus target hypermetropia is about 7.

Q: What did the Infant Aphakia Treatment Study (IATS) show?
A: In infants aged 1–6 months with unilateral cataract, a primary IOL gave no better vision at 4.5 years than contact lens aphakia, with more adverse events and reoperations (mostly visual axis opacification). So contact lens correction is reasonable in early infancy, with an IOL later.

Q: How do you correct aphakia in an infant without an IOL?
A: An extended-wear silicone or rigid gas-permeable contact lens, over-corrected by about +2 to +3 D because the infant's world is near; spectacles in bilateral aphakia; secondary IOL later (often after 2 years); patching of the better eye for amblyopia.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: What is aphakic glaucoma, and how is it managed?
A: Glaucoma after lens removal, commonest after childhood surgery (3–32%). Early: pupil block by vitreous, air or membrane (iridectomy, membranectomy). Late: open-angle, years later, so check IOP, corneal diameter, axial length and disc for life. Treat with beta-blockers, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, prostaglandin analogues (no brimonidine under 2 years); then a drainage device; cyclophotocoagulation if refractory.

Q: What are the risk factors for glaucoma after paediatric cataract surgery?
A: Surgery in the first month of life, microcornea or microphthalmos, persistent fetal vasculature, retained lens matter, repeated surgery and postoperative inflammation.

Q: What other complications follow aphakia or paediatric cataract surgery?
A: Amblyopia, visual axis opacification, inflammatory membranes, IOL decentration, retinal detachment (about 1–1.5%, more after intracapsular surgery), cystoid macular oedema, endophthalmitis, aphakic bullous keratopathy from vitreous touch, strabismus.

Q: What is the visual prognosis?
A: Lamellar cataract does well, being partial and later in onset; dense early bilateral cataract with nystagmus, unilateral cataract with poor occlusion, microphthalmos and other anomalies do worse. Most failures are from amblyopia, not surgery.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: What is the bag-in-the-lens IOL?
A: An IOL with a peripheral groove into which matched anterior and posterior capsulorhexis edges are captured, sealing the bag and keeping the visual axis clear without vitrectomy; used in children.

Q: Why is optic capture used in children?
A: Pushing the optic through the posterior capsulorhexis behind the capsule keeps the IOL centred and the visual axis clear; in older children it can replace an anterior vitrectomy.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Saying "aphakia" without the cause and without looking for capsular remnants: the capsule decides the secondary IOL.
- Forgetting IOP and the disc: aphakic glaucoma after childhood surgery is silent, and S.No 48 has 0.9 cups.
- Prescribing glasses for unilateral aphakia: aniseikonia makes them unwearable.
- Calling a lamellar cataract "nuclear": the lamellar one has a clearer centre, clear cortex and riders.
- Operating on every lamellar cataract: a partial one with good dilated vision can be observed.
- Offering an anterior chamber IOL to a glaucomatous eye or a child.
- Forgetting amblyopia treatment after childhood surgery.
:::

:::recall
- Aphakia: deep chamber, tremulous iris, black pupil, two Purkinje images, about +10 D.
- Aphakic glasses: 25–30% magnification, ring scotoma, jack-in-the-box, pincushion; never for unilateral aphakia.
- Lamellar cataract: opaque shell, clearer centre, riders; heredity, low calcium, low vitamin D.
- Dense congenital cataract: operate before 6 weeks (unilateral) or 8 weeks (bilateral).
- Under-correct the IOL: 20% under 2 years, 15% to 4, 10% to 8, emmetropia after 8.
- Posterior capsulorhexis with anterior vitrectomy under 5 years.
- Mnemonic for aphakia signs, **DIPPS**: Deep chamber, Iridodonesis, Pupil jet-black, Purkinje 3 and 4 absent, Scar and iridectomy.
:::
