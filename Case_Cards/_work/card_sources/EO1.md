## At a glance
- **What it is:** pallor of the optic disc *plus* evidence of optic nerve dysfunction — the end stage of retinal ganglion cell axon loss anywhere from the retina to the lateral geniculate body. It is a sign, not a diagnosis: always name the type and the cause.
- **Post-neuritic optic atrophy:** follows optic neuritis. After papillitis it has the secondary pattern (dirty grey disc, blurred margins, gliosis, sheathed vessels); after retrobulbar neuritis it looks primary, often with temporal pallor.
- **Why examiners keep it:** optic nerve function testing, the look of each type, causes and work-up, the Optic Neuritis Treatment Trial (ONTT), neuromyelitis optica and rehabilitation.
- **S.No 2 (patient already here):** "Both eyes post-neuritic (secondary) optic atrophy following optic neuritis; best-corrected acuity __ and __; relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) __; red–green dyschromatopsia; field defects __; cause — multiple sclerosis (MS), neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) or myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) antibody-associated disease (MOGAD) — to be established."
- **S.No 37:** "Both eyes retinitis pigmentosa (RP) with consecutive optic atrophy (waxy disc pallor), arteriolar attenuation and bone-spicule pigmentation."
- **Rule:** pallor alone is not atrophy — prove dysfunction with acuity, RAPD, colour vision and fields.

## What to look for and present

| Onset and course | Sudden and painful (neuritis); sudden and painless (ischaemia); gradual (compression, toxic, hereditary, glaucoma); one eye, both, or sequential; recovery and recurrences. |

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
Mrs X, 32, had painful loss of vision in the right eye three years ago and in the left eye one year ago; each recovered partly over six to eight weeks, and she received intravenous steroids for the second episode. She has no other neurological symptoms and no relevant drug or family history. Best-corrected vision is 6/24 in the right eye and 6/12 in the left, with a right relative afferent pupillary defect, Ishihara plates read 4 of 21 on the right and 12 of 21 on the left, and red desaturation on the right. Both discs are dirty greyish-white with slightly blurred margins, cups filled with glial tissue, lamina not visible, few capillaries on the disc, and narrowed, sheathed arterioles near the disc; the maculae and peripheries are normal. Fields show a right central scotoma and a left paracentral scotoma, and the neurological examination is normal.

**Diagnosis (S.No 2):** both eyes post-neuritic (secondary) optic atrophy after sequential optic neuritis, right worse than left; the cause — multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder or myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody disease — to be established by brain and orbit imaging and antibody tests.

**S.No 37:** both eyes retinitis pigmentosa with consecutive optic atrophy (waxy disc pallor), arteriolar attenuation and bone-spicule pigmentation.
:::

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Define optic atrophy.
A: Not a disease but the morphological end point of any process that irreversibly damages retinal ganglion cells and their axons in the anterior visual pathway, seen as disc pallor with loss of nerve function.

Q: Why does an atrophic disc look pale?
A: Healthy axon bundles conduct light into the disc, where it picks up the pink of the capillaries. After axon loss, astrocytes lie across the incoming light and reflect it white; capillaries are fewer; and loss of tissue exposes the white lamina cribrosa.

Q: What is the Kestenbaum capillary index?
A: The number of small capillaries (not the main vessels) crossing the disc margin — normally about 10–12; 6 or fewer indicates atrophy. A fall in the number of disc vessels is called Kestenbaum's sign.

Q: Classify optic atrophy.
A: Ophthalmoscopic — primary, secondary, consecutive, glaucomatous (cavernous) and partial (temporal, band, altitudinal, wedge). Pathological — ascending (anterograde) or descending (retrograde), plus transsynaptic. Aetiological — hereditary, consecutive, post-inflammatory, pressure or traction, ischaemic, toxic or nutritional, metabolic, traumatic.

Q: What are ascending and descending atrophy?
A: Ascending — the lesion is in the retina or disc and degeneration travels towards the lateral geniculate body (central retinal artery occlusion, RP, glaucoma, extensive panretinal photocoagulation). Descending — the lesion is in the nerve, chiasm or tract and degeneration travels towards the eye (compression, trauma, retrobulbar neuritis); pallor appears after 4–6 weeks and is complete by 6–8 weeks.

Q: Describe primary optic atrophy and give causes.
A: Orderly axon loss replaced by glial columns without disturbing the disc architecture: chalky white disc, sharp margins, lamina cribrosa well seen (laminar dots), shallow saucer cup, normal vessels and retina. Causes: retrobulbar neuritis, compression (pituitary tumour, meningioma, glioma, aneurysm), trauma, hereditary, toxic and nutritional optic neuropathy.

Q: Describe secondary optic atrophy and give causes.
A: Follows long-standing disc swelling, with marked glial proliferation: dirty grey disc, blurred margins, cup filled with fibroglial tissue hiding the lamina, peripapillary sheathing and narrowing of arteries, tortuous veins, sometimes drusen-like bodies. Causes: papilloedema, papillitis, anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy.

Q: Describe consecutive optic atrophy and give causes.
A: Ascending atrophy from retinal or choroidal disease: waxy yellow pallor, normal margins, normal cup, marked arteriolar attenuation, with the causative retinal disease visible. Causes: RP, central retinal artery occlusion, extensive photocoagulation, diffuse chorioretinitis, long-standing retinal detachment, high myopia.

Q: What exactly is "post-neuritic" optic atrophy?
A: The atrophy that follows optic neuritis. After papillitis or neuroretinitis it is secondary in pattern (grey, blurred margins, gliosis, sheathed vessels); after retrobulbar neuritis it is primary in pattern, typically temporal pallor from loss of the papillomacular bundle. Say which pattern you see.

Q: Why is the disc waxy in RP (S.No 37)?
A: Ganglion cell loss secondary to outer retinal degeneration plus a layer of astroglial tissue over the disc, which gives a waxy, yellowish sheen, with marked arteriolar attenuation.

### Examination and signs
Q: Which tests show optic nerve dysfunction?
A: Reduced acuity, RAPD, dyschromatopsia (mainly red–green), red desaturation, reduced brightness and contrast sensitivity, field defects (central, centrocaecal, arcuate, altitudinal), prolonged visual evoked potential (VEP), and a normal photostress recovery (prolonged in macular disease).

Q: How do you grade an RAPD?
A: Swinging flashlight test: grade 1 — weak initial constriction then greater redilation; 2 — initial stall then dilation; 3 — immediate dilation; 4 — no reaction (amaurotic pupil). Quantify with neutral density filters (0.3–1.8 log units) before the normal eye. If one pupil cannot move, judge the other pupil (reverse RAPD).

Q: How do you test red desaturation and brightness?
A: Show a red bottle top to each eye in turn and ask how red it looks in the bad eye if the good eye scores 100%; do the same for the brightness of a torch.

Q: How does colour vision separate optic nerve from retinal disease?
A: Optic nerve disease gives a red–green defect early and out of proportion to acuity; macular disease gives a blue–yellow defect. Exceptions: glaucoma and dominant optic atrophy cause blue–yellow (tritan) loss.

Q: What do patterns of pallor tell you?
A: Temporal pallor — papillomacular bundle (MS, toxic and nutritional, Leber hereditary optic neuropathy, dominant optic atrophy); band or bow-tie pallor — loss of nasal retinal fibres (chiasmal lesion, or the eye opposite an optic tract lesion); altitudinal — ischaemic optic neuropathy; wedge — branch retinal artery occlusion; diffuse — most others.

Q: How do you tell glaucomatous from non-glaucomatous atrophy?
A: Glaucoma — cupping exceeds pallor, rim stays pink, vertical cup, bayoneting, nasal shift of vessels, laminar dots, acuity and colour preserved until late. Non-glaucomatous — pallor exceeds cupping, pale rim, shallow cup, acuity, colour and RAPD affected early.

Q: What is the Foster Kennedy syndrome?
A: Optic atrophy in one eye from direct compression (frontal lobe or olfactory groove meningioma) with papilloedema in the other from raised intracranial pressure. Pseudo-Foster Kennedy is commoner: sequential ischaemic optic neuropathy, with atrophy in the old eye and swelling in the new.

Q: What pale-looking discs are not optic atrophy?
A: Normal temporal pallor, pseudophakia, myopic or tilted discs, a large physiological cup, hypoplasia, coloboma, morning glory, optic pit and drusen — function is normal or otherwise explained.

### Investigations
Q: How would you investigate S.No 2?
A: Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of brain and orbits with gadolinium and fat suppression; optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the retinal nerve fibre layer (RNFL) and ganglion cell layer; VEP; serum aquaporin-4 (AQP4) and MOG antibodies by cell-based assay; blood count, B12, syphilis serology, angiotensin-converting enzyme, antinuclear antibody, erythrocyte sedimentation rate; cerebrospinal fluid oligoclonal bands if MS is suspected.

Q: What does OCT add?
A: Measures RNFL and ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer thinning (temporal after neuritis, bow-tie in chiasmal disease) and detects loss long before pallor — pallor needs about 50% axon loss, whereas OCT detects 7–9 µm thinning. Severe atrophy reaches a "floor" (about 40–50 µm), beyond which RNFL cannot track change.

Q: What does the VEP show?
A: Demyelination — prolonged P100 latency with relatively preserved amplitude, a delay that often persists after recovery and marks past neuritis; axonal, ischaemic or compressive loss — reduced amplitude with near-normal latency. It also helps in suspected non-organic loss.

Q: How does MRI separate MS, NMOSD and MOGAD?
A: MS — short posterior optic nerve lesion, ovoid periventricular plaques perpendicular to the ventricles; NMOSD — long posterior lesion reaching the chiasm, spinal lesion over 3 or more vertebral segments; MOGAD — long anterior lesion with perineural sheath enhancement and disc swelling.

Q: What confirms consecutive atrophy in S.No 37?
A: Full-field electroretinogram — rod responses reduced or extinguished first, then cones; fields show ring scotoma and later a tubular field; OCT shows outer retinal loss and any cystoid macular oedema.

### Management
Q: Can optic atrophy be treated?
A: Lost axons do not regenerate. Treat the cause to save what remains (decompress a tumour, stop ethambutol, give B12, lower intracranial or intraocular pressure, prevent further neuritis), protect the other eye, and rehabilitate.

Q: Describe the Optic Neuritis Treatment Trial.
A: 455 patients aged 18–46 with acute unilateral optic neuritis within 8 days, randomised to intravenous methylprednisolone 250 mg 6-hourly for 3 days then oral prednisone 1 mg/kg/day for 11 days; oral prednisone 1 mg/kg/day for 14 days; or placebo — each with a short taper.

Q: What did the ONTT show?
A: Intravenous steroid speeded recovery but did not change vision at 1 year; oral prednisone alone increased recurrences, so it is not used; MS conversion fell at 2 years in the intravenous group with an abnormal MRI, but not after 3 years. Fifteen-year risk of MS: 50% overall — 25% with a normal baseline MRI, 72% with one or more lesions. Ten-year recurrence: 35%.

Q: When would you give steroids today?
A: For faster recovery, severe or bilateral loss, an only eye or occupational need: intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days, then oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day for 11 days and taper — after excluding tuberculosis, diabetes and peptic ulcer. Refer to a neurologist for MS disease-modifying therapy when MRI shows lesions.

Q: How are NMOSD and MOGAD treated?
A: NMOSD (AQP4 antibody, women 9:1, severe, poor recovery): intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g for 5 days with early plasma exchange; prevent relapse with azathioprine, mycophenolate or rituximab, or newer eculizumab, inebilizumab or satralizumab — MS drugs such as interferon-beta and natalizumab can worsen it. MOGAD (often bilateral with marked disc swelling, good recovery): steroids with a slow taper over months, as relapses follow rapid withdrawal; intravenous immunoglobulin for relapsing disease.

Q: Outline low-vision rehabilitation.
A: Best refraction; hand, stand and electronic magnifiers and telescopes; large print, contrast and lighting; tints for glare; screen readers; eccentric viewing training for central scotomas; mobility training and a white cane for field loss; disability certification, vocational support and counselling.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: What is the visual prognosis after typical optic neuritis?
A: Recovery begins within 3 weeks in about 80% and within 5 weeks in over 90%; two-thirds regain 6/6 and fewer than 15% stay worse than 6/12. Colour, contrast and depth often remain subtly impaired, and RNFL thinning persists.

Q: Which forms carry a poor prognosis?
A: NMOSD (severe, bilateral, recurrent), arteritic ischaemic neuropathy, Leber hereditary optic neuropathy with the 11778 mutation, long-standing compression and severe toxic damage. Established atrophy itself does not recover.

Q: What are the features of Leber hereditary optic neuropathy?
A: Mitochondrial (maternal) inheritance; young men; painless sequential central loss weeks to months apart; acute disc hyperaemia with peripapillary telangiectatic vessels that do not leak, later temporal atrophy; centrocaecal scotoma. Mutations 11778 (commonest, worst recovery), 3460 and 14484 (best). Idebenone 900 mg/day; avoid tobacco and alcohol.

Q: How does ethambutol cause optic atrophy?
A: Dose-related toxicity (risk rises above 15 mg/kg/day), usually after 2–8 months: bilateral central or centrocaecal scotomas, dyschromatopsia, later temporal pallor. Stop the drug at once; check vision and colour at baseline and monthly; recovery is partial.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: What is the Uhthoff phenomenon?
A: Transient blurring in a previously affected eye when body temperature rises — exercise, a hot bath, fever — recovering within minutes to an hour on cooling. Heat worsens conduction block in demyelinated axons. It predicts MS and recurrence.

Q: What is the Pulfrich phenomenon?
A: Slower conduction in one optic nerve makes a pendulum swinging in a flat plane appear to move in an ellipse — a false depth cue from the latency difference. A neutral density filter over the normal eye can equalise it.

Q: How is the Lhermitte sign different?
A: An electric-shock feeling down the spine or limbs on bending the neck, from demyelination in the cervical cord — not a visual symptom. Do not swap it with Uhthoff.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Diagnosing optic atrophy from a pale disc alone — pseudophakia and normal temporal pallor fool many; prove dysfunction.
- Calling every post-neuritic disc "primary" — after papillitis it is secondary in pattern; describe colour, margins, cup and vessels.
- Saying "no RAPD, so the nerves are fine" in S.No 2 — symmetrical bilateral damage abolishes the RAPD.
- Swapping Uhthoff (heat-induced blur) and Lhermitte (electric shock on neck flexion).
- Quoting the ONTT as proof that steroids improve final vision — they only speed recovery; oral prednisone alone increases recurrences.
- Forgetting NMOSD and MOGAD in bilateral or severe neuritis, and treating NMOSD with MS drugs.
- In S.No 37, calling the waxy disc "primary" — it is consecutive (ascending) atrophy from RP.
:::
