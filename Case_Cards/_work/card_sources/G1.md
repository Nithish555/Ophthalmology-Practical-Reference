## At a glance
- **Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG)**: a chronic, progressive optic neuropathy with an **open, normal-looking angle** and reduced outflow; usually asymptomatic, found on routine examination or follow-up.
- Commonly kept: stable signs (cupped disc, measurable pressure, open angle). The **glaucomatous disc** is also a short case.
- Examiners test the **disc description in order**, intraocular pressure (IOP) with method, time and central corneal thickness (CCT), gonioscopy, target pressure and drugs.

## Case format — history
**Chief complaint:** "Mr X, a 70-year-old man, has come for regular follow-up for glaucoma" — or "…came with complaints of defective vision in both eyes for 1 year".

**House wording:** "Patient was apparently normal ___ years ago, then had defective vision in both eyes, gradual in onset, progressive in nature, not associated with pain or headache. He was found to have increased pressure elsewhere and started on eye drops (1 drop at bedtime)."

| What to ask | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **History of present illness:** onset, progression; blurring at one time of day | Gradual, often silent until late; blurring matches the IOP peak |
| Frequent change of near glasses | Pressure on zonules and ciliary body impairs accommodation; field loss mistaken for poor vision |

| **Treatment history:** "E/D (eye drops) timolol 0.5% BD (twice daily)" | Drug, strength, frequency; drops for **> 3 years** mean mitomycin C at trabeculectomy |
| **Personal and family:** glaucoma in relatives | Risk **4-fold in siblings, 2-fold in offspring** |

## Case format — examination
### General and systemic (this case only)
- **Pulse, blood pressure, chest**: bradycardia, heart block or wheeze bars beta-blockers; low diastolic perfusion pressure is a risk factor.
- **Face and skin**: haemangioma (Sturge–Weber syndrome), neurofibromatosis, carotid–cavernous fistula: raised episcleral venous pressure.

### Special tests
**Applanation tonometry** (card G8)
1. Do it first: gonioscopy lowers the reading; dilatation raises it by **4–5 mm Hg**.
2. GAT in each eye; note the time and the CCT (the GAT is accurate at **520 µm**).

Record: "Tn by GAT: RE 18, LE 16 mm Hg at 10 am."

**Gonioscopy** (card G9)
1. After tonometry, before dilatation.
2. Each quadrant: deepest structure seen and Shaffer grade (**4** = 35–40°, **3** = 25–35°, **2** = 20°, **1** = 10°, **slit** < 10°, **0** = closed).

:::gonio
RE | S=SS | T=SS | I=SS | N=SS
LE | S=SS | T=SS | I=SS | N=SS
caption: Deepest structure seen in each quadrant (SS = scleral spur) — open angles in all quadrants
:::

**Disc examination**
1. Dilate after tonometry and gonioscopy; direct ophthalmoscope first.
2. Slit lamp, **+90 dioptre (D)** lens: cup, rim against the **ISNT rule** (thickest Inferiorly, then Superiorly, Nasally, Temporally), notch, haemorrhage, laminar dots; red-free light for retinal nerve fibre layer (RNFL) defects.

Record: in slide order (Short-case version).

**Confrontation fields** (card G10)
1. Face to face, 60 cm apart; his right eye fixes your left, other eyes closed.
2. Bring a finger in from the periphery, midway, in each quadrant; compare with your own field.

Record: "Fields (confrontation test): full in all quadrants."

## What you must know
### Definition
**Glaucoma**: a chronic, progressive optic neuropathy with irreversible nerve damage and matching field defects, with or without raised IOP. **POAG**: open, normal-looking angle, reduced outflow, no other eye disease; onset after 40 (juvenile 3–40 years). Normal IOP **10–21 mm Hg**.

**Risk factors:** raised IOP; age (prevalence doubles each decade over 40); Black race; first-degree relatives; myopia; thin CCT; diastolic perfusion pressure < 55 mm Hg; diabetes, hypertension, thyroid disease; vasospasm.

### Classification
Grading of glaucomatous optic nerve head damage, with the Z value for target pressure:

@widths 18 22 46 14
| Stage | Disc | Field (mean deviation, MD, decibels, dB) | Z |
|---|---|---|---|
| Suspect | — | — | 0 |
| Mild (early) | Minimal cupping | Nasal step or paracentral scotoma; MD better than −6 dB | 1 |
| Moderate | NRR thinning | Arcuate scotoma; MD −6 to −12 dB | 3 |
| Severe | Marked cupping | Within the central 5°; MD worse than −12 dB | 5 |
| End-stage | Gross cupping | Small residual field | 7 |

### Pathogenesis
- **Outflow resistance**: greatest at the juxtacanalicular trabecular meshwork.
- **Mechanical**: raised IOP bows the lamina cribrosa back → axoplasmic transport blocked → ganglion cell apoptosis.
- **Vascular** (key in NTG): vasospasm, nocturnal hypotension, blood loss.
- **Poles first** (larger laminar pores): cupping often starts inferotemporally → early **upper arcuate scotoma**.
- **Genes**: myocilin (chromosome 1), optineurin (chromosome 10).

### Investigations
@widths 26 34 40
| Test | Why | Expected finding |
|---|---|---|
| Diurnal phasing: IOP 3-hourly for 24 hours | Baseline, peak, drop timing; NTG versus POAG | Swing **≥ 8 mm Hg** is significant (normal 3–6) |
| Pachymetry (CCT) | Correct the IOP reading | Thin **< 500 µm** reads falsely low; thick **> 570 µm** high |
| Humphrey 24-2 or 30-2 | Stage and follow (card G10) | Paracentral, arcuate, nasal step; respects the horizontal meridian |
| OCT of RNFL; macular ganglion cell complex (GCC) | Pre-perimetric diagnosis; progression | Superior and inferior thinning; double hump lost; eyes differ by **> 9 µm**; GCC thinning |

### Management
**Target pressure**: the IOP at or below which further damage is unlikely.
- **TP = IP (1 − IP/100) − Z ± 2** (TP target pressure; IP initial pressure; Z from the staging table).
- Example: IP 30 mm Hg, moderate (Z 3): 30 × 0.7 − 3 = 18, so **16–20 mm Hg**.
- Or lower IOP by **20%** (OHT), **30%** (mild damage, NTG), **40%** (advanced).

**Medical:** start one drug, a **PG analogue**; review at 2 months; if not at target, switch before adding.

| CAI (15–20%) | **Dorzolamide 2%**<br>Acetazolamide 250 mg tablet | Three times<br>2–4 times | Stinging; tablets: paraesthesia, renal stones; not in sulpha allergy |
| Miotic (15–20%) | **Pilocarpine 0.5–4%** | 2–4 times | Browache, induced myopia; not in uveitic or neovascular glaucoma |

**Laser trabeculoplasty**, selective (SLT) or argon (ALT): when drops cannot be used reliably (cost, memory, instillation, intolerance). Suits POAG, pigmentary, exfoliation, steroid-induced glaucoma; not uveitic, neovascular or angle-recession glaucoma.

**Trabeculectomy indications** (card G2): not at target on **3 drugs**, or fields progressing on maximal therapy; side effects or poor compliance; baseline IOP **> 40 mm Hg**; one eye lost; difficult follow-up.

**Cataract with glaucoma**: expect a miotic pupil, synechiae, weak zonules (PXF), bleeding, a postoperative IOP rise.
- **Cataract alone**: IOP controlled on 1–2 drugs, no significant damage; risks an early IOP spike; later filtering surgery fails more.
- **Combined with filtering surgery**: many drugs, poor compliance, significant field loss, PXF, one-eyed. More complications, slower recovery.

**Follow-up:** IOP and compliance 3-monthly (mild–moderate) or monthly (advanced); fields 6-monthly or 3-monthly; gonioscopy yearly (a thickening lens can add angle closure).

### Complications
- **Disease**: progressive field loss; blindness; sudden loss (acute IOP rise, vein occlusion, snuff-out).
- **Treatment**: surface toxicity, drug side effects; long-term drops lower surgical success.

### Prognosis and counselling
- Damage is **irreversible**; treatment stops further loss. Central vision goes last.
- Chronic: drops and reviews for life; check compliance every visit.
- **Drop technique**: PG at bedtime, timolol in the morning; after each drop, lacrimal occlusion (press the inner corner) with eyes closed for **3 minutes**.
- Examine first-degree relatives (high risk).

### Recent advances
- **SLT**: frequency-doubled neodymium-doped yttrium aluminium garnet (Nd:YAG) 532 nm laser targeting pigmented trabecular cells.
- **Microinvasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS)**: Schlemm's canal stents, goniotomy, canal dilatation, suprachoroidal stents.

## Viva questions
### Why did you ask / examine…?
Q: Why did you ask about night blindness?
A: Dark adaptation is defective in open-angle glaucoma; miotics worsen it.

Q: Why did you ask about frequent change of glasses?
A: Pressure on the zonules and ciliary body impairs accommodation; field loss is mistaken for poor vision.

Q: Why did you ask about haloes and headache with vomiting?
A: To rule out angle closure; haloes mean corneal oedema.

Q: Why did you ask about sudden loss of vision?
A: Acute IOP rise, central retinal vein occlusion, or snuff-out after trabeculectomy.

Q: Why did you ask about asthma, heart disease and sulpha allergy?
A: No beta-blocker in asthma, COPD, heart failure or heart block; no CAI in sulpha allergy.

Q: Why did you measure tension before gonioscopy and dilatation?
A: Gonioscopy lowers it; dilatation raises it by 4–5 mm Hg.

### Diagnosis
Q: What makes you suspect glaucoma?
A: Raised IOP; vertical CDR ≥ 0.7 or asymmetry > 0.2; rim breaking ISNT; RNFL defect; field defect.

Q: Why is the vertical cup–disc ratio more important?
A: Early rim loss is at the poles; a normal cup is horizontally oval.

Q: What is the significance of a disc haemorrhage?
A: Active disease: flame-shaped, inferotemporal, precedes field loss by months; commoner in NTG.

Q: What are bayoneting, nasalisation and baring of circumlinear vessels?
A: Double angulation of a vessel; vessels pushed nasally; a vessel left bare as the rim recedes.

Q: What are the laminar dot sign and beta-zone atrophy?
A: Laminar pores seen in an advanced cup; atrophy next to the disc with sclera visible.

Q: How does NTG differ from POAG?
A: A diagnosis of exclusion: IOP below 22 mm Hg on diurnal testing; more haemorrhages, notching and PPA.

Q: When will you suspect a lesion other than glaucoma?
A: Pallor more than cupping, dyschromatopsia, a field respecting the vertical meridian: order neuroimaging.

### Investigations
Q: What is the role of CCT?
A: The GAT is accurate at 520 µm; thin corneas read falsely low, thick ones high.

Q: Why do diurnal phasing?
A: Baseline and peak; progression despite good readings; drop timing; NTG versus POAG.

Q: Can the field be normal with a glaucomatous disc?
A: Yes: 25–30% of ganglion cells are lost before white-on-white perimetry shows it.

Q: How does glaucoma show on OCT?
A: Superior and inferior RNFL thinning (7 o'clock right eye, 5 o'clock left first); flattened double hump.

### Management and surgery
Q: How do you calculate target pressure?
A: TP = IP (1 − IP/100) − Z ± 2; Z 0 suspect, 1 early, 3 moderate, 5 severe, 7 end-stage.

Q: Why is a PG analogue given at bedtime?
A: Its effect peaks 10–14 hours later, with fewer vascular-dilatation symptoms.

Q: Why is timolol best given in the morning?
A: It blunts the early-morning rise and avoids hypotension in sleep.

Q: When will you advise trabeculectomy?
A: Not at target on 3 drugs or progressing; poor compliance or side effects; baseline > 40 mm Hg; one eye lost.

### Complications
Q: What are the systemic effects of timolol, and how do you reduce them?
A: Bradycardia, hypotension, bronchospasm, depression; lacrimal occlusion with eyes closed for 3 minutes.

### Recent advances
Q: What is SLT?
A: A 532 nm frequency-doubled Nd:YAG laser targeting melanin in trabecular cells.

Q: What is MIGS?
A: Minimal-tissue pressure-lowering surgery for mild to moderate glaucoma: canal stents, goniotomy, suprachoroidal stents.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- IOP without method, time and CCT, or after gonioscopy or dilatation.
- "Open angle" without gonioscopy; reversing Scheie and Shaffer (Scheie 4: only Schwalbe's line visible; Shaffer 4: wide open).
- "CDR 0.8" alone: give the vertical ratio, rim (ISNT), notch, haemorrhage, vessels, PPA, RNFL.
- Missing PXF at the pupil or lens: it changes diagnosis and cataract surgery.
- CCT in the clinical table: it is an investigation.
- Pallor more than cupping called glaucoma: needs neuroimaging.
:::

## Short-case version
:::short
**Spot diagnosis:** "Glaucomatous optic disc: vertically enlarged cup with neuroretinal rim thinning, suggestive of glaucomatous optic neuropathy."

**Focused examination:**
1. Pupils for RAPD before dilating.
2. Direct ophthalmoscope at arm's length (red glow), then close: media, disc.
3. Slit lamp with +90 D: cup, rim (ISNT), notch, haemorrhage, laminar dots; red-free light for RNFL defects.
4. Describe in slide order: size, disc shape, cup shape, margins, colour, cup–disc ratio, rim, vessels, arteriovenous ratio, peripapillary changes, RNFL defects.

**Model:** "Disc normal in size, vertically oval; cup vertically oval; margins well defined; pale; cup–disc ratio 0.9; circumferential rim thinning; centrally arising, dichotomously branching vessels; arteriovenous ratio 2:3; peripapillary atrophy; superior and inferior wedge-shaped RNFL defects; suggestive of glaucomatous optic neuropathy."

**Three signs to show:**
1. Vertical cup–disc ratio **≥ 0.7**, or asymmetry **> 0.2**.
2. Rim thinning or a **notch** breaking the ISNT rule.
3. **Bayoneting** and **nasalisation**.
:::

## Say-it script
:::say
My patient is a 70-year-old man on regular follow-up, on eye drops for glaucoma in both eyes for 2 years. He has gradual, painless, progressive defective vision in both eyes, with no coloured haloes, headache with vomiting, redness, trauma or steroid use.

Best-corrected visual acuity is 6/18 in the right eye and 6/9 in the left. Intraocular pressure by Goldmann applanation tonometry is 18 and 16 millimetres of mercury at ten in the morning. The anterior chambers are Van Herick grade four, there is no relative afferent pupillary defect, and both eyes are pseudophakic. Gonioscopy shows open angles, with the scleral spur seen in all four quadrants. The right disc has a vertical cup–disc ratio of 0.8 with superior and inferior rim thinning, nasalisation, bayoneting and peripapillary atrophy; the left has 0.6 with an inferior notch. Confrontation fields are full.

My diagnosis is both eyes primary open-angle glaucoma with pseudophakia, with intraocular pressure under control on medical management.

I would do pachymetry, Humphrey fields, optical coherence tomography and diurnal phasing, and set a target pressure. If it is met, I continue the prostaglandin analogue; if not, I switch or add a drug, then consider laser trabeculoplasty or trabeculectomy. I will counsel him on lifelong treatment, compliance and drop technique.
:::

## Quick recall
:::recall
- Normal IOP **10–21 mm Hg**; asymmetry **≥ 4 mm Hg** is significant.
- Vertical CDR **≥ 0.7** or asymmetry **> 0.2**: suspect glaucoma.
- **ISNT**: rim thickest Inferiorly, then Superiorly, Nasally, Temporally.
- **25–30%** of ganglion cells are lost before a field defect.
- **TP = IP (1 − IP/100) − Z ± 2**; Z = 0, 1, 3, 5, 7.
- PG analogue first, at bedtime; timolol in the morning.

**Mnemonic:** **PART B** before dilating: Pupillary reflex, Angle, Rubeosis, Tension, BCVA (best-corrected visual acuity).
:::
