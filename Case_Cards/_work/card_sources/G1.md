## At a glance
- **Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG)**: a chronic, progressive optic neuropathy with an open, normal-looking angle and reduced outflow. Usually silent; found at a routine check.
- Kept often for its stable signs: cupped disc, measurable pressure, open angle. The **glaucomatous optic disc** is also a short case.
- Examiners test the reasons, the examination order, the disc description and the ladder.

**Keywords the examiner listens for:** Goldmann applanation tonometry · central corneal thickness · Van Herick · relative afferent pupillary defect · gonioscopy · vertical cup–disc ratio · ISNT rule · neuroretinal rim · bayoneting · disc haemorrhage · retinal nerve fibre layer · Humphrey · target pressure · prostaglandin analogue · trabeculectomy

### Step 1 — Visual acuity, pinhole and near vision
**Do:** Each eye unaided, with pinhole and with glasses; near vision at 33 cm; colour vision.

**Record:** "RE 6/18, PH 6/12; LE 6/9; N6 at 33 cm both eyes."

Q: Why is central vision kept until late in glaucoma?
A: **Central vision** fibres arise closest to the disc, lie most superficially and leave by its centre. Arcuate fibres at the poles suffer first.

Q: Why test colour vision?
A: **Asymmetric dyschromatopsia** suggests another optic neuropathy, not glaucoma: an indication for neuroimaging.

### Step 2 — Pupils, for a relative afferent pupillary defect (before any drop)
**Do:** The direct and consensual reflexes of each eye; then the swinging torch light test.

**Record:** "RTL 3 mm, no RAPD", or "RE: RAPD present".

Q: Why check the pupils before any drop?
A: A **relative afferent pupillary defect** cannot be judged in a pupil made small or large by drugs. Hence the mnemonic PART B.

Q: What does an RAPD mean in glaucoma?
A: **Advanced, asymmetric optic nerve damage**. With only mild cupping it suggests another cause, so I order neuroimaging.

### Step 3 — Slit-lamp examination of the anterior segment, with Van Herick grading
**Do:** Lids to lens. Van Herick: a thin slit 1 mm inside the temporal limbus at 60°; peripheral AC depth against corneal thickness.

**Record:** "AC: VH grade IV (ND). Iris CPN. Lens PCIOL. No PXF."

Q: How do you grade the anterior chamber by Van Herick?
A: **Van Herick** compares peripheral AC depth with corneal thickness. Grade 4 ≥ 1; grade 3 ¼–½; grade 2 = ¼; grade 1 < ¼; grade 0 contact. Below ¼, the angle may be occludable.

Q: Which secondary causes do you look for at the slit lamp?
A: **Pseudoexfoliation** flakes on the pupil and lens. A **Krukenberg spindle** (pigment dispersion), keratic precipitates (uveitis), iris new vessels, signs of trauma.

### Step 4 — Applanation tonometry
**Do:** I explain the test and instil topical anaesthetic and fluorescein. Under cobalt blue light, I turn the dial until the inner edges of the two green semicircles touch. Dial × 10 = mm Hg. I note the time.

**Record:** "Tn by GAT: RE 18 mm Hg, LE 16 mm Hg at 10 am". Central corneal thickness (CCT) is an investigation.

Q: What is the normal IOP, and why is 21 mm Hg the upper limit?
A: **Normal IOP** is 10–21 mm Hg. The mean is 15.5 ± 2.57 mm Hg. About 95% of people lie within 2 standard deviations of the mean; the upper end is about 20.5 mm Hg. An asymmetry of 4 mm Hg or more is significant.

Q: Why measure the pressure before gonioscopy and dilatation?
A: **Gonioscopy** presses on the cornea and lowers the reading. Dilatation raises the pressure by 4–5 mm Hg.

Q: What is the principle of applanation tonometry?
A: **Imbert–Fick law**: pressure = flattening force ÷ flattened area (P = F/A). At 3.06 mm, corneal rigidity and tear capillary attraction cancel out.

Q: How does central corneal thickness affect the reading?
A: **Goldmann applanation tonometry** is calibrated for a mean central corneal thickness of 520 µm. A thinner cornea reads falsely low, a thicker one falsely high, by about 0.7 mm Hg per 10 µm.

### Step 5 — Gonioscopy
**Do:** After tonometry, before dilatation: a Goldmann lens with coupling fluid on the anaesthetised cornea, through all four quadrants. Zeiss lens indentation if narrow.

**Record:** a cross for each eye, with the deepest structure seen in each quadrant.

Q: Why is gonioscopy essential in POAG?
A: **Gonioscopy** proves the angle is open. It excludes angle closure and shows recession, pseudoexfoliation, pigment, PAS or new vessels.

Q: What is the principle of gonioscopy?
A: Light from the angle undergoes **total internal reflection** at the tear–air interface. The gonioscopy lens replaces that interface.

Q: Name the angle structures, and the Shaffer grades of an open angle.
A: From behind forward: **ciliary body band, scleral spur, trabecular meshwork, Schwalbe's line**. Shaffer grades 3 (25–35°) and 4 (35–40°) are open; closure is impossible.

### Step 6 — Dilated disc examination: direct ophthalmoscope, +90 dioptre lens, red-free light
**Do:** Direct ophthalmoscope: red glow at arm's length, then media, disc and macula. Slit lamp: a +90 dioptre (D) lens, then red-free (green) light for nerve fibre layer defects. Indirect ophthalmoscope for the periphery.

**Record:** each eye separately, in this order. Red glow, media. Disc: size, shape, cup shape, margins, colour, CDR, NRR. Vessels, arteriovenous (AV) ratio, PPA, nerve fibre layer defects. Background, macula and foveal reflex, periphery (indirect). "Confirmed with +90 D."

Q: What are the optic nerve head changes in POAG?
A: **Papillary**: enlarged, vertically elongated or asymmetric cup; focal rim narrowing (notch); saucerisation (shallow bowing, not deep excavation). **Peripapillary**: disc haemorrhage, nerve fibre layer defects, peripapillary atrophy. **Vascular**: nasalisation, baring of circumlinear vessels, bayoneting.

Q: What is the best way to examine and to document the disc?
A: **Slit-lamp biomicroscopy** with a Hruby, 60, 78 or 90 D lens. Disc photography is the best documentation.

Q: What do you assess on the disc, qualitatively and quantitatively?
A: **Qualitative**: rim contour, disc haemorrhage, parapapillary atrophy, bared vessels, nerve fibre layer. **Quantitative**: disc size, cup–disc ratio, rim–disc ratio.

### Step 7 — Confrontation fields
**Do:** Face to face, 60 cm apart; his right eye fixes my left, the other eyes closed. A finger comes in from the periphery, midway, in each quadrant.

**Record:** "Fields (confrontation test): full in all quadrants."

Q: Where do glaucomatous field defects begin?
A: In **Bjerrum's area**, 10–25° from fixation, arching from the blind spot. Confrontation is rough, so I confirm with automated perimetry.

Q: How does a glaucomatous field defect differ from a neurological one?
A: **Glaucomatous** defects respect the horizontal meridian: they follow the arcuate nerve fibres, which end at the horizontal raphe. They are relative and incongruous. **Neurological** defects respect the vertical meridian, and are absolute and congruous.

Q: What is the extent of the normal monocular field?
A: **The normal monocular field** reaches about 60° superiorly, 75° inferiorly, 60° nasally and 100° temporally. My own field must be normal for confrontation.

### Reading the Humphrey field
- **Reliability first**: fixation losses < 20%. False positives > 15% (white scotomas) or false negatives > 33% (clover leaf) make it unreliable.
- **Pattern deviation plot**: shows focal defects hidden by general depression from cataract or a small pupil. Whether a defect is glaucomatous (Anderson–Patella criteria): card G10.
- **Indices**: MD is normally within 2 decibels (dB) of 0 and grows more negative as the field worsens. Pattern standard deviation (PSD) shows focal loss.

### Reading the OCT
- **What it measures**: RNFL thickness on a circle around the disc. At the macula, the GCC: RNFL, ganglion cell and inner plexiform layers.
- **Normal**: an hourglass map; a double hump on the TSNIT (temporal–superior–nasal–inferior–temporal) graph. Red means below the 1st percentile.
- **Glaucoma**: inferior and superior thinning first (7 o'clock right eye, 5 o'clock left); the double hump flattens.
- **Advanced disease**: the RNFL reaches a floor; I follow the GCC and 10-2 fields.

**Say:** "I will proceed with pachymetry and diurnal phasing to confirm the true pressure. Then I will stage the damage with Humphrey fields and OCT."

## How I will manage
### Aim
To reach and keep a **target pressure**, at or below which further damage is unlikely. Damage already done cannot be reversed.

### The ladder
1. **I set the target.** TP = IP (1 − IP/100) − Z ± 2 (TP target pressure, IP initial pressure, Z from the stage). Or I lower IOP by 20% (OHT), 30% (mild damage, NTG) or 40% (advanced).
2. **I start one drug**: a PG analogue at bedtime. If unsuitable, a beta-blocker or an alpha-2 agonist.
3. **I review at 2 months.** Not at target: I switch to a better drug before adding one.
4. **I add a second drug**, as a fixed combination where possible.
5. **I offer laser trabeculoplasty** when drops cannot be used reliably: cost, memory, instillation, intolerance.
6. **I advise trabeculectomy with MMC** when any of these applies:
  - not at target on 3 drugs, or fields progressing on maximal tolerated therapy;
  - side effects or poor compliance;
  - baseline IOP over 40 mm Hg, one eye already lost, or difficult follow-up.
7. **I choose a glaucoma drainage device** after failed filtering surgery with antimetabolites, or for a badly scarred conjunctiva.

### Follow-up and counselling
- IOP and compliance 3-monthly (mild to moderate) or monthly (advanced). Fields 6-monthly or 3-monthly.
- Gonioscopy yearly: a thickening lens can add angle closure, needing an iridotomy.
- The damage is permanent, but treatment stops further loss. Drops are for life.
- PG analogue at bedtime, timolol in the morning. After each drop, close the eyes and press the inner corner for 3 minutes.

## What you must know
### Definition
**Glaucoma** is "a chronic progressive optic neuropathy caused by a group of disorders leading to irreversible damage to optic nerves and corresponding visual field defects with or without high intraocular pressure."

**POAG**: "In open-angle glaucoma the iridocorneal angle is open (unobstructed) and normal in appearance but aqueous outflow is diminished." Primary means no other eye disease; adult onset is after 40 years.

In plain words: the nerve is slowly lost behind an open drain that works poorly. Diagnosis rests on the **disc**, **field** and **open angle**.

- **Ocular hypertension**: IOP above normal (often > 21 mm Hg), open angles, no glaucomatous damage. About 1 in 10 develop glaucoma over 10 years.
- **Normal-tension glaucoma**: optic nerve damage without IOP exceeding the normal range (below 22 mm Hg on a diurnal curve).
- **Glaucoma suspect**: a normal white-on-white field, but other risk factors such as disc changes.
- **Target pressure**: the IOP at which further glaucomatous damage is unlikely.
- **Juvenile open-angle glaucoma**: POAG starting at 3–40 years; linked to the myocilin gene.

### Classification
**By the angle**: open-angle or angle-closure, each primary or secondary (secondary open-angle: pseudoexfoliation, pigment dispersion).

**Grading of glaucomatous optic nerve head damage**, with the Z value for the target formula. Stage the patient from his Humphrey MD.

### Pathogenesis
- **Risk factors**: raised IOP; age (prevalence doubles each decade over 40); Black race; first-degree relatives; myopia; thin CCT; diastolic perfusion pressure < 55 mm Hg; diabetes; vasospasm.
- **Outflow**: the meshwork has uveal, corneoscleral and juxtacanalicular parts. The juxtacanalicular part gives most resistance.
- **Mechanical**: raised IOP bows the lamina cribrosa back. Axoplasmic transport is blocked; ganglion cells die by apoptosis.
- **Vascular** (key in NTG): vasospasm, nocturnal hypotension, blood loss.
- **Neurodegenerative**: released glutamate raises intracellular calcium, causing more apoptosis.
- **Poles first**: larger laminar pores. Cupping often starts inferotemporally, giving an upper arcuate scotoma.
- *(extra)* **Genes**: myocilin (chromosome 1), optineurin (chromosome 10).

### Complications
- **Disease**: field loss to a central and a temporal island, then blindness; sudden loss (acute pressure rise, CRVO, snuff-out).
- **Treatment**: surface toxicity, drug side effects, lower success of later surgery, surgical complications.

### Prognosis
- **Good**: IOP kept at target with good compliance; central vision lasts until late. In the young, cupping can reverse once IOP falls.
- **Poor**: advanced damage at presentation (needs a 40% fall); disc haemorrhages; wide pressure swings; poor compliance; pseudoexfoliation.

### Recent advances
- *(extra)* Non-penetrating surgery (deep sclerectomy); Rho kinase inhibitors (under study); neuroprotection (memantine, brimonidine, calcium channel blockers).

## Viva questions
### Why did you ask…?
Q: Why did you ask about night blindness?
A: **Defective dark adaptation** is a feature of open-angle glaucoma. Miotics such as pilocarpine make it worse.

Q: Why did you ask about frequent change of glasses?
A: Constant **high pressure on the zonules and ciliary body** weakens accommodation, so near glasses change often. Field loss is also mistaken for poor vision.

Q: Why did you ask about sudden loss of vision?
A: **Sudden loss** points to an acute pressure rise with corneal oedema, or a **central retinal vein occlusion**. In advanced glaucoma, **snuff-out** after trabeculectomy is another cause.

Q: Why did you ask about steroids?
A: **Steroid-induced glaucoma** mimics POAG. Any form counts: drops, nasal spray, tablets, after transplant or keratoplasty; also Cushing's syndrome.

Q: Why did you ask about asthma, heart and kidney disease and sulpha allergy?
A: They decide the drug. **Beta-blockers** are barred in asthma, COPD, heart failure, heart block and bradycardia. **CAIs** are barred in sulpha allergy, renal failure and chronic liver disease.

Q: Why did you ask about migraine, Raynaud's, blood loss and head injury?
A: **Vasospasm** and haemodynamic crises damage the optic nerve, above all in NTG. Past head injury or shock can mimic NTG.

### Diagnosis and differentials
Q: Which findings make you investigate for open-angle glaucoma?
A: **Raised IOP**. A vertical CDR ≥ 0.7 or asymmetry > 0.2. Rim thinning that breaks the **ISNT rule** (rim thickest inferiorly, then superiorly, nasally, temporally). An RNFL defect in red-free light. A field defect.

Q: How does NTG differ from POAG?
A: **NTG** is a diagnosis of exclusion: IOP stays below 22 mm Hg on diurnal testing, with an open angle. Disc haemorrhages, notches and beta-zone atrophy are commoner.

Q: When will you suspect a lesion other than glaucoma?
A: **Pallor more than cupping**, asymmetric dyschromatopsia, a vertical-meridian field, or an RAPD with mild cupping. Mimics: chiasmal compression; ischaemic, toxic or shock optic neuropathy. I order **neuroimaging**.

Q: Where is aqueous made, how does it leave the eye, and what sets the IOP?
A: The **ciliary epithelium** of the pars plicata makes it. About 90% leaves by the **trabecular meshwork** into Schlemm's canal, 10% by the **uveoscleral** route. IOP depends on aqueous production, outflow resistance and episcleral venous pressure (normally 8–10 mm Hg).

### Investigations
Q: What is diurnal variation, and why do phasing?
A: **IOP** peaks in the morning, falls by day and rises at night, following aqueous production. The normal swing is 3–6 mm Hg; 8 mm Hg or more is significant. Phasing finds the peak and explains progression despite good readings.

Q: How many ganglion cells are lost before a field defect appears?
A: **25–30%** are lost before a defect shows on white-on-white perimetry. So the disc and RNFL often change first.

Q: Which tools detect pre-perimetric glaucoma?
A: **OCT**, scanning laser polarimetry (GDx), Heidelberg retinal tomography, **short-wavelength automated perimetry** and frequency-doubling perimetry.

Q: In what order do glaucomatous field defects appear?
A: **Generalised depression** and baring of the blind spot come first but are non-specific. Then a **paracentral scotoma**, Seidel's scotoma, **arcuate (Bjerrum's) scotoma**, double arcuate (ring) scotoma and nasal (Roenne) step. Last, a central and a temporal island remain.

Q: How will you know that his glaucoma is progressing?
A: **Progression** is judged against a baseline. Glaucoma progression analysis compares each follow-up field with the baseline. A scotoma that deepens or enlarges, or a new one, means progression. On OCT, event analysis flags a change beyond test–retest variability; trend analysis measures the rate of thinning. At the disc, a new haemorrhage or more nasalisation warns of it.

### Management — drugs
Q: How do you calculate the target pressure?
A: **TP = IP (1 − IP/100) − Z ± 2**; Z is 0 suspect, 1 early, 3 moderate, 5 severe, 7 end-stage. For 30 mm Hg, moderate: 30 × 0.7 − 3 = 18, so 16–20 mm Hg.

Q: Why is a prostaglandin analogue first line, and why at bedtime?
A: A **prostaglandin analogue** lowers IOP most, without the systemic effects of beta-blockers. Its effect peaks 10–14 hours later, and bedtime use lessens hyperaemia symptoms.

Q: Why is timolol best given in the morning?
A: **Timolol** cuts aqueous production more in the morning; production falls at night anyway. It also blunts the early rise and avoids hypotension in sleep.

Q: What are the systemic side effects of timolol, and how do you reduce them?
A: **Bradycardia, hypotension, heart failure, bronchospasm** and depression. I advise lacrimal occlusion and closing the eyes for 3 minutes after each drop.

Q: Why is brimonidine avoided in young children?
A: **Brimonidine** crosses the blood–brain barrier. In infants it causes somnolence, hypotension, seizures and apnoea, so it is barred under 2 years.

Q: What are the advantages and disadvantages of fixed combinations?
A: **Fixed combinations** give simpler dosing, better adherence, less preservative, lower cost. But dosing times clash (timolol morning, prostaglandin bedtime), and each drug's benefit is unproven.

Q: How would you treat NTG?
A: I lower IOP by **30%**, including betaxolol for optic nerve blood flow. I treat systemic risks and repeat fields 4–6-monthly.

Q: How would you manage ocular hypertension?
A: **Ocular hypertension** can often be watched carefully, with baseline fields and nerve fibre layer or disc imaging. Treatment is with drops or laser. The risk rises with higher IOP, older age, a larger cup–disc ratio and a CCT below 555 µm.

### Management — laser and surgery
Q: How does laser trabeculoplasty lower IOP?
A: Burns shrink the **trabecular meshwork** and stretch the areas between. Released cytokines (interleukin-1β, tumour necrosis factor-α) induce matrix metalloproteinases that open the meshwork.

Q: Why use mitomycin C in trabeculectomy, and when is it essential?
A: **Mitomycin C** prevents bleb scarring and failure. It is essential after failed trabeculectomy or past conjunctival or cataract surgery. Also in secondary glaucoma, the young, and after 3 years of drops.

Q: Why is a peripheral iridectomy done with trabeculectomy?
A: The **peripheral iridectomy** stops the iris blocking the ostium and prevents pupillary block. It must be larger than the internal ostium.

Q: When will you choose a glaucoma drainage device?
A: A **glaucoma drainage device** suits failed filtering surgery with antimetabolites, or a badly scarred conjunctiva. It also suits neovascular, uveitic and post-keratoplasty glaucoma.

### Complications
Q: What are the complications of mitomycin C?
A: A **cystic, overfiltering bleb** (commonest), late bleb leak, corneal decompensation, scleral or corneal melt, hypotony, endophthalmitis and cataract.

### Recent advances
Q: What is microinvasive glaucoma surgery? *(extra)*
A: **Microinvasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS)** lowers IOP with minimal tissue handling, in mild to moderate glaucoma. Types: Schlemm's canal stents, goniotomy, canal dilatation, suprachoroidal stents.
## Examiner traps
:::trap
- IOP without method, time and CCT, or measured after gonioscopy or dilatation.
- Pupils checked after dilating: an RAPD can no longer be judged.
- "Open angle" without gonioscopy; mixing up Shaffer 4 (wide open) and Scheie 4 (only Schwalbe's line seen).
- "CDR 0.8" alone: give the vertical ratio, rim (ISNT), notch, haemorrhage, vessels, PPA, RNFL.
- Missing PXF at the pupil or lens: it changes the diagnosis and the cataract plan.
- CCT in the clinical table: it is an investigation.
- Pallor more than cupping called glaucoma: it needs neuroimaging.
- Describing the disc alone: start with red glow and media; end with macula, periphery and "+90 D confirmed".
:::

## Short-case version
### Glaucomatous optic disc
**The instruction you may get:** "Examine the fundus of this patient", or "Describe this optic disc."

**Spot diagnosis:** "Glaucomatous optic disc: a vertically enlarged cup with neuroretinal rim thinning, suggestive of glaucomatous optic neuropathy."

**Focused examination:**
1. Pupils for an RAPD, before dilating.
2. Direct ophthalmoscope: red glow at arm's length, then media, disc, vessels and macula close up.
3. Slit lamp, +90 D lens: cup, rim (ISNT), notch, haemorrhage, laminar dots; red-free light for RNFL defects. Indirect ophthalmoscope for the periphery.
4. Describe in the department's order, as below.

:::say
On distant direct ophthalmoscopy at arm's length, the right eye shows a good red glow. Close direct ophthalmoscopy reveals clear media. The optic disc is normal in size and vertically oval. The cup is vertically oval. The margins are well defined. The disc is pale. The cup–disc ratio is 0.9, with circumferential thinning of the neuroretinal rim. The vessels arise centrally and branch dichotomously, with bayoneting and nasal shift. The arteriovenous ratio is 2 to 3. There is peripapillary atrophy. Red-free light shows superior and inferior wedge-shaped nerve fibre layer defects. The background retina and the macula are normal, with a foveal reflex. Indirect ophthalmoscopy shows the peripheries to be normal. Slit-lamp biomicroscopy with a plus 90 dioptre lens confirmed these findings. My diagnosis is right eye glaucomatous optic neuropathy, with a cup–disc ratio of 0.9 and circumferential rim thinning.
:::

**Three signs to show:**
1. Vertical cup–disc ratio **≥ 0.7**, or asymmetry **> 0.2** between the eyes.
2. Rim thinning or a **notch** that breaks the ISNT rule.
3. **Bayoneting** and **nasalisation** of the vessels.

Q: What is the ISNT rule?
A: The normal **neuroretinal rim** is thickest Inferiorly, then Superiorly, Nasally and Temporally. Glaucoma thins the inferior and superior rim first.

Q: Why is the vertical cup–disc ratio more important?
A: Early rim loss is at the **upper and lower poles**, so the cup elongates vertically. A normal cup is horizontally oval.

Q: What is the significance of a disc haemorrhage?
A: A **disc haemorrhage** (Drance haemorrhage) is flame-shaped, usually inferotemporal. It signals active disease and precedes field loss by months; commoner in NTG.

Q: What are bayoneting, nasalisation and baring of circumlinear vessels?
A: **Bayoneting**: double angulation of a vessel. **Nasalisation**: vessels pushed nasally as the cup enlarges. **Baring**: a circumlinear vessel exposed as the rim recedes.

Q: What are the laminar dot sign and beta-zone atrophy?
A: **Laminar dots** are lamina cribrosa pores seen in an advanced cup. **Beta-zone atrophy** lies next to the disc, with sclera visible; the outer alpha zone is irregularly pigmented.

Q: How do you tell a large physiological cup from a glaucomatous cup?
A: A **physiological cup** (the atrophied Bergmeister's papilla) is round, with a rim obeying ISNT. A glaucomatous cup is vertically elongated, with notching, haemorrhage or an RNFL defect.

He has no asthma, heart disease, sulpha allergy, migraine, Raynaud's phenomenon, stroke or head injury. He has never used steroids. He has used bimatoprost drops at bedtime in both eyes regularly for two years. He does not smoke or drink alcohol. There is no family history of glaucoma.

:::say
To summarise, my patient is a 70-year-old man on bimatoprost drops for glaucoma in both eyes for two years. He has gradual, painless, progressive defective vision, with no haloes, trauma or steroid use. Visual acuity is 6/18 in the right eye and 6/9 in the left. Intraocular pressure by Goldmann applanation tonometry is 18 and 16 millimetres of mercury at ten in the morning. ## Quick recall
:::recall
- Normal IOP **10–21 mm Hg**; asymmetry **≥ 4 mm Hg** and a diurnal swing **≥ 8 mm Hg** are significant.
- Order: acuity → pupils → slit lamp → tonometry → gonioscopy → dilate → disc → fields.
- GAT is calibrated for a CCT of **520 µm**. Thinner reads low, thicker high: about 0.7 mm Hg per 10 µm.
- Vertical CDR **≥ 0.7** or asymmetry **> 0.2**: suspect glaucoma.
- **25–30%** of ganglion cells are lost before a white-on-white field defect.
- OCT: RNFL thins first at **7 o'clock RE, 5 o'clock LE**; eyes differ by **> 9 µm**.
- **TP = IP (1 − IP/100) − Z ± 2**; Z = 0, 1, 3, 5, 7.
- PG analogue first, at bedtime; timolol in the morning.

**Mnemonic:** **PART B** before dilating: Pupillary reflex, Angle, Rubeosis, Tension, BCVA (best-corrected visual acuity).
:::
