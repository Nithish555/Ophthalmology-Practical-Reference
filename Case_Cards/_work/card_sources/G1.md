## At a glance
- **Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG)** is a chronic, progressive optic neuropathy with an open, normal-looking angle and reduced aqueous outflow. It is usually silent and is found on routine examination or at follow-up.
- It is kept often because its signs are stable: a cupped disc, a measurable pressure and an open angle. The **glaucomatous optic disc** is also a short case.
- Examiners test the history with its reasons, the examination in order, the disc description, target pressure and the treatment ladder.

**Keywords the examiner listens for:** Goldmann applanation tonometry · central corneal thickness · Van Herick · relative afferent pupillary defect · gonioscopy · vertical cup–disc ratio · ISNT rule · neuroretinal rim · bayoneting · disc haemorrhage · retinal nerve fibre layer · Humphrey · target pressure · prostaglandin analogue · trabeculectomy

## History — positive and negative, with reasons
**Chief complaint:** "Mr X, a 70-year-old man, has come for regular follow-up of glaucoma." Or: "…came with complaints of defective vision in both eyes for 1 year."

**History of present illness (house wording):** "Patient was apparently normal ___ years before, after which he had defective vision in both eyes, gradual in onset, progressive in nature, not associated with pain or headache. ## Examination — must-do steps and their viva
### General and systemic (this case only)
- **Pulse and blood pressure**: bradycardia or heart block bars beta-blockers. A diastolic perfusion pressure below 55 mm Hg is a risk factor.
- **Chest**: wheeze from asthma or COPD bars beta-blockers.
- **Face and skin**: port-wine haemangioma (Sturge–Weber syndrome), neurofibromatosis, carotid–cavernous fistula and thyroid eye signs raise episcleral venous pressure. Petechiae or purpura suggest a bleeding disorder.
- **Nervous system**: examine with care, because a neurological lesion can mimic glaucomatous damage.

### Step 1 — Visual acuity, pinhole and near vision
**Do:** I test each eye unaided, with pinhole and with his glasses, then near vision at 33 cm. I also test colour vision.

**Record:** "RE 6/18, PH 6/12; LE 6/9; N6 at 33 cm both eyes."

Q: Why is central vision kept until late in glaucoma?
A: **Central vision** fibres arise closest to the disc, lie most superficially and leave by its central part. The arcuate fibres at the poles are damaged first.

Q: Why test colour vision in a glaucoma patient?
A: **Asymmetric dyschromatopsia** suggests another optic neuropathy, not glaucoma. It is an indication for neuroimaging.

Q: Why note myopia?
A: **Myopia** raises the risk of POAG and stretches the disc into a more oval shape. Low scleral rigidity also makes IOP readings faulty.

### Step 2 — Pupils, for a relative afferent pupillary defect (before any drop)
**Do:** In dim light, I check the direct and consensual reflexes of each eye. Then I do the swinging torch light test.

**Record:** "RTL 3 mm, no RAPD", or "RE: RAPD present".

Q: Why must the pupils be checked before any drop?
A: A **relative afferent pupillary defect** cannot be judged in a pupil made small or large by drugs. So pupils come before dilatation, as in the mnemonic PART B.

Q: What does an RAPD mean in a glaucoma patient?
A: It means **advanced, asymmetric optic nerve damage**. An RAPD with only mild cupping does not fit glaucoma, so I would order neuroimaging.

### Step 3 — Slit-lamp examination of the anterior segment, with Van Herick grading
**Do:** I examine from lids to lens. For Van Herick, I place a thin slit 1 mm inside the temporal limbus at 60°. I compare the peripheral AC depth with the corneal thickness.

**Record:** "AC: VH grade IV (ND). Iris CPN. Lens PCIOL. No PXF, no Krukenberg spindle."

Q: How do you grade the anterior chamber by Van Herick?
A: **Van Herick** compares peripheral AC depth with corneal thickness: grade 4 ≥ 1; grade 3 ¼–½; grade 2 = ¼; grade 1 < ¼; grade 0 iridocorneal contact. Below ¼ the angle may be occludable.

Q: What signs of long-term drops do you look for?
A: **Prostaglandin analogue** use pigments the lid margin and lengthens the lashes. Any drop can congest the inferior conjunctiva and cause corneal surface toxicity, seen with fluorescein.

Q: Which secondary causes do you look for at the slit lamp?
A: **Pseudoexfoliation** flakes on the pupil margin and lens, a **Krukenberg spindle** (pigment dispersion), keratic precipitates (uveitis), new vessels on the iris, and signs of trauma.

### Step 4 — Applanation tonometry
**Do:** I explain the test, then instil a topical anaesthetic and fluorescein. Under cobalt blue light, I turn the dial until the inner edges of the two green semicircles touch. The dial reading × 10 gives mm Hg. I note the time.

**Record:** "Tn by GAT: RE 18 mm Hg, LE 16 mm Hg at 10 am." Central corneal thickness (CCT) goes under investigations.

Q: Why measure the pressure before gonioscopy and dilatation?
A: **Gonioscopy** presses on the cornea and lowers the reading. Dilatation raises the pressure by 4–5 mm Hg. Both give a false value.

Q: What is the principle of applanation tonometry?
A: **Imbert–Fick law**: pressure equals the flattening force divided by the flattened area (P = F/A). At a diameter of 3.06 mm, corneal rigidity and tear capillary attraction cancel out.

Q: How does central corneal thickness affect the reading?
A: **Goldmann applanation tonometry** is accurate at a central corneal thickness of 520 µm. Thin corneas read falsely low and thick ones falsely high, by about 0.7 mm Hg per 10 µm.

Q: Why is 21 mm Hg taken as the upper limit of normal?
A: **Mean IOP** is 15.5 ± 2.57 mm Hg, and the mean plus two standard deviations is about 20.5. So normal is 10–21 mm Hg, a rough statistical limit.

### Step 5 — Gonioscopy
**Do:** After tonometry and before dilatation, I place a Goldmann gonioscopy lens with coupling fluid on the anaesthetised cornea. I rotate it to see all four quadrants. I indent with a Zeiss four-mirror lens if the angle is narrow.

**Record:** a cross for each eye, with the deepest structure seen in each quadrant.

Q: Why is gonioscopy essential in POAG?
A: **Gonioscopy** proves the angle is open. It excludes angle closure and shows secondary causes: angle recession, pseudoexfoliation, pigment dispersion, PAS and new vessels.

Q: What is the principle of gonioscopy?
A: Light from the angle undergoes **total internal reflection** at the tear–air interface. The gonioscopy lens replaces that interface, so the angle can be seen.

Q: Name the angle structures, and the Shaffer grades of an open angle.
A: From behind forward: **ciliary body band, scleral spur, trabecular meshwork, Schwalbe's line**. Shaffer grades 3 (25–35°) and 4 (35–40°) are open; closure is impossible.

Q: Why repeat gonioscopy every year?
A: With age the **lens thickens**, and an angle-closure element can develop. It may then need a peripheral iridotomy.

### Step 6 — Dilated disc examination: direct ophthalmoscope, +90 D lens, red-free light
**Do:** After dilatation, I look for the red glow with the direct ophthalmoscope at arm's length. Then I examine the media and disc close up. At the slit lamp I use a +90 dioptre (D) lens, then red-free (green) light.

**Record:** "Media clear; disc vertically oval, CDR 0.8, NRR thinning (superior, inferior), nasalisation +, bayoneting +, PPA +."

Q: What is the best way to examine and to document the disc?
A: **Slit-lamp biomicroscopy** with a Hruby lens or a 60, 78 or 90 D lens is the best examination. Disc photography is the best documentation.

Q: Why use red-free light?
A: **Retinal nerve fibre layer** defects, focal wedges or diffuse loss, are best seen through a green (red-free) filter.

Q: What are the qualitative and quantitative features of the disc in glaucoma?
A: **Qualitative**: rim contour, disc haemorrhage, parapapillary atrophy, bared circumlinear vessels and the nerve fibre layer. **Quantitative**: disc size (vertical diameter), cup–disc ratio and rim–disc ratio.

### Step 7 — Confrontation fields
**Do:** We sit face to face, 60 cm apart. His right eye fixes my left eye; his left and my right eyes are closed. I bring a finger in from the periphery, midway between us, in each quadrant, and compare his field with mine.

**Record:** "Fields (confrontation test): full in all quadrants."

Q: Where do glaucomatous field defects begin?
A: In **Bjerrum's area**, 10–25° from fixation, arching from the blind spot. Confrontation is rough, so I confirm with automated perimetry.

Q: How does a glaucomatous field defect differ from a neurological one?
A: **Glaucomatous** defects respect the horizontal meridian and are relative and incongruous. **Neurological** defects respect the vertical meridian and are absolute and mostly congruous.

Q: What is the normal extent of the field?
A: In one eye, about **60° superiorly, 75° inferiorly, 60° nasally and 100° temporally**. For confrontation, the examiner's own field must be normal.

### Reading the Humphrey field
- **Reliability first**: fixation losses < 20%. False positives > 15% (white scotomas) or false negatives > 33% (clover-leaf pattern) make the test unreliable.
- **Test conditions**: correct age and refraction, pupil > 2.5 mm, Goldmann size III white stimulus.
- **Pattern deviation plot**: it shows focal defects hidden by general depression from cataract or a small pupil.
- **Indices**: glaucoma hemifield test; MD, more negative as the field worsens; pattern standard deviation (PSD) for focal loss.

### Reading the OCT
- OCT measures RNFL thickness on a circle around the disc, from the internal limiting membrane to the outer edge of the RNFL.
- At the macula it measures the **ganglion cell complex**: the RNFL, ganglion cell layer and inner plexiform layer.
- **Normal**: an hourglass thickness map and a double hump on the TSNIT (temporal–superior–nasal–inferior–temporal) graph. Green is normal, yellow borderline, red below the 1st percentile.
- **Glaucoma**: inferior and superior thinning first, at 7 o'clock in the right eye and 5 o'clock in the left.
- **Pitfalls**: red disease (the thin RNFL of a high myope flagged red); green disease (a normal average hiding asymmetry); averaging and shadow artefacts. Different machines cannot be compared.
- **Advanced disease**: the RNFL reaches a floor, so I follow the macular GCC and a 10-2 field.

**Say:** "I will proceed with pachymetry and diurnal phasing to confirm the true pressure. Then I will stage the damage with Humphrey 24-2 fields and OCT, with disc photographs as a baseline."

## How I will manage
### Aim
To reach and keep a **target pressure**, at or below which further damage is unlikely. It depends on the initial pressure, the stage and the speed of progression. Damage already done cannot be reversed.

### The ladder
1. **Set the target.** TP = IP (1 − IP/100) − Z ± 2 (TP target pressure, IP initial pressure, Z from the stage). Or lower IOP by 20% (OHT), 30% (mild damage, NTG) or 40% (advanced damage).
2. **Start one drug**: a prostaglandin analogue at bedtime. If it is unsuitable, a beta-blocker or an alpha-2 agonist.
3. **Review at 2 months.** At target, I continue. Not at target, I switch to a better drug before adding one.
4. **Add a second drug**, preferably as a fixed combination, when one drug is not enough.
5. **Laser trabeculoplasty** when drops cannot be used reliably: cost, memory, difficulty instilling or intolerance.
6. **Trabeculectomy with MMC** when any of these is present:
  - IOP not at target on 3 drugs, or fields progressing on maximal tolerated therapy;
  - side effects or poor compliance;
  - baseline IOP over 40 mm Hg, one eye already lost, or difficult follow-up.
7. **Glaucoma drainage device** after failed filtering surgery with antimetabolites, or when the conjunctiva is badly scarred.

Medical treatment is safe, but it lowers IOP less than surgery, costs more, wanes with time and lowers later surgical success.

**Cataract with glaucoma.** Expect a miotic pupil, posterior synechiae, weak zonules in PXF, bleeding from a congested eye, a scarred conjunctiva or bleb, and a pressure rise after surgery.

- Its risks: an early pressure spike, uncertain long-term control, and a later trabeculectomy more prone to fail.
- **Combined surgery** when the triggers in the table are present.

### Follow-up and counselling
- IOP by GAT, compliance and tolerance: every 3 months in mild to moderate disease, monthly in advanced disease.
- Humphrey fields: yearly in high-risk OHT, 6-monthly in mild to moderate disease, 3-monthly in advanced disease. Gonioscopy yearly.
- Counselling: the damage is permanent, but treatment stops further loss. Drops are for life, so compliance is checked at every visit.
- Drop technique: PG analogue at bedtime, timolol in the morning. After each drop, close the eyes and press the inner corner for 3 minutes.
- Examine his first-degree relatives.

## What you must know
### Definition
**Glaucoma** is "a chronic progressive optic neuropathy caused by a group of disorders leading to irreversible damage to optic nerves and corresponding visual field defects with or without high intraocular pressure."

**POAG**: "In open-angle glaucoma the iridocorneal angle is open (unobstructed) and normal in appearance but aqueous outflow is diminished." Primary means no other associated eye disease. Adult-onset POAG starts after 40 years.

In plain words: the optic nerve is slowly lost behind an open drain that looks normal but works poorly. The diagnosis rests on the **disc**, the **field** and the **open angle**.

- **Ocular hypertension**: IOP above normal (often above 21 mm Hg) with open angles and no detectable glaucomatous damage. About 1 in 10 develop glaucoma over 10 years.
- **Normal-tension glaucoma**: optic nerve damage without the IOP exceeding the normal range; on a diurnal curve IOP stays below 22 mm Hg.
- **Glaucoma suspect**: a normal white-on-white field, but other risk factors such as suspicious disc changes. I observe if risk is low and review at 2 months.
- **Target pressure**: the IOP at which further glaucomatous optic neuropathy is unlikely.
- **Juvenile open-angle glaucoma**: POAG starting between 3 and 40 years; linked to the myocilin gene.

### Classification
**By the angle**: open-angle or angle-closure glaucoma, each primary or secondary (secondary open-angle: pseudoexfoliation, pigment dispersion). POAG is adult-onset or juvenile-onset.

**Types of disc damage**: type I focal ischaemic, type II myopic glaucomatous, type III senile sclerotic, type IV concentrically enlarging, and mixed.

### Pathogenesis
- **Risk factors**: raised IOP; age (prevalence roughly doubles each decade over 40); Black race; first-degree relatives; myopia; thin CCT; diastolic perfusion pressure < 55 mm Hg; diabetes, hypertension, thyroid disease; vasospasm.
- **Outflow resistance**: the juxtacanalicular meshwork gives most resistance. Glycosaminoglycan deposits also block the collector channels.
- **Mechanical theory**: raised IOP bows the lamina cribrosa backwards. Axoplasmic transport is blocked, and ganglion cells die by apoptosis.
- **Vascular theory** (key in NTG): vasospasm, nocturnal hypotension, blood loss and poor perfusion.
- **Neurodegeneration**: damaged cells release glutamate. It raises intracellular calcium and causes more apoptosis, a vicious cycle.
- **Poles first**: the poles have larger laminar pores and are watershed zones. Cupping often starts inferotemporally, giving an early upper arcuate scotoma.
- **Genes**: myocilin (chromosome 1), optineurin (chromosome 10).

### Complications
- **Of the disease**: progressive field loss to a central and a temporal island, then blindness. Sudden loss from an acute pressure rise, CRVO or snuff-out after surgery.
- **Of treatment**: ocular surface toxicity and drug side effects; lower success of later surgery; surgical complications (table above).

### Prognosis
- **Good**: IOP kept at target with good compliance; central vision lasts until late. In children and young patients, cupping can reverse after the pressure falls.
- **Poor**: advanced damage at presentation; disc haemorrhages; thin CCT; wide pressure swings; poor compliance; pseudoexfoliation (higher IOP, wider swings, more field loss).

### Recent advances
- *(extra)* **Microinvasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS)** and **non-penetrating surgery** (deep sclerectomy, viscocanalostomy).
- *(extra)* **Neuroprotection**: memantine, brimonidine, calcium channel blockers. **Rho kinase (ROCK) inhibitors** raise outflow; still under study.
- *(extra)* **Pressure measurement**: corneal hysteresis by the ocular response analyser; dynamic contour tonometry, independent of CCT.
- *(extra)* **Glaucoma progression analysis** software for fields and OCT.

## Viva questions
### Why did you ask…?
Q: Why did you ask about night blindness?
A: **Defective dark adaptation** is a feature of open-angle glaucoma. Miotics such as pilocarpine make night blindness worse.

Q: Why did you ask about frequent change of glasses?
A: Constant **high pressure on the zonules and ciliary body** weakens accommodation, so near glasses change often. Patients also mistake field loss for poor vision.

Q: Why did you ask about sudden loss of vision?
A: In a glaucoma patient it suggests an **acute pressure rise** with corneal oedema, a **central retinal vein occlusion**, or **snuff-out** after trabeculectomy in advanced glaucoma.

Q: Why did you ask about steroids, and in what forms?
A: **Steroid-induced glaucoma** mimics POAG. I ask about drops, nasal sprays and tablets, steroids after transplant or keratoplasty, and Cushing's syndrome.

Q: Why did you ask about asthma, heart disease, kidney disease and sulpha allergy?
A: They decide the drug. **Beta-blockers** are barred in asthma, COPD, heart failure, heart block and bradycardia. **CAIs** are barred in sulpha allergy, renal failure and chronic liver disease.

Q: Why did you ask about migraine, Raynaud's, blood loss and head injury?
A: **Vasospasm** and haemodynamic crises damage the optic nerve, above all in NTG. A past head injury or shock can mimic NTG.

### Diagnosis and differentials
Q: Which findings make you investigate for open-angle glaucoma?
A: **Raised IOP**; a **vertical cup–disc ratio** ≥ 0.7 or asymmetry > 0.2; rim thinning that breaks the ISNT rule; an RNFL defect in red-free light; a glaucomatous field defect; risk factors.

Q: How does NTG differ from POAG?
A: **NTG** is a diagnosis of exclusion: IOP stays below 22 mm Hg on diurnal testing, with an open angle. Disc haemorrhages, focal notches and beta-zone atrophy are commoner, and defects lie closer to fixation.

Q: When will you suspect a lesion other than glaucoma?
A: **Pallor more than cupping**, asymmetric dyschromatopsia, a field respecting the vertical meridian, rapid progression, or an RAPD with mild cupping. I would order **neuroimaging**.

Q: Which other conditions can mimic POAG?
A: **Chiasmal compression**, arteritic and non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy, toxic optic neuropathy, and shock optic neuropathy after severe hypotension.

Q: Where is aqueous made, and how does it leave the eye?
A: The **ciliary epithelium** of the pars plicata makes it. About 90% leaves by the **trabecular meshwork** into Schlemm's canal, and 10% by the **uveoscleral** route.

Q: What determines IOP, and where is the main resistance in POAG?
A: **Aqueous production, outflow resistance and episcleral venous pressure** (normally 8–10 mm Hg). The **juxtacanalicular meshwork** gives the most resistance, then Schlemm's canal.

### Investigations
Q: What is diurnal variation, and why do phasing?
A: **IOP** is highest in the morning, falls through the day and rises at night; the normal swing is 3–6 mm Hg. Phasing finds the peak, explains progression despite good readings, and times the drops.

Q: How many ganglion cells are lost before a field defect appears?
A: **25–30%** of ganglion cells are lost before a defect shows on white-on-white perimetry. So the disc and RNFL often change first.

Q: Which tools detect pre-perimetric glaucoma?
A: **OCT**, scanning laser polarimetry (GDx), Heidelberg retinal tomography, **short-wavelength automated perimetry (SWAP)**, which is blue-on-yellow, and frequency-doubling perimetry.

Q: What are the Anderson–Patella criteria?
A: On two consecutive reliable fields: a cluster of ≥ 3 contiguous non-edge points on the pattern deviation plot at p < 5%, one at p < 1%; **PSD** p < 5%; **glaucoma hemifield test** outside normal limits.

Q: In what order do glaucomatous field defects appear?
A: **Paracentral scotoma**, then Seidel's scotoma, **arcuate (Bjerrum's) scotoma**, double arcuate (ring) scotoma and a nasal (Roenne) step. Last, only a central and a temporal island remain.

### Management — drugs
Q: How do you calculate the target pressure?
A: **TP = IP (1 − IP/100) − Z ± 2**, with Z 0 for a suspect, 1 early, 3 moderate, 5 severe, 7 end-stage. For 30 mm Hg and moderate damage: 30 × 0.7 − 3 = 18, so 16–20 mm Hg.

Q: Why is a prostaglandin analogue first line, and why at bedtime?
A: A **prostaglandin analogue** lowers IOP most (25–32%), without the systemic effects of beta-blockers. Its effect peaks 10–14 hours later. Bedtime use also lessens hyperaemia symptoms.

Q: Why is timolol best given in the morning?
A: **Timolol** cuts aqueous production more in the morning; production falls naturally at night. A morning dose blunts the early-morning rise and avoids hypotension in sleep.

Q: What are the systemic side effects of timolol, and how do you reduce them?
A: **Bradycardia, hypotension, heart failure, bronchospasm** and depression. I advise lacrimal occlusion and closing the eyes for 3 minutes after each drop.

Q: Why is brimonidine avoided in young children?
A: **Brimonidine** crosses the blood–brain barrier. In infants it can cause somnolence, hypotension, seizures and apnoea, so it is contraindicated under 2 years.

Q: What are the advantages and disadvantages of fixed combinations?
A: **Fixed combinations** give simpler dosing, better adherence, less preservative and systemic toxicity, and lower cost. But dosing times clash (timolol morning, prostaglandin bedtime), and each drug's own benefit is unproven.

Q: How would you treat NTG?
A: Lower IOP by **30%**, with betaxolol in the regimen, as it may help optic nerve blood flow. I treat systemic risks and repeat fields every 4–6 months.

### Management — laser and surgery
Q: How does laser trabeculoplasty lower IOP?
A: Burns shrink the **trabecular meshwork** and stretch the areas between. Released interleukin-1β and tumour necrosis factor-α induce matrix metalloproteinases, which open the meshwork.

Q: Why is mitomycin C used in trabeculectomy, and when is it essential?
A: **Mitomycin C** prevents excess scarring, so the bleb is less likely to fail. It is essential after failed trabeculectomy, conjunctival or cataract surgery, in secondary glaucoma, in young or dark-skinned patients, and after 3 years of drops.

Q: Why is a peripheral iridectomy done with trabeculectomy?
A: It stops the **peripheral iris blocking the ostium**, and it prevents pupillary block. It must be larger than the internal ostium.

Q: Why place a trabeculectomy superotemporally or superonasally, not at 12 o'clock?
A: This keeps **other sites free for repeat surgery**. At 12 o'clock, the anterior ciliary vessel, 3–4 mm from the limbus, may bleed during sclerectomy.

Q: When will you choose a glaucoma drainage device over trabeculectomy?
A: A **glaucoma drainage device** is for failed filtering surgery with antimetabolites and a badly scarred conjunctiva. It also suits refractory glaucomas: neovascular, uveitic, post-keratoplasty and aniridia.

Q: When will you advise cataract surgery alone in a glaucoma patient?
A: When **IOP is controlled on 1–2 drugs**, with no significant disc or field damage. It is one easier procedure, but expect an early pressure spike.

### Complications
Q: What are the complications of mitomycin C?
A: A **cystic, overfiltering bleb** (commonest), a late leaking bleb, corneal decompensation, scleral or corneal melt, hypotony, endophthalmitis and cataract.

Q: How does long-term medical treatment affect later surgery?
A: **Prolonged drops** reduce the success of filtering surgery. After more than 3 years of drops, mitomycin C becomes essential at trabeculectomy.

### Recent advances
Q: What is microinvasive glaucoma surgery? *(extra)*
A: **MIGS** lowers IOP with minimal tissue handling, for mild to moderate glaucoma: Schlemm's canal stents, goniotomy, canal dilatation and suprachoroidal stents.

Q: What is non-penetrating glaucoma surgery? *(extra)*
A: **Deep sclerectomy** removes the roof of Schlemm's canal under a scleral flap without entering the AC, so hypotony is less. **Viscocanalostomy** dilates the canal with viscoelastic.

Q: What is neuroprotection? *(extra)*
A: **Neuroprotection** aims to save ganglion cells apart from lowering IOP. Memantine blocks the glutamate (N-methyl-D-aspartate) receptor; others are brimonidine and calcium channel blockers.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- IOP without method, time and CCT, or measured after gonioscopy or dilatation.
- Pupils checked after dilating: an RAPD can no longer be judged.
- "Open angle" without gonioscopy; mixing up Scheie and Shaffer (Shaffer 4 is wide open; Scheie 4 shows only Schwalbe's line).
- "CDR 0.8" alone: give the vertical ratio, rim (ISNT), notch, haemorrhage, vessels, PPA and RNFL.
- Missing PXF at the pupil margin or lens: it changes the diagnosis and the cataract plan.
- CCT in the clinical table: it is an investigation.
- Pallor more than cupping called glaucoma: it needs neuroimaging.
- A beta-blocker for an asthmatic, or a CAI despite sulpha allergy.
:::

## Short-case version
### Glaucomatous optic disc
**The instruction you may get:** "Examine the fundus of this patient", or "Describe this optic disc."

**Spot diagnosis:** "Glaucomatous optic disc: a vertically enlarged cup with neuroretinal rim thinning, suggestive of glaucomatous optic neuropathy."

**Focused examination:**
1. Pupils for an RAPD, before dilating.
2. Direct ophthalmoscope at arm's length for the red glow; then close, for the media and disc.
3. Slit lamp with a +90 D lens: cup, rim against the ISNT rule, notch, haemorrhage, laminar dots.
4. Red-free light for RNFL defects.
5. Describe in the slide order: size, disc shape, cup shape, margins, colour, cup–disc ratio, rim, vessels, arteriovenous ratio, peripapillary changes, RNFL defects.

:::say
The right optic disc is normal in size and vertically oval. The cup is vertically oval. The margins are well defined. The disc is pale. The cup–disc ratio is 0.9, with circumferential thinning of the neuroretinal rim. The vessels arise centrally and branch dichotomously, with bayoneting and nasal shift. The arteriovenous ratio is 2 to 3. There is peripapillary atrophy. Red-free light shows superior and inferior wedge-shaped nerve fibre layer defects. My diagnosis is right eye glaucomatous optic neuropathy, with a cup–disc ratio of 0.9 and circumferential rim thinning.
:::

**Three signs to show:**
1. Vertical cup–disc ratio **≥ 0.7**, or asymmetry **> 0.2** between the eyes.
2. Rim thinning or a **notch** that breaks the ISNT rule.
3. **Bayoneting** and **nasalisation** of the vessels.

Q: What is the ISNT rule?
A: The normal **neuroretinal rim** is thickest Inferiorly, then Superiorly, then Nasally, and thinnest Temporally. Glaucoma breaks the rule, thinning the inferior and superior rim first.

Q: Why is the vertical cup–disc ratio more important?
A: Early rim loss is at the **upper and lower poles**, so the cup elongates vertically. A normal cup is horizontally oval, so its horizontal ratio is the larger.

Q: What is the significance of a disc haemorrhage?
A: A **disc haemorrhage** (Drance haemorrhage) is a flame-shaped bleed, most often inferotemporal. It signals active disease, precedes field loss by months and predicts progression. It is commoner in NTG.

Q: What are bayoneting, nasalisation and baring of circumlinear vessels?
A: **Bayoneting** is double angulation of a vessel. **Nasalisation** is nasal displacement of vessels as the cup enlarges. **Baring** is a circumlinear vessel left exposed as the rim recedes.

Q: What are the laminar dot sign and beta-zone atrophy?
A: **Laminar dots** are pores of the lamina cribrosa seen in an advanced cup. **Beta-zone atrophy** lies next to the disc margin, with sclera visible; the outer alpha zone is irregularly pigmented.

Q: How do you tell a large physiological cup from a glaucomatous cup?
A: A **physiological cup** (the atrophied Bergmeister's papilla) is round, with a rim that obeys ISNT. A glaucomatous cup is vertically elongated, with notching, haemorrhage or an RNFL defect.

## Quick recall
:::recall
- Normal IOP **10–21 mm Hg**; asymmetry **≥ 4 mm Hg** and a diurnal swing **≥ 8 mm Hg** are significant.
- Order: pupils (RAPD) → slit lamp → tonometry → gonioscopy → dilate → disc → fields.
- GAT is accurate at a CCT of **520 µm**; thin < 500 µm reads low, thick > 570 µm reads high.
- Vertical CDR **≥ 0.7** or asymmetry **> 0.2**: suspect glaucoma.
- **ISNT**: rim thickest Inferiorly, then Superiorly, Nasally, Temporally.
- **25–30%** of ganglion cells are lost before a white-on-white field defect.
- Bjerrum's area **10–25°**; glaucoma defects respect the horizontal meridian.
- OCT: superior and inferior RNFL first; **7 o'clock RE, 5 o'clock LE**; eyes differ by **> 9 µm**.
- **TP = IP (1 − IP/100) − Z ± 2**; Z = 0, 1, 3, 5, 7. Or lower IOP by 20%, 30% or 40%.
- PG analogue first, at bedtime; timolol in the morning.
- Trabeculectomy: not at target on **3 drugs**, progression, baseline **> 40 mm Hg**, one eye lost.

**Mnemonic:** **PART B** before dilating: Pupillary reflex, Angle, Rubeosis, Tension, BCVA (best-corrected visual acuity).
:::
