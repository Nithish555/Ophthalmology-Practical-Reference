## Buphthalmos (primary congenital glaucoma)
**Definition:** **Buphthalmos** is "a term that is applied to congenital glaucomas with enlargement of globe that appear during the first 3 years of life". In plain words, raised pressure stretches the soft infant eye, so the cornea and globe enlarge ("ox-eye"). The main cause is **primary congenital glaucoma**, from a malformed angle (**isolated trabeculodysgenesis**).

### Signs to show
1. **Observe first.** The classic triad is **epiphora** (watering), **photophobia** and **blepharospasm**. Shine a torch: the child screws the eyes shut.
2. **Globe and sclera.** The eyes are large, bilateral in 65–80%. The sclera looks **bluish** because it is stretched.
3. **Cornea.** The horizontal **corneal diameter** is over 12 mm, and the cornea is hazy from oedema. **Haab's striae** are horizontal breaks in Descemet's membrane. The oedema clears with treatment; the striae stay for life.
4. **Anterior chamber** deep; **iris** normal or with loss of crypts; **lens** normal.
5. **Examination under anaesthesia (EUA)** gives the rest, in both eyes:

@widths 20 27 23 30
| What I measure | How | Normal infant | Suggests congenital glaucoma |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intraocular pressure (IOP) | Perkins hand-held applanation tonometer or Tonopen | 11–14 mm Hg | Over 14 mm Hg, or over 5 mm Hg difference between the eyes |
| Horizontal corneal diameter | Both eyes, compared | 10–10.5 mm; grows 0.5–1.0 mm in the first year | Over 12 mm |
| Angle | Direct gonioscopy, infant Koeppe lens, child supine | No vessels in the angle | High iris insertion; compacted meshwork; **Loch Ness monster** vessels; **Lister's morning mist** |
| Optic disc | Ophthalmoscopy | Small physiological cup; pink disc | Cup–disc ratio over 0.3, or asymmetry between the eyes |
| Refraction | Cycloplegic refraction | — | A **myopic shift** as the globe enlarges |

Most general anaesthetics lower IOP, but ketamine and suxamethonium raise it.

### Diagnosis line
- "A 6-month-old boy with **primary congenital glaucoma (infantile type) in both eyes**, with buphthalmos, corneal oedema and Haab's striae."

### How to tell it apart
@widths 28 72
| Condition | How to tell apart |
|---|---|
| **Megalocornea** | Non-progressive, symmetric enlargement over 12 mm, with normal corneal thickness and contour. Buphthalmos adds raised IOP, a cloudy cornea, Haab's striae and cupping. |
| **Congenital hereditary endothelial dystrophy (CHED)** | The cornea is two to three times thicker but not enlarged, with symmetric clouding and no Descemet breaks. The eye is white, without photophobia; IOP is normal. |
| **Birth trauma (forceps injury)** | Descemet tears are **vertical or oblique**. Oedema appears at once and clears by itself. Corneal size and IOP are normal, and there is no photophobia. |

### How I will manage
1. **Confirm under anaesthesia, and examine the siblings.** About 10% of cases are inherited, autosomal recessive, in the **CYP1B1** gene.
2. **Drops only bridge the gap; treatment is surgical.** Timolol, a beta-blocker, reduces aqueous production; avoid it in asthma and bradycardia. A carbonic anhydrase inhibitor also reduces production; avoid it in sulpha allergy. A prostaglandin analogue increases uveoscleral outflow. I give **no alpha-2 agonists under 2 years**: they cause respiratory depression, coma, hypotension and bradycardia.
3. **Goniotomy if the cornea is clear.** Under a direct goniolens, a Swan knife cuts the anterior meshwork just behind Schwalbe's line, over about 120°. Advantage: aqueous flows straight into Schlemm's canal. Disadvantage: it needs a clear cornea; risks are hyphaema, iridodialysis and a shallow chamber.
4. **Trabeculotomy if the cornea is hazy.** Ab externo, I find Schlemm's canal where blue cornea meets white sclera. I deroof it and rotate the probe into the anterior chamber, on both sides. Advantage: no view of the angle is needed. Disadvantage: if it fails, filtering surgery follows. Next come combined trabeculotomy–trabeculectomy, a drainage device, and cyclodestruction for refractory eyes.
5. **Follow up for life and counsel the parents.** I repeat the EUA, and treat refractive error, amblyopia, squint and corneal scarring (penetrating keratoplasty). A second child is affected in about 3%, and up to 25% if two already are. Full profiles: card GX.

### Viva
Q: What does "buphthalmos" mean, and how common is it?
A: **Buphthalmos** means "ox-eyed": enlarged eyes with raised IOP in the first 3 years. It affects 1 in 3,300 live births in India and 1 in 10,000 in the West. It is bilateral in 65–80%, and 65% are boys. About 90% are sporadic.
Q: How is primary congenital glaucoma classified?
A: By when the IOP rises. **Newborn (true congenital)**: in intrauterine life (25%). **Infantile**: before 3 years (65%). **Juvenile**: after 3 years, before adulthood (10%). The earlier the onset, the more severe the damage.
Q: What is the pathogenesis, and what is Barkan's membrane?
A: In **isolated trabeculodysgenesis** the angle recess is absent. The iris inserts flat (commoner) or concave straight into a thick trabeculum, so outflow falls. **Barkan's membrane** is a proposed sheet of unresorbed mesodermal tissue over the angle. Its existence is debated, but **goniotomy** is based on cutting it.
Q: What is the Hoskins classification?
A: The **Hoskins classification** groups developmental glaucomas by anatomy; it guides treatment and prognosis. (A) **Isolated trabeculodysgenesis**: flat or concave iris insertion. (B) **Iridodysgenesis**: aniridia, iris hypoplasia, congenital iris ectropion. (C) **Iridocorneal dysgenesis**: Axenfeld–Rieger or Peters anomaly, sclerocornea, micro- or megalocornea.
Q: What else causes a cloudy or large cornea in an infant?
A: **Clouding**: CHED, mucopolysaccharidoses (no IOP rise, no enlargement), sclerocornea, forceps injury. **Enlargement**: axial myopia, megalocornea, keratoglobus, Marfan syndrome, homocystinuria. **Watering**: nasolacrimal duct obstruction, ophthalmia neonatorum. The key is the **corneal diameter** and IOP at **examination under anaesthesia**.
Q: Is the damage reversible, and what is the prognosis?
A: **Cupping** progresses faster in infants than in adults, but can reverse if IOP is lowered early. The immature lamina cribrosa bows back under pressure and springs back when it falls. **Haab's striae** and corneal scars stay. The outlook is worse with earlier onset, corneal scarring and amblyopia.

## Sturge–Weber syndrome
**Definition:** **Sturge–Weber syndrome** (encephalotrigeminal angiomatosis) is "a rare congenital neuro-oculocutaneous disorder present at birth". Its classic triad is a facial **port-wine stain** that respects the midline, ipsilateral glaucoma and intracranial angiomas. It is not inherited.

### Signs to show
1. **Face.** A one-sided red **port-wine stain** (naevus flammeus) over the ophthalmic (V1) and maxillary (V2) divisions. It rarely crosses the midline and thickens with age. Look for opposite-side hemiparesis; ask about seizures (75% in the first year).
2. **Lids.** The stain involves the lid, upper more than lower, with mechanical ptosis. Lid involvement raises the glaucoma risk.
3. **Episclera and conjunctiva.** A dense **episcleral vascular plexus** and dilated conjunctival vessels.
4. **Cornea and iris.** Oedema, or buphthalmos if the glaucoma is congenital; heterochromia.
5. **IOP and gonioscopy.** IOP is raised on the stain's side in about half. In infants the angle may look like congenital glaucoma, or show blood in Schlemm's canal from raised venous pressure. In adults it usually looks normal.
6. **Fundus.** A diffuse **choroidal haemangioma** gives a dark red **"tomato-ketchup" fundus**; look for cupping.

### Diagnosis line
- "A 12-year-old boy with **Sturge–Weber syndrome** (right V1 and V2 port-wine stain) and **associated right-eye glaucoma**."

### How to tell it apart
@widths 30 70
| Condition | How to tell apart |
|---|---|
| **Primary congenital glaucoma** | Usually bilateral, with no port-wine stain and rarely any systemic disease. Sturge–Weber glaucoma is usually one-sided, under the stain, with neurological involvement. |
| **Primary open-angle glaucoma** | In adults both show an open, normal-looking angle. Sturge–Weber adds the facial stain, dilated episcleral vessels and a one-sided rise. |

### How I will manage
1. **Look for brain involvement.** A skull X-ray or computed tomography shows **tramline (gyriform) calcification**; magnetic resonance imaging shows the pial angioma.
2. **Drugs for late-onset glaucoma.** A beta-blocker, an alpha-2 agonist or a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, alone or combined. I avoid **prostaglandin analogues**: the venous pressure is already high, and they can cause anterior uveal effusion.
3. **Goniotomy first under 2 years.** Advantage: no risk of intraoperative choroidal effusion. Disadvantage: it needs a clear cornea.
4. **Filtering surgery with care.** Plain trabeculectomy risks choroidal effusion, expulsive haemorrhage and bleb failure. So I prefer combined trabeculotomy–trabeculectomy, with sclerotomies first. An **Ahmed valve** controls pressure better long term than a Molteno tube.
5. **Treat the stain; follow up for life.** Pulsed dye laser works best early, before the skin thickens, in sittings 2–4 weeks apart. Full profiles: card GX.

### Viva
Q: What is the mechanism of glaucoma in Sturge–Weber syndrome?
A: It depends on the onset. In infants (about 60%), an **angle anomaly** blocks outflow, as in congenital glaucoma. In late onset (about 40%), raised **episcleral venous pressure** is the cause. Hypersecretion by the ciliary body or the choroidal haemangioma also contributes.
Q: Which patients with a port-wine stain get glaucoma?
A: Glaucoma occurs in 30–70% when the **port-wine stain** covers both V1 and V2; V2 alone carries the lowest risk. Lid involvement raises the risk further.
Q: How is the syndrome described by the systems involved?
A: **Tri-symptomatic**: skin, eye and central nervous system. **Bisymptomatic**: skin with the nervous system, or skin with the eye. **Monosymptomatic**: skin or nervous system alone.
Q: Why prefer combined surgery?
A: Combined **trabeculotomy**–trabeculectomy treats both causes: the angle anomaly and the raised venous pressure. Plain trabeculectomy carries more choroidal effusion and expulsive haemorrhage here.

## Lens-induced glaucomas
**Definition:** **Lens-induced glaucomas** are "a group of secondary glaucoma that share the lens as a common pathogenic cause". In plain words, the lens either clogs the meshwork or closes the angle. **Open-angle**: phacolytic, lens-particle and phacoanaphylactic. **Closed-angle**: phacomorphic and ectopia lentis.

### Signs to show
1. **Phacolytic glaucoma.** An elderly patient with long poor vision has sudden pain and redness. Signs: hyperaemia and microcystic corneal oedema. The chamber is **deep**, with cells and flare but **no keratic precipitates**. **White flocculent material** floats in it, sometimes as a pseudohypopyon. The angle is open. The lens is a **mature, hypermature or morgagnian** cataract, with a wrinkled capsule. IOP is over 35 mm Hg.
2. **Phacomorphic glaucoma.** A **unilateral intumescent cataract** with a **shallow central chamber**, unlike the fellow eye. The angle is closed.
3. **Lens-particle glaucoma.** After cataract surgery or capsulotomy: **free cortical material**, dense flare and cells, synechiae and raised IOP.
4. **Phacoanaphylactic glaucoma.** After surgery or a penetrating injury: keratic precipitates on the cornea **and** lens surface, low-grade vitritis and residual lens matter.
5. **Ectopia lentis.** A forward-displaced lens causes pupillary block and a shallow chamber. Look for Marfan syndrome or homocystinuria.

### Diagnosis line
- "Right eye **phacolytic glaucoma** with hypermature (morgagnian) cataract."
- "Left eye **phacomorphic glaucoma** with intumescent mature cataract."

### How to tell it apart
@widths 30 70
| Condition | How to tell apart |
|---|---|
| **Primary angle-closure glaucoma** (versus phacomorphic) | It occurs in hypermetropic eyes with normal lenses, and **both** chambers are shallow. Phacomorphic glaucoma affects **one** eye with a swollen cataract. |
| **Acute angle closure** (versus phacolytic) | Phacolytic glaucoma mimics an acute attack. But its chamber is deep, its angle open, and white flocculent material floats in the aqueous. |

### How I will manage
1. **Lower the IOP first.** I use a beta-blocker, **apraclonidine**, a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor and, if needed, intravenous **mannitol** 20%, 1–2 g/kg. Mannitol draws water out of the vitreous; avoid it in anuria, severe dehydration or cardiac decompensation. No pilocarpine in phacolytic glaucoma.
2. **Phacolytic and phacomorphic: remove the lens once IOP is controlled.** Cataract extraction is definitive. In phacomorphic glaucoma I add topical steroids and cycloplegics; a laser peripheral iridotomy can help.
3. **Lens-particle glaucoma.** Aqueous suppressants, topical steroids and cycloplegics (against posterior synechiae); surgery removes the lens debris.
4. **Ectopia lentis with pupillary block.** Laser iridotomy is the treatment of choice. Cycloplegics flatten the lens and pull it back; miotics worsen the block. A lens in the anterior chamber is removed. Full profiles: card GX.

### Viva
Q: What is the mechanism of phacolytic glaucoma?
A: In an ageing, hypermature lens, high-molecular-weight lens protein leaks through microscopic capsular openings. Large phagocytes engulf it. Protein and phagocytes clog the meshwork, so **phacolytic** glaucoma is open-angle.
Q: How does phacomorphic glaucoma develop?
A: **Phacomorphic** glaucoma is "secondary angle closure glaucoma due to lens intumescence". A rapidly maturing or traumatic cataract swells and pushes the iris root against the cornea.
Q: When do you suspect phacoanaphylactic glaucoma?
A: When granulomatous inflammation follows surgery or injury, because the patient is sensitised to his own lens protein. Criteria: polymorphonuclear leucocytes in the aqueous or vitreous, and too little lens protein to explain the glaucoma.
Q: What is inverse glaucoma?
A: **Inverse glaucoma** occurs in spherophakia. Miotics slacken the zonules, the lens moves forward, and pupillary block worsens. So miotics are avoided when the lens causes angle closure.

## Uveitic glaucoma
**Definition:** **Uveitic glaucoma** is a secondary glaucoma in which intraocular inflammation raises the IOP, by open-angle or closed-angle mechanisms. In plain words, inflammation clogs the meshwork or closes the angle; steroid treatment can add a rise.

### Signs to show
1. **Conjunctiva.** Circumciliary congestion, minimal in Posner–Schlossman syndrome.
2. **Cornea.** **Keratic precipitates**; band keratopathy in chronic cases.
3. **Anterior chamber and iris.** Flare and cells; posterior synechiae; **iris bombé** if they are complete.
4. **Lens.** Complicated cataract.
5. **IOP.** Raised; it can be low if the inflamed ciliary body makes less aqueous, or with a detachment.
6. **Gonioscopy.** An open angle with debris, or **peripheral anterior synechiae** closing it.

### Diagnosis line
- "Right eye **uveitic glaucoma** (open angle) with acute anterior uveitis and complicated cataract."

### How to tell it apart
@widths 30 70
| Condition | How to tell apart |
|---|---|
| **Posner–Schlossman syndrome** (glaucomatocyclitic crisis) | One eye, recurrent crises of high IOP in a white eye. Mild flare, fine white keratic precipitates, an open angle without synechiae. Normal between attacks. |
| **Fuchs heterochromic iridocyclitis** | One eye, chronic and white; the affected iris is usually lighter. **Stellate keratic precipitates**, cataract, and fine vessels bridging the angle. Posterior synechiae are rare. |

### How I will manage
1. **Control the inflammation.** Topical **prednisolone acetate 1%**, titrated; periocular or systemic steroid if needed. **Cycloplegics** (atropine 1%) prevent posterior synechiae and relieve ciliary spasm.
2. **Lower the pressure with aqueous suppressants.** A beta-blocker, alpha-2 agonist or carbonic anhydrase inhibitor is first line. **Miotics and prostaglandin analogues are contraindicated**: miotics increase inflammation, and prostaglandins can cause mild anterior uveitis.
3. **Treat pupillary block.** Laser iridotomy is safer than surgical iridectomy, but often fails from inflammation, fibrin or new vessels.
4. **Surgery in a quiet eye.** Trabeculectomy under heavy steroid cover with 5-fluorouracil, or an Ahmed valve. Laser trabeculoplasty is contraindicated. Full profiles: card GX.

### Viva
Q: What are the mechanisms of uveitic glaucoma?
A: **Open-angle**: trabeculitis, fibrinous aqueous, raised episcleral venous pressure, more aqueous secretion, steroid response. **Closed-angle**: posterior synechiae cause iris bombé, or synechiae seal the angle. A swollen ciliary body can also rotate forward and close it.
Q: What is Posner–Schlossman syndrome?
A: **Posner–Schlossman syndrome** (glaucomatocyclitic crisis) is recurrent, one-sided mild anterior uveitis with a marked IOP rise. Crises last hours to weeks. Cytomegalovirus DNA has been found in the aqueous. Treat with anti-glaucoma drugs (apraclonidine especially) and cycloplegics.
Q: What is Fuchs heterochromic iridocyclitis?
A: **Fuchs heterochromic iridocyclitis** is chronic, non-granulomatous, one-sided iridocyclitis. Its triad is heterochromia, cataract and keratic precipitates. Glaucoma is open-angle, from trabecular sclerosis.
Q: Why is laser trabeculoplasty contraindicated in uveitic glaucoma?
A: **Laser trabeculoplasty** does not work in an inflamed eye with open-angle glaucoma. It may cause a further significant rise in IOP.

## Steroid-induced glaucoma
**Definition:** **Steroid-induced glaucoma** is "a form of open angle glaucoma occurring as an adverse effect of exogenous corticosteroid therapy or excess endogenous production of glucocorticoids". In plain words, a steroid by any route can raise IOP in a susceptible person; the angle stays open.

### Signs to show
1. **Lids and cornea.** Lid skin atrophy or ptosis; increased corneal thickness.
2. **Anterior chamber** quiet; **pupil** dilated by topical steroid.
3. **Lens.** **Posterior subcapsular cataract**, another steroid effect.
4. **IOP.** High while the steroid continues; it may be normal after it stops.
5. **Gonioscopy** open in all quadrants; **disc** cupped.

### Diagnosis line
- "Both eyes **steroid-induced glaucoma** in a known case of vernal keratoconjunctivitis on topical dexamethasone, with posterior subcapsular cataract."

### How to tell it apart
@widths 30 70
| Condition | How to tell apart |
|---|---|
| **Primary open-angle glaucoma** | Bilateral, high IOP, no steroid history. Steroid glaucoma may be one-sided, with normal or high IOP. |
| **Normal-tension glaucoma** | Thin corneas and normal IOP. Steroid glaucoma after the steroid stops looks similar, so ask about steroids in every form. |

### How I will manage
1. **Stop the steroid.** IOP usually returns to normal within weeks to months. If it cannot stop, I switch to fluorometholone or loteprednol, or a non-steroidal drug. The rise follows potency: dexamethasone 0.1% raises IOP by a mean 22 mm Hg; fluorometholone 0.1%, by 6.
2. **Anti-glaucoma drugs** while the pressure settles. After intravitreal triamcinolone I continue them for about 6 months, until the crystals clear.
3. **Remove a depot** if the rise is intractable: pars plana vitrectomy with trabeculectomy.
4. **Laser trabeculoplasty, then trabeculectomy,** when drugs fail. Full profiles: card GX.
5. **Prevent it.** I record a baseline IOP and check it every 2 weeks on steroids.

### Viva
Q: What is a steroid responder, and how is the response graded?
A: A **steroid responder** shows a rise in IOP on repeated steroid drops. **Becker** (four times daily, 6 weeks, final IOP): low under 20, intermediate 20–30, high over 31 mm Hg. **Armaly** (three times daily, 4 weeks, rise): low under 6, intermediate 6–15, high over 15 mm Hg.
Q: How soon does the IOP rise?
A: With a potent topical steroid four times daily, in 2–6 weeks. Intravitreal triamcinolone 4 mg takes 4–8 weeks. Inhaled and skin steroids take about 3 months; oral prednisolone, 2–15 months.
Q: Who is at risk?
A: Patients with **primary open-angle glaucoma** or a family history of it, and children under 10. Also high myopes, diabetics and patients with rheumatoid arthritis. Infants on steroids can develop a picture like congenital glaucoma.
Q: What is the mechanism?
A: Steroids raise **outflow resistance** in the trabecular meshwork. Glycosaminoglycans accumulate, extracellular matrix increases, and meshwork cells phagocytose less debris. *(extra)* The myocilin gene is the most studied.

## Pigmentary glaucoma
**Definition:** **Pigmentary glaucoma** is a secondary open-angle glaucoma. Pigment shed from the back of the iris blocks the meshwork. In plain words, the mid-peripheral iris bows back and rubs on the zonules. About 35% of **pigment dispersion syndrome** develops glaucoma.

### Signs to show
1. **Patient.** A young (20–30 years), myopic man; usually both eyes.
2. **Cornea.** A **Krukenberg spindle**: pigment on the corneal endothelium.
3. **Anterior chamber.** Deep, with the mid-peripheral iris bowed back (**reverse pupillary block**).
4. **Iris.** **Mid-peripheral transillumination defects**; pigment granules.
5. **Lens.** Pigment on the posterior lens surface (Zentmayer's ring).
6. **Gonioscopy.** A wide open angle with a **dense, homogeneous dark-brown band** around the meshwork.
7. **Fundus.** Look for lattice degeneration and retinal detachment.

### Diagnosis line
- "Both eyes **pigmentary glaucoma** with Krukenberg spindles and mid-peripheral iris transillumination defects."

### How to tell it apart
@widths 30 70
| Condition | How to tell apart |
|---|---|
| **Pseudoexfoliation glaucoma** | Older (fifth decade on), both sexes. White powdery material on the pupil margin and lens; transillumination defects at the pupillary margin; blotchy angle pigment with Sampaolesi's line. |
| **Primary open-angle glaucoma** | No Krukenberg spindle, no mid-peripheral defects, no dense pigment band. |

### How I will manage
1. **Drops first.** Timolol 0.5% twice daily and dorzolamide 2% three times daily reduce aqueous production. Avoid timolol in asthma, and dorzolamide in sulpha allergy. **Pilocarpine 1%** limits iris movement and pigment release. But young patients tolerate it poorly, and it raises the risk of retinal detachment.
2. **Laser iridotomy** relieves the **reverse pupillary block**, so the iris flattens. Advantage: it treats the mechanism. Disadvantage: iridotomy risks such as an IOP spike and uveitis.
3. **Laser trabeculoplasty.** Advantage: a good initial response. Disadvantage: it may not last.
4. **Trabeculectomy** with an antimetabolite, or a drainage device. Full profiles: card GX.

### Viva
Q: What is the mechanism?
A: By **Campbell's mechanical theory**, the concave mid-peripheral iris rubs against the zonules and sheds pigment. Pigment and reduced outflow raise IOP. Later the meshwork cells degenerate and the meshwork scars.
Q: What is a Krukenberg spindle, and where else is it seen?
A: A **Krukenberg spindle** is pigment on the corneal endothelium. It also occurs in chronic angle closure, in Vogt's triad with glaukomflecken and sectoral iris atrophy.
Q: What is reverse pupillary block?
A: **Reverse pupillary block** is backward bowing of the mid-peripheral iris, with a deep chamber. Laser iridotomy relieves it.
Q: How does it differ from pseudoexfoliation glaucoma?
A: Pigmentary glaucoma affects young men (2:1) and lessens with age; it has no systemic link. It is mostly treated medically. Pseudoexfoliation affects older people and worsens with age; it mostly needs surgery.

## Angle-recession glaucoma
**Definition:** "**Angle recession glaucoma** is a secondary open angle glaucoma that is associated with blunt trauma to eye. Angle recession is a tear between the longitudinal and circular muscles of the ciliary body." It is a gonioscopy diagnosis. In plain words, the pressure can rise months or years after a blunt blow.

### Signs to show
1. **Old injury.** Lid or periocular scars; the patient may have forgotten the injury.
2. **Iris and pupil.** Iridodialysis, sphincter tears, traumatic mydriasis.
3. **Lens.** Subluxation, traumatic cataract, Vossius ring.
4. **IOP.** Raised in one eye; it may be low early, from ciliary shutdown.
5. **Gonioscopy**, 4–6 weeks after injury. A **widened ciliary body band** is the key sign, with a prominent scleral spur and irregular pigment. Compare the other eye: simultaneous bilateral Koeppe gonioscopy shows subtle recession best.
6. **Fundus.** Retinal dialysis or detachment; glaucomatous cupping.

### Diagnosis line
- "Right eye **angle-recession glaucoma** following blunt trauma __ years ago."

### How to tell it apart
@widths 30 70
| Condition | How to tell apart |
|---|---|
| **Cyclodialysis** | In recession the longitudinal muscle stays attached; in cyclodialysis the whole ciliary body is detached. |
| **Other one-sided glaucomas** (pseudoexfoliation, neovascular, phacolytic) | Look for pseudoexfoliative material, iris new vessels or lens matter. A trauma history and a widened band confirm recession. |

### How I will manage
1. **Acute injury.** I use timolol 0.5% twice daily or brimonidine 0.2%, with topical atropine and a steroid for pain and inflammation.
2. **Chronic glaucoma: prostaglandin analogues are the drug of choice.** **Miotics are ineffective** because the meshwork is scarred.
3. **Laser trabeculoplasty fails in most eyes**, because the angle is distorted and scarred.
4. **Trabeculectomy with an antimetabolite** when drugs fail. Advantage: it controls IOP. Disadvantage: success is lower than in primary open-angle glaucoma. Full profiles: card GX.

### Viva
Q: How common is angle recession, and how many get glaucoma?
A: **Angle recession** follows 20–94% of blunt injuries and 85% of traumatic hyphaemas. Only about 5.5% develop glaucoma; the risk is higher beyond 180°. Glaucoma peaks within 1 year and again after 10 years.
Q: What is the mechanism of the glaucoma?
A: **Trabecular damage**, not the recession itself. That is why miotics fail.
Q: What are the seven rings of trauma?
A: Iris sphincter tear, iridodialysis, angle recession, cyclodialysis, trabecular tear, zonular dialysis with lens subluxation, and retinal dialysis or tear.
Q: When should gonioscopy be done after blunt trauma?
A: **Gonioscopy** is deferred for 4–6 weeks after an acute injury. Early on, a hyphaema often masks the recession.

## Malignant glaucoma (aqueous misdirection)
**Definition:** **Malignant glaucoma** (ciliary block glaucoma) is a secondary angle closure after eye surgery. Aqueous is blocked "at the secreting portion of the ciliary body, so that aqueous is forced backwards into the vitreous". In plain words, fluid trapped behind pushes everything forward.

### Signs to show
1. **History.** Recent trabeculectomy, cataract surgery, laser iridotomy or cyclophotocoagulation.
2. **Anterior chamber.** **Diffusely shallow**, centre and periphery.
3. **Bleb** absent; **Seidel test negative**.
4. **IOP.** Raised or normal.
5. **Ultrasound biomicroscopy.** The ciliary processes are rotated forward. All anterior structures press against the cornea, with or without supraciliary fluid.

### Diagnosis line
- "Right eye **malignant glaucoma**, third day after trabeculectomy."

### How to tell it apart
@widths 30 70
| Condition | How to tell apart |
|---|---|
| **Pupillary block** | The iris bows forward and the iridotomy is not patent; laser iridotomy relieves it. |
| **Wound leak** | A soft eye, a poor bleb and a positive Seidel test. |
| **Excessive filtration** | Very low IOP, a good bleb and a negative Seidel test. |

### How I will manage
1. **Strong topical mydriatics** first.
2. **If they fail, intravenous mannitol** 20%, 1–2 g/kg. It shrinks the vitreous by drawing water into the plasma.
3. **If that fails, neodymium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet (Nd:YAG) laser** through a patent iridectomy, to break the anterior hyaloid face. Advantage: it acts directly on the barrier. Disadvantage: it needs a patent iridectomy.
4. **If the laser fails, pars plana anterior vitrectomy** removes the trapped fluid.
5. **Prevent it.** After past malignant glaucoma, or in angle closure, I suture the trabeculectomy flap more tightly. Full profiles: card GX.

### Viva
Q: What is the mechanism?
A: **Ciliary block**: aqueous is forced into the vitreous, and the anterior hyaloid face stops it coming forward.
Q: How does the laser help?
A: The **Nd:YAG laser** disrupts the anterior hyaloid face and relieves ciliovitreal compression.
Q: What does ultrasound biomicroscopy add?
A: **Ultrasound biomicroscopy** shows where the angle closes. The four sites are the iris (pupillary block), ciliary body (plateau iris), lens (phacomorphic) and behind the iris (malignant glaucoma).
Q: What causes a shallow chamber with raised IOP after trabeculectomy?
A: **Malignant glaucoma**, pupillary block from an incomplete iridectomy, or delayed suprachoroidal haemorrhage. A soft eye points instead to a wound leak or excessive filtration.

## Quick recall
:::recall
- **Buphthalmos**: epiphora, photophobia, blepharospasm; cornea over 12 mm (normal 10–10.5); IOP over 14 mm Hg (normal 11–14); cup over 0.3.
- **Haab's striae** are horizontal; forceps tears are vertical or oblique.
- **Goniotomy** if the cornea is clear; **trabeculotomy** if hazy. No alpha-2 agonists under 2 years.
- **Sturge–Weber**: V1 and V2 stain; raised episcleral venous pressure; no prostaglandins.
- **Lens-induced**: lower IOP, then remove the lens.
- **Phacolytic**: deep chamber, white flocculent material, open angle.
- **Uveitic**: steroid, cycloplegic, aqueous suppressant; no miotics or prostaglandins.
- **Steroid responders**: Becker final IOP under 20 / 20–30 / over 31; Armaly rise under 6 / 6–15 / over 15 mm Hg.
- **Pigmentary**: Krukenberg spindle, mid-peripheral defects; laser iridotomy.
- **Angle recession**: widened ciliary body band; miotics fail.
- **Malignant**: diffuse shallow chamber, no bleb, Seidel negative → mydriatics → mannitol → Nd:YAG → vitrectomy.

**Mnemonic:** Buphthalmos — **5 B's**: Boys · Bilateral in 2/3 · Blepharospasm · Blue sclera · Bull's eye (big eyes).

Congenital glaucoma — **ABCDDE**: Axial myopia · Buphthalmos · Cloudy cornea · Descemet's breaks · Diameter enlarged · Disc cupping · Examination under anaesthesia.

Angle pigment — **PIGMENT**: Pseudoexfoliation and pigment dispersion · Iritis · Glaucoma after angle closure · Melanosis · Endocrine · Naevus · Trauma.
:::
