## The instruction you may get
- "Examine the fundus of this glaucoma patient and draw it." (last year's hemi-CRVO case)
- "Do applanation tonometry and gonioscopy on this patient." (last year's CRVO case, which also had a Humphrey field: card G10)

## Spot
- **Flame-shaped and dot-blot haemorrhages** radiate from the disc into all four quadrants, with **dilated, tortuous veins**. This is central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO), the **"blood and thunder"** fundus.
- The same picture in the superior or inferior **hemisphere** only: hemi-central retinal vein occlusion (hemi-CRVO).
- **Glaucomatous cupping** in either eye: primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG).
- An old occlusion adds **optociliary shunts**, sheathed veins, laser scars and an **epiretinal membrane**.

**Keywords the examiner listens for:** blood and thunder · relative afferent pupillary defect · neovascularisation of the iris · undilated gonioscopy · ischaemic CRVO · 10 disc areas · lamina cribrosa · 100-day glaucoma · panretinal photocoagulation · anti-VEGF

## Focused examination — steps and their viva
### Step 1 — Visual acuity
**Do:** Snellen distance vision in each eye, then with a pinhole.
**Record:** "RE 6/36, no improvement with pinhole; LE 6/9."
Q: Why does the presenting vision matter?
A: It guides the type and the prognosis. Counting fingers or worse suggests an ischaemic occlusion; better than 6/60 is a good sign.

### Step 2 — Pupils, before any drop
**Do:** Swinging flashlight test in dim light, before dilating.
**Record:** "RE relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) present" or "No RAPD."
Q: Why test before dilating?
A: A dilated pupil makes the test useless. A marked **relative afferent pupillary defect**, over 0.7 log unit, is an ominous sign of ischaemic occlusion.
Q: Why is it less reliable in this patient?
A: It needs normal discs, pupils and fellow eye, so glaucomatous discs or a miotic pupil spoil it. The electroretinogram (ERG) does not need them.

### Step 3 — Iris, before dilating
**Do:** Slit lamp at high magnification: the pupillary margin first, then the iris. Describe vessels as thin, friable, arborising and irregular, with their extent in clock hours.
**Record:** "RE neovascularisation of the iris (NVI) at the pupillary margin, 2 clock hours" or "No NVI."
Q: Why look so carefully?
A: **Neovascularisation of the iris** starts at the pupillary margin, and it decides treatment. NVI over 2 clock hours, or any new vessels in the angle (NVA), needs prompt panretinal photocoagulation.

### Step 4 — Tonometry
**Do:** Goldmann applanation with topical anaesthetic and fluorescein, before gonioscopy and dilatation. Note the time.
**Record:** "Tn by GAT (Goldmann applanation) 18 / 16 mm Hg at 10 am."
Q: The pressure is 34 mm Hg. What are the causes?
A: Any pressure over 22 mm Hg needs a cause: the **open-angle glaucoma**, neovascular glaucoma (NVG), angle closure (cause or effect), or intravitreal steroids.

### Step 5 — Gonioscopy, before dilating
**Do:** Goldmann gonioscopy lens, each quadrant: new vessels, peripheral anterior synechiae, an occludable angle.
**Record:** "BE scleral spur seen in all quadrants; no NVA; no synechiae."
Q: Why before dilating?
A: **Undilated gonioscopy** is essential, because NVA occurs without NVI in 12% of eyes. It also shows angle closure.

### Step 6 — Dilated fundus, in the department's order
**Do:** Red glow, media, disc, vessels, background, macula. Then the periphery with the indirect ophthalmoscope; confirm with a 90 D (dioptre) lens.
**Record:** Draw both fundi: red haemorrhages, blue veins, yellow sclerosed vessels, black laser scars, green epiretinal membrane.
Q: Is that a shunt or new vessels on the disc (NVD)?
A: **Optociliary shunts** are thicker and carry retinal blood to the choroid, a sign of good compensation in an old occlusion. NVD are finer and branch into a network.

### Step 7 — The other eye
**Do:** Pressure, angle and disc; look for diabetic or hypertensive retinopathy.
**Record:** "LE cup–disc ratio 0.8, inferior rim thinning; open angles; no retinopathy."
Q: Why examine it carefully?
A: The **fellow eye** risk is 1% a year, 7% in 5 years. Its disc shows the glaucoma; retinopathy shows the systemic cause. An occludable angle suggests angle closure caused the occlusion.

## Say-it description
:::say
Mr X, a 65-year-old hypertensive man on drops for primary open-angle glaucoma, has sudden painless defective vision in the right eye for two months.

Vision: right eye 6/36, no better with pinhole; left eye 6/9. No relative afferent pupillary defect. Goldmann applanation tonometry: 18 and 16 millimetres of mercury at 10 in the morning.

Both eyes: anterior segment normal, no new vessels on the iris, grade 2 nuclear sclerosis. Undilated gonioscopy: scleral spur seen in all quadrants, no new vessels.

Right fundus: good red glow, clear media. Disc vertically oval, pink, well-defined margins. Cup–disc ratio 0.7, with inferior rim thinning and nasalisation, suggestive of glaucomatous cupping. Superior veins dilated and tortuous. Multiple red flame-shaped lesions, with red dots and blots, radiate from the disc into the superior hemisphere only. These are suggestive of superficial and deep retinal haemorrhages. No cotton-wool spots. Foveal reflex absent, retina thickened, suggestive of macular oedema. Indirect ophthalmoscopy: no peripheral new vessels. Confirmed with a 90 dioptre lens.

Left fundus: cup–disc ratio 0.8, inferior rim thinning, suggestive of glaucomatous cupping.

My diagnosis: right eye clinically non-ischaemic hemi-central retinal vein occlusion involving the superior hemisphere, with macular oedema; both eyes primary open-angle glaucoma, pressure under control on medical treatment; both eyes grade 2 nuclear sclerosis.
:::

## History to ask, if the examiner allows

| Question | Why (what it rules in or out) |
|---|---|
| Since when, and did it start suddenly? | Sudden, painless loss in one eye is typical. New vessels are likeliest in the first 6 months; angiography waits about 3 months |
| Do you have diabetes? | A major risk factor; diabetic retinopathy is the main look-alike |
| Do you have high blood pressure? | A major risk factor: the hardened artery compresses the vein inside their shared sheath |
| Which glaucoma drops, for how long, and used regularly? | A raised pressure slows venous outflow. Pilocarpine (a miotic) spoils the pupil test |
| Any bleeding or blood disorder, or a past clot? | Hyperviscosity (myeloma, leukaemia, polycythaemia) or a clotting disorder, commoner under 60: needs a blood work-up |
| Any oral contraceptive pills or diuretics? | Both are listed drug risk factors |

## Differentials
@widths 28 72
| Condition | How to tell apart (sign or test) |
|---|---|
| Ischaemic versus non-ischaemic CRVO | Bedside: vision, RAPD, cotton-wool spots, disc and macular oedema. Angiography confirms (table under Must know) |
| Branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO) versus hemi-CRVO | BRVO: a wedge of haemorrhage with its apex at an arteriovenous crossing, usually one quadrant. A block at the disc covers two quadrants. Even a major BRVO has no RAPD; a hemiretinal occlusion may |
| Ocular ischaemic syndrome | Veins dilated but not tortuous; mid-peripheral dot-blot haemorrhages; normal disc; carotid stenosis on imaging |
| Diabetic retinopathy | Always bilateral; beaded veins; hard exudates always present (absent in CRVO) |

## How I will proceed
@widths 26 40 34
| Test | What it shows | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA), once the haemorrhages clear (about 3 months) | Delayed arteriovenous transit, blocked fluorescence, vessel-wall staining, capillary non-perfusion, leaking new vessels | Over 10 disc areas of non-perfusion = ischaemic; shows macular ischaemia and later conversion |
| Optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the macula | Cystoid macular oedema, subretinal fluid; epiretinal membrane when old | Decides and monitors injections |
| Visual fields (card G10) and electroretinogram | Central scotoma is the commonest field defect; ERG b-wave under 60% of normal when ischaemic | Grade ischaemia when the pupil test fails; fields also stage the glaucoma |
| Systemic work-up | Blood pressure, fasting glucose, lipids, blood count, electrocardiogram, renal tests. Under 60: thrombophilia screen, homocysteine, autoantibodies | Treat the cause with the physician; young patients risk clots elsewhere |

## How I will manage
1. **Aim:** keep central vision by treating macular oedema, and prevent neovascular glaucoma. A physician treats blood pressure, diabetes and lipids.
2. **Macular oedema: intravitreal anti-VEGF first.** Ranibizumab 0.5 mg, bevacizumab 1.25 mg or aflibercept 2 mg, each in 0.05 ml. It blocks vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which makes ischaemic capillaries leak. Disadvantage: repeated injections, three or six monthly, then as needed. Grid laser does not improve vision in CRVO.
3. **Second line: intravitreal triamcinolone or the dexamethasone implant.** It reduces leakage and VEGF. It causes cataract and raises the pressure, a real risk in this eye.
4. **Prompt panretinal photocoagulation (PRP)** for NVI over 2 clock hours, any NVA, NVD or new vessels elsewhere (settings: card G5). It is definitive, but costs peripheral field and night vision. I do not give it prophylactically, unless follow-up is impossible in ischaemic CRVO.
5. **Pressure:** antiglaucoma drops to target; trabeculectomy or a drainage valve if they fail. Neovascular glaucoma: card G5. Full profiles: card GX.
6. **Follow-up** monthly for 6 months: vision, pupils, pressure, undilated gonioscopy, fundus, OCT and the fellow eye.

## Must know
- **Definition.** "Retinal vein occlusion (RVO) is an obstruction of the retinal venous system" that "may involve the central, hemicentral or branch retinal vein." In CRVO a thrombus forms in the central retinal vein at or just proximal to the **lamina cribrosa**.
- **Hemiretinal vein occlusion** *(Kanski — not in your practical books)*: regarded by some as a CRVO variant, ischaemic or non-ischaemic. It occludes the **superior or inferior branch** of the central retinal vein. **Hemispheric**: a major branch at or near the disc. **Hemicentral**: one trunk of a dual-trunked central retinal vein. It looks like a branch occlusion covering that hemisphere. Iris new vessels are commoner than in branch, rarer than in central occlusion. Extensive ischaemia: manage as ischaemic CRVO.
- **Types (Central Vein Occlusion Study, CVOS):** ischaemic; non-ischaemic, about **75%**; indeterminate, when haemorrhage hides the perfusion. **34%** of non-ischaemic eyes turn ischaemic by **3 years**.

@widths 24 36 40
| Feature | Non-ischaemic | Ischaemic |
|---|---|---|
| Vision | Moderate to severe loss | **Counting fingers or worse** |
| RAPD | Absent or mild | Marked, **over 0.7 log unit** |
| Fundus | Fewer haemorrhages and cotton-wool spots; less disc and macular oedema | Extensive haemorrhages, many cotton-wool spots, marked oedema |
| FFA non-perfusion | **Under 10 disc areas** | **Over 10 disc areas** |
| ERG | Normal or mildly depressed | **b-wave under 60% of normal; b-to-a ratio under 1** |

- **Glaucoma both ways.** Open-angle glaucoma heads the ocular associations. A raised pressure slows venous outflow (stasis, Virchow's triad). A bowed, stretched lamina cribrosa and nasalisation compress the vein. CRVO in turn causes neovascular glaucoma, steroid-induced glaucoma and angle closure.
- **"100-day glaucoma."** After ischaemic CRVO, NVI appears in about half the eyes, usually at 2–4 months. Neovascular glaucoma follows in **one-third** of these, usually at 3–5 months. Within 6 months: NVI **49%**, NVA 37%, NVG **29%**.

## Viva questions
Q: What is the commonest cause of visual loss in CRVO?
A: **Macular oedema**. Others: macular ischaemia, neovascular glaucoma, vitreous haemorrhage and optic atrophy.
Q: Why do you call this eye non-ischaemic?
A: Vision 6/36, no RAPD, no cotton-wool spots. Angiography would confirm under **10 disc areas** of non-perfusion; over that, it is **ischaemic CRVO**.
Q: Why classify the type?
A: It predicts the **risk of new vessels**, the visual prognosis and spontaneous recovery. It also sets the follow-up interval.
Q: What is "blood and thunder"?
A: **Blood and thunder**: four-quadrant haemorrhages radiating from the disc with dilated, tortuous veins. Widespread haemorrhage in ischaemic CRVO is called "tomato ketchup" fundus.
Q: Where is the block in each type?
A: Ischaemic: at or just behind the **lamina cribrosa**, with few collaterals. Non-ischaemic: about 6 mm further back, with plenty of collaterals.
Q: What is the ischaemic index?
A: Non-perfused area ÷ total retinal area. About 50%, roughly 10 disc areas, is the threshold for significant neovascular risk.
Q: What converts a non-ischaemic CRVO to ischaemic?
A: Vision under 6/60, 5–9 disc areas of non-perfusion, age over 60, cardiovascular disease, blood dyscrasias and nocturnal hypotension.
Q: Why are iris new vessels commoner than disc new vessels?
A: The retina is too ischaemic to form new vessels, but anterior segment capillaries stay viable.
Q: New vessels appear in a non-ischaemic CRVO. What do you suspect?
A: Another cause of ischaemia: **diabetes** or carotid artery disease.
Q: What did the Central Vein Occlusion Study show?
A: Grid laser did not improve vision. Prophylactic PRP did not prevent iris or angle new vessels. It advised monthly review and prompt **panretinal photocoagulation** once they appear.
Q: Why not prophylactic PRP?
A: When new vessels appeared, PRP regressed them in **56%** of untreated eyes but only **22%** of pre-treated eyes.
Q: Why does grid laser fail in CRVO?
A: Leakage is diffuse, involving the foveal centre and all four parafoveal quadrants. Collaterals form at the disc, not across the horizontal raphe.
Q: Can anti-VEGF replace PRP?
A: No. **Anti-VEGF** only delays new vessels, which return more strongly when it stops.
Q: Why is vision often worse on waking?
A: **Nocturnal hypotension** slows venous flow and turns a partial thrombosis into a complete one.
Q: How will you investigate a patient under 60?
A: Thrombophilia screen (protein C, protein S, factor V Leiden, lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin), homocysteine, autoantibodies, chest X-ray and treponemal serology.
Q: What is the visual prognosis?
A: Non-ischaemic: final vision better than 6/60 in 50%. Ischaemic: worse than 6/60 in 93%.

## Quick recall
:::recall
- Before dilating: pupils (RAPD), iris margin (NVI), pressure, undilated gonioscopy (NVA).
- Ischaemic: counting fingers or worse, RAPD over 0.7 log unit, over 10 disc areas, b-wave under 60%.
- 100-day glaucoma: NVI over 2 clock hours or any NVA means prompt PRP, never prophylactic.
- Macular oedema: anti-VEGF first; steroids raise the pressure; grid laser does not help.
- **Mnemonic:** none for CRVO; RUBEOTIC (causes of neovascular glaucoma) is on card G5.
:::
