## The instruction you may get
- "Examine the fundus of this glaucoma patient and draw it."
- "Do applanation tonometry and gonioscopy; read his Humphrey field" (card G10).

## Spot
- **Superficial flame-shaped and deep dot-blot haemorrhages** radiate from the disc into all four quadrants, with **dilated, tortuous veins**. This is central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO).
- The same picture in one **hemisphere** only: hemi-central retinal vein occlusion (hemi-CRVO).
- **Glaucomatous cupping** in either eye: primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG).
- Old occlusion: **optociliary shunts**, laser scars, **epiretinal membrane**.

**Keywords the examiner listens for:** blood and thunder · relative afferent pupillary defect · neovascularisation of the iris · undilated gonioscopy · ischaemic CRVO · 10 disc areas · lamina cribrosa · 100-day glaucoma · panretinal photocoagulation · anti-VEGF

## Focused examination — steps and their viva
### Step 1 — Visual acuity
**Do:** Snellen distance vision in each eye, then with a pinhole.

**Record:** "RE (right eye) 6/36, no improvement with pinhole; LE (left eye) 6/9."
Q: Why does the presenting vision matter?
A: It guides type and prognosis. Counting fingers or worse suggests ischaemia; better than 6/60 is a good sign.

### Step 2 — Pupils, before any drop
**Do:** Swinging flashlight test.

**Record:** "RE relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD)" or "No RAPD."
Q: Why test before dilating, and how reliable is it here?
A: A dilated pupil makes it useless. A marked **relative afferent pupillary defect**, over 0.7 log unit, means ischaemia. Glaucomatous discs or a miotic pupil make it unreliable; the electroretinogram (ERG) is not affected.

### Step 3 — Iris, before dilating
**Do:** Slit lamp, high magnification, pupillary margin first. Vessels: thin, friable, arborising, irregular; extent in clock hours.

**Record:** "RE neovascularisation of the iris (NVI), 2 clock hours" or "No NVI."
Q: Why look so carefully?
A: **Neovascularisation of the iris** starts at the pupillary margin and decides treatment. Over 2 clock hours, or any angle new vessels (NVA), needs prompt laser. It appears at 2–4 months: the **100-day glaucoma**.

### Step 4 — Tonometry
**Do:** Goldmann applanation with anaesthetic and fluorescein, before gonioscopy and dilatation.

**Record:** "Tn (tension) by GAT (Goldmann applanation tonometry) 18 / 16 mm Hg at 10 am."
Q: Why might the pressure be high?
A: Over 22 mm Hg needs a cause: the **open-angle glaucoma**, neovascular glaucoma (NVG), angle closure or intravitreal steroids.

### Step 5 — Gonioscopy, before dilating
**Do:** Goldmann gonioscopy lens, each quadrant: new vessels, synechiae, an occludable angle.

**Record:** "BE (both eyes) scleral spur in all quadrants; no NVA."
Q: Why before dilating?
A: **Undilated gonioscopy** is essential, because NVA occurs without NVI in 12% of eyes. It also shows angle closure.

### Step 6 — Dilated fundus
**Do:** In the department's order: red glow, media, disc, vessels, background, macula, periphery; confirm with a 90 D (dioptre) lens. At the disc: swelling (acute), pallor (late), cupping, shunts, new vessels.

**Record:** Draw both fundi: red haemorrhages, blue veins, yellow sclerosed vessels, black laser scars, green epiretinal membrane.
Q: Is that a shunt or new vessels on the disc (NVD)?
A: **Optociliary shunts** are thicker and drain retinal blood to the choroid, a sign of good compensation. NVD are finer and form a network.
Q: Why are some haemorrhages flame-shaped and others dot-blot?
A: **Flame-shaped haemorrhages** lie in the nerve fibre layer, which runs parallel to the surface. **Dot-blot haemorrhages** lie in the deeper, compact middle layer, which runs perpendicular to it.

### Step 7 — The other eye
**Do:** Pressure, angle and disc; diabetic or hypertensive retinopathy.

**Record:** "LE cup–disc ratio 0.8; open angles; no retinopathy."
Q: Why examine it carefully?
A: The **fellow eye** risk is 1% a year, 7% in 5 years. Its disc shows the glaucoma; an occludable angle suggests angle closure.

## Say-it description
:::say
Mr X is a 65-year-old hypertensive man on drops for primary open-angle glaucoma. He has sudden painless defective vision in the right eye for two months.

Vision: right eye 6/36, no better with pinhole; left eye 6/9. No relative afferent pupillary defect. Goldmann applanation tonometry: 18 and 16 millimetres of mercury at 10 in the morning.

Both eyes: anterior segment normal, no new vessels on the iris, grade 2 nuclear sclerosis. Undilated gonioscopy: scleral spur seen in all quadrants, no new vessels.

Right fundus: good red glow, clear media. Disc vertically oval, pink, well-defined margins. Cup–disc ratio 0.7, with inferior rim thinning and nasalisation, suggestive of glaucomatous cupping. Superior veins dilated and tortuous. Red flame-shaped lesions, dots and blots radiate from the disc into the superior hemisphere only, suggestive of retinal haemorrhages. No cotton-wool spots. Foveal reflex absent, retina thickened, suggestive of macular oedema. Confirmed with a 90 dioptre lens.

Left fundus: cup–disc ratio 0.8, inferior rim thinning, suggestive of glaucomatous cupping.

My diagnosis: right eye clinically non-ischaemic hemi-central retinal vein occlusion involving the superior hemisphere, with macular oedema. Both eyes: primary open-angle glaucoma, pressure under control on medical treatment, and grade 2 nuclear sclerosis.
:::

| Any diabetes? | A major risk factor; diabetic retinopathy is the main look-alike |
| Any high blood pressure? | A major risk factor: the hardened artery compresses the vein |
| Which glaucoma drops, how regularly? | A raised pressure slows venous outflow; pilocarpine (a miotic) spoils the pupil test |
| Any blood disorder or past clot? | Hyperviscosity or a clotting disorder, commoner under 60: blood work-up |
| Oral contraceptives or diuretics? | Both are drug risk factors for vein occlusion |

## Differentials
@widths 28 72
| Condition | How to tell apart (sign or test) |
|---|---|
| Branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO) versus hemi-CRVO | BRVO: a wedge of haemorrhage, apex at an arteriovenous crossing, one quadrant; a block at the disc covers two. Even a major BRVO has no RAPD; hemiretinal occlusion may |
| Ocular ischaemic syndrome | Veins dilated but not tortuous; mid-peripheral dot-blot haemorrhages; normal disc; carotid stenosis on imaging |
| Diabetic retinopathy | Always bilateral; beaded veins; hard exudates (absent in CRVO) |

## How I will proceed

| Test | What it shows | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA), once the haemorrhages clear (about 3 months) | Delayed transit, blocked fluorescence, vessel-wall staining, capillary non-perfusion, leaking new vessels | Over 10 disc areas of non-perfusion = ischaemic; shows macular ischaemia |
| Optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the macula | Cystoid macular oedema, subretinal fluid; epiretinal membrane when old | Decides and monitors injections |
| Visual fields (card G10); ERG | Central scotoma is the commonest defect; b-wave under 60% if ischaemic | Grade ischaemia when the pupil test fails |
| Systemic work-up | Blood pressure, fasting glucose, lipids, blood count, electrocardiogram, renal tests; thrombophilia screen if under 60 | Treat the cause |

## How I will manage
1. **Aim:** save central vision by treating macular oedema, and prevent neovascular glaucoma; a physician treats risk factors.
2. **Macular oedema: intravitreal anti-VEGF first.** Bevacizumab 1.25 mg/0.05 ml, ranibizumab 0.5 mg/0.05 ml or aflibercept 2 mg/0.05 ml. It blocks vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which makes ischaemic capillaries leak. Disadvantage: repeated injections, monthly for three or six months, then as needed.
3. **Second line: intravitreal triamcinolone or the dexamethasone implant.** It reduces leakage but causes cataract and raises the pressure, a real risk here.
4. **Prompt panretinal photocoagulation (PRP)** for NVI over 2 clock hours, any NVA, NVD or new vessels elsewhere (settings: card G5). It is definitive but costs peripheral field and night vision. Not prophylactic, unless follow-up is impossible in ischaemic CRVO.
5. **Pressure:** drops to target; trabeculectomy or a drainage valve if they fail; neovascular glaucoma, card G5. Full profiles: card GX.
6. **Follow-up** monthly for 6 months: undilated gonioscopy, OCT, fellow eye.

## Must know
- **Definition.** "Retinal vein occlusion (RVO) is an obstruction of the retinal venous system" that "may involve the central, hemicentral or branch retinal vein." In CRVO, a thrombus blocks the central retinal vein at or just proximal to the **lamina cribrosa**.
- **Hemiretinal vein occlusion** *(Kanski — not in your practical books)*: to some a CRVO variant, ischaemic or non-ischaemic. It blocks the **superior or inferior branch** of the central retinal vein. **Hemispheric**: a major branch at or near the disc. **Hemicentral**: one trunk of a dual-trunked vein. It looks like a branch occlusion of that hemisphere. Iris new vessels: commoner than in branch, rarer than in central occlusion. Extensive ischaemia: manage as ischaemic CRVO.
- **Types (Central Vein Occlusion Study, CVOS):** ischaemic, non-ischaemic (about **75%**) and indeterminate (haemorrhage hides perfusion). About **34%** of non-ischaemic eyes turn ischaemic by **3 years**.

@widths 24 36 40
| Feature | Non-ischaemic | Ischaemic |
|---|---|---|
| Vision | Moderate to severe loss | **Counting fingers or worse** |
| RAPD | Absent or mild | Marked, **over 0.7 log unit** |
| Fundus | Fewer haemorrhages and cotton-wool spots | Extensive haemorrhages, many cotton-wool spots, marked oedema |
| FFA non-perfusion | **Under 10 disc areas** | **Over 10 disc areas** |
| ERG | Normal or mildly depressed | **b-wave under 60% of normal; b-to-a ratio under 1** |

- **Why POAG is a risk factor.** Raised pressure causes venous stasis (Virchow's triad); a bowed lamina cribrosa and nasalisation compress the vein.
- **CRVO causes glaucoma too:** neovascular, steroid-induced and angle-closure glaucoma.
- **100-day glaucoma.** After ischaemic CRVO, NVI appears in about half the eyes, usually at 2–4 months. Neovascular glaucoma follows in **one-third** of these, usually at 3–5 months.

## Viva questions
Q: What are the risk factors for CRVO?
A: **Systemic**: hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, cardiac disease and carotid insufficiency, mainly over 60. Under 60: clotting disorders and vasculitis. Drugs: oral contraceptives and diuretics. **Ocular**: open-angle glaucoma first, then ischaemic optic neuropathy, tilted disc, disc drusen and hypermetropia.
Q: What is the pathogenesis, and where is the block in each type?
A: Artery and vein share a sheath through the rigid **lamina cribrosa**. A hardened artery or a bowed lamina compresses the vein, and a **thrombus** forms. Ischaemic: at or just behind the lamina, few collaterals. Non-ischaemic: 6 mm behind it, many.
Q: What is "blood and thunder"?
A: **Blood and thunder**: four-quadrant haemorrhages radiating from the disc, with dilated, tortuous veins. Ischaemic CRVO's widespread haemorrhages give a "tomato ketchup" fundus.
Q: Why do you call this eye non-ischaemic?
A: Vision 6/36, no RAPD, no cotton-wool spots (ischaemic infarcts of the nerve fibre layer). Over **10 disc areas** of non-perfusion would make it **ischaemic CRVO**.
Q: Why does the type matter?
A: **The type** predicts new-vessel risk, visual prognosis, spontaneous improvement and the follow-up interval. New vessels in a non-ischaemic eye suggest diabetes or carotid artery disease.
Q: What are the complications?
A: **Macular oedema**, the commonest cause of visual loss. Also macular ischaemia, neovascular glaucoma, vitreous haemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment and optic atrophy.
Q: What is the ischaemic index?
A: **Ischaemic index** = non-perfused area ÷ total retinal area; about 50% (10 disc areas) marks significant neovascular risk.
Q: What turns a non-ischaemic CRVO ischaemic?
A: Vision under 6/60, 5–9 disc areas of non-perfusion, age over 60, cardiovascular disease, blood dyscrasias and nocturnal hypotension.
Q: Why is NVI commoner than NVD in CRVO?
A: The retina is too ischaemic to form them; anterior segment capillaries stay viable.
Q: What did the CVOS show?
A: Grid laser did not improve vision, because leakage is diffuse. Prophylactic laser did not prevent anterior new vessels; delayed laser regressed them better. So: prompt **panretinal photocoagulation** once they appear.
Q: Can anti-VEGF replace PRP?
A: No. **Anti-VEGF** only delays new vessels, which return more strongly when it stops.
Q: Why is vision worse on waking?
A: **Nocturnal hypotension** slows venous flow, completing a partial thrombosis.
Q: How will you investigate a patient under 60?
A: Thrombophilia screen (protein C and S, factor V Leiden, antiphospholipid antibodies), homocysteine, autoantibodies, treponemal serology.
Q: What is the visual prognosis?
A: Non-ischaemic: final vision better than 6/60 in 50%. Ischaemic: worse than 6/60 in 93%.

## Quick recall
:::recall
- Before dilating: RAPD, iris margin, pressure, undilated gonioscopy.
- Ischaemic: counting fingers or worse, RAPD over 0.7 log unit, over 10 disc areas.
- NVI over 2 clock hours or any NVA: prompt PRP, not prophylactic.
- Macular oedema: anti-VEGF first; steroids raise the pressure.
:::
