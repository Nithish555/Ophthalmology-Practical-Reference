## Particulars and chief complaints
**Particulars:** name (Mr X), age, sex, occupation, place. The medical record number goes on the written sheet.

@widths 22 78
| Patient | Write the opening line like this |
|---|---|
| New complaint | "Mr X, 35-year-old male, labourer by occupation, came with c/o pain, redness, watering and defective vision (LE) for the past 10 days." |
| Review patient | "Mr X, 70-year-old male, has come for regular follow-up." |

- Put the eye in brackets after the complaints, and the duration as "for the past …".
- With several complaints, give them in time order, each with its own eye and duration.
- Use the patient's complaint ("prominence of both eyes", "drooping of the left upper lid"), never your diagnosis.

- Each "No h/o …" line rules out a named differential.

  - No h/o headache with vomiting and pain.
  - No h/o coloured haloes.
  - No h/o redness, watering, pain.
  - No h/o trauma to the eye.
  - No h/o frequent change of glasses.

**Tension:** before gonioscopy (which lowers the reading) and before dilating (which raises it); always the method and the time. The department's tables list "Tn by GAT" straight after visual acuity. That is fine on paper if you measured it first.

**Van Herick:** a thin slit 1 mm inside the temporal limbus, at 60°. Compare the peripheral chamber depth with the corneal thickness. **Grade 4** ≥ 1 (normal depth) · **3** ¼–½ · **2** ¼ · **1** < ¼ (potentially occludable).

**No slit lamp: the pen-torch method.** Hold the torch on the temporal side. Whole iris lit = grade 4, normal depth. Two-thirds lit = grade 2, shallow.

**Hirschberg test (before dilating):** hold a pencil torch in the midline, 2 feet away. Ask him to look at it. Normally both corneal reflexes lie at the centre of the pupil. A reflex at the pupillary border means about 7° (14 prism dioptres) of deviation. At mid-iris it means about 15° (30 prism dioptres); at the limbus about 30° (60 prism dioptres). It is a rough estimate only.

**Gonioscopy:** draw one cross per eye and write the deepest structure seen in each quadrant. Name the system: "by modified Shaffer's grading", as the department writes. Last year's exam sheet:

:::gonio
RE | S=SS | T=SS | I=SS | N=SS
LE | S=SS | T=SS | I=SS | N=SS
caption: Deepest structure seen in each quadrant — scleral spur all round: open angles (last year's exam sheet)
:::

**Fundus:** describe it by card B. In a long case a normal fundus is written "Media clear; disc and vessels appear normal; macula FR ⊕".

**Fields by confrontation:**
1. Sit face to face with him, 60 cm apart.
2. His right eye fixes your left eye; the other two eyes are closed.
3. Bring your finger in from the periphery, midway between you, in each quadrant.
4. Compare his field with your own.

**More examples:**
- "Bilateral axial proptosis, probably of thyroid-related orbitopathy, in active stage" (active or inactive by the signs).
- "Right eye: primary open-angle glaucoma, status post trabeculectomy with failed bleb and grade 2 nuclear sclerosis. Left eye: primary open-angle glaucoma and grade 2 nuclear sclerosis."

## Differentials, investigations and management
- **Differentials:** 4–6, most likely first. For each, give the points for, the points against, and the sign or test that tells it apart (ask 3).
- **Investigations:** confirm → stage or grade → find the cause → baseline → fitness for surgery (ask 4). Central corneal thickness goes here, not in the clinical table.
- **Management:** aim → medical → laser → surgery → follow-up and counselling (ask 5). Give the trigger for each step up.

## The five-asks method
Every case card answers the same five things, in the same places. Learn the method here once. Each card then fills it in for its own case.

@widths 6 30 64
| No. | The ask | What you must be able to say |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | History, with a reason for every question | What each answer tells you. For each negative, what a "yes" would point to, and how to tell the look-alikes apart |
| 2 | The must-do examination, in order | How to do each step, how to record it, and the 2–4 viva questions it brings |
| 3 | Differentials | The clinching sign or test that tells each one apart |
| 4 | Investigations | The purpose of each: to confirm, to stage, to find the cause, baseline, fitness for surgery |
| 5 | Management | Aim → medical → laser → surgery → follow-up, with the trigger for each step up |

### Ask 1 — a reason for every history question
- **One question per row.** "No redness, watering or pain" is three questions with three different reasons.
- **Positive history:** onset, duration, progression, laterality, associated symptoms and treatment so far. Say what each answer tells you: the cause, how chronic it is, its stage, or a complication.
- **Negative history:** for each question, say what a "yes" would point to. Where your books give one, add the bedside sign or test that tells the look-alikes apart.
- **Past, drug, treatment, personal and family history:** give the consequence for diagnosis or for treatment.
- **Treatment history:** drug, strength, frequency, duration, compliance and side effects.

**Worked example — the glaucoma negative history** (the department's starred lines, one per row):

@widths 22 40 38
| Question | A "yes" would point to | How to tell them apart |
|---|---|---|
| Any coloured haloes? | **Corneal epithelial oedema** from a pressure rise: angle closure. Other causes of haloes: mucus on the cornea in conjunctivitis, incipient cataract, vitreous opacities, snow blindness, a tilted intraocular lens | **Fincham (stenopaeic slit) test**: a slit is moved across the pupil. The glaucoma halo stays intact; the halo of incipient cataract breaks into its component colours |
| Any headache with vomiting and pain? | An **acute angle-closure attack**: pain, redness, headache, nausea and vomiting | Signs of the attack: a mid-dilated, vertically oval, poorly reacting pupil and a shallow chamber. Gonioscopy shows the closed angle |
| Any frequent change of glasses? | Raised pressure on the zonules and ciliary body impairs accommodation, so near vision suffers. Or he mistakes a field defect for poor vision | Perimetry: a field defect shows on the field chart |
| Any night blindness? | **Defective dark adaptation**, a feature of open-angle glaucoma. Miotic drops make it worse | Treatment history: is he using pilocarpine, a miotic? |
| Any injury to the eye? | **Angle-recession glaucoma** after trauma, usually in one eye | Gonioscopy shows the angle recession |

**Worked example — past and drug history:**

@widths 36 64
| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease? | **Beta-blocker drops** are contraindicated, so no timolol |
| Steroid use: which form, and for how long? | **Steroid-induced glaucoma**: open angle and raised pressure. Look for a posterior subcapsular cataract |

**Answering "Why did you ask…?"** Give the reason. Then say what a "yes" would mean, and the test that would confirm it.

Q: Why did you ask about coloured haloes?
A: **Coloured haloes** come from corneal epithelial oedema, which splits white light into its colours. A "yes" points to angle closure with a pressure rise. I would confirm it by Van Herick grading and gonioscopy, and use the Fincham test to exclude a cataract halo.

### Ask 2 — the order of the examination earns marks
1. **Pupils before any drop.** Look for a relative afferent pupillary defect before the pupil is dilated. Look for iris new vessels at the pupillary margin before dilating, too. Count anterior chamber cells, measure a squint by Hirschberg and test colour vision before dilating.
2. **Tonometry before gonioscopy and dilatation.** Gonioscopy presses on the cornea and opens the angle, so a later reading is falsely low. Dilatation raises the pressure by 4–5 mm Hg, so a later reading is falsely high. Always note the method and the time.
3. **The disc after dilatation.** Examine the disc and nerve fibre layer through a dilated pupil. Use a +90 dioptre (D) or +78 D lens at the slit lamp. **Exception:** do not dilate an eye with a narrow or closed angle until a laser iridotomy is done.

**Mnemonic — before you dilate: PART B.** Pupillary reflex · Angle · Rubeosis · Tension · BCVA (best corrected visual acuity). The angle means both the anterior chamber angle and the angle of a squint.

**The long-case order, step by step:**
1. Visual acuity: unaided, pinhole, best corrected, near.
2. Head posture; Hirschberg test and ocular movements.
3. Pupils: direct and consensual reflexes; the swinging torch light test.
4. Torch, then slit lamp, undilated. Lids and adnexa → conjunctiva → cornea → anterior chamber (Van Herick) → iris → pupil → lens.
5. Intraocular pressure by applanation, with the time.
6. Gonioscopy, with the cross diagram.
7. Dilate, unless the angle is occludable. Look at the lens again, then the fundus: distant direct → direct → +90 D → indirect.
8. Fields by confrontation (the seniors' addition).

**Every step on a card has three parts:**
- **Do:** how, in 1–3 lines, including what you tell the patient.
- **Record:** the department's shorthand.
- **Viva:** the 2–4 questions it brings, with their answers.

**Worked example — the Van Herick step:**
- **Do:** set a thin slit beam 1 mm inside the temporal limbus, at 60°. Compare the peripheral chamber depth with the corneal thickness.
- **Record:** "VH Gr IV (ND)", that is, Van Herick grade 4, normal depth.

Q: Why do you grade the anterior chamber by Van Herick?
A: **Van Herick grading** is an initial guide to which patients need a detailed examination of the angle. Gonioscopy then shows the angle itself.

Q: What does Van Herick grade 1 mean?
A: **Grade 1** means the peripheral chamber depth is less than a quarter of the corneal thickness. The angle may be occludable, so I would do gonioscopy and not dilate yet.

### Ask 3 — differentials, each with its clinching sign
- **Table:** Condition · Points for · Points against · How to tell apart. Give 4–6 rows, the most likely first.
- **The last column** names a sign you can show or a test result. Never write "clinical features".
- **Exclude-first line:** under the table, name the differential to exclude first, and how.
- **Say it like this:** "The differentials are A, which would show X, and B, which would show Y."

**Worked example — primary open-angle glaucoma:**

@widths 22 24 24 30
| Condition | Points for | Points against | How to tell apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ocular hypertension | Pressure above 21 mm Hg on two readings; open angle | Glaucomatous disc and field | Disc, rim and field stay normal in ocular hypertension |
| Normal-tension glaucoma | Glaucomatous disc and field | Pressure above 21 mm Hg | Diurnal phasing: the pressure stays below 22 mm Hg; more disc haemorrhages and notching |
| Pseudoexfoliation glaucoma | Open angle; high pressure | No white material | White flakes on the pupillary margin and lens; Sampaolesi's line on gonioscopy |
| Chronic primary angle-closure glaucoma | Raised pressure; cupping | Deep chamber, Van Herick grade 4 | Gonioscopy: closed angle. Indentation tells appositional closure from synechiae |

**Exclude first:** angle closure, by gonioscopy, before you call any glaucoma open-angle.

### Ask 4 — investigations, each with its purpose
Say them in this order:
1. **To confirm** the diagnosis.
2. **To stage or grade** it.
3. **To find the cause**, or a risk factor.
4. **Baseline** for follow-up.
5. **Fitness for surgery.**

For each test, know what you do, why, the expected finding and what it changes.

**Worked example — primary open-angle glaucoma:**

@widths 20 28 52
| Purpose | Test | What it shows and what it changes |
|---|---|---|
| To confirm | Gonioscopy | An open angle. It excludes angle closure and secondary causes such as angle recession and pigment dispersion |
| To confirm and stage | Humphrey 24-2 or 30-2 field | Paracentral scotoma, arcuate scotoma, nasal step. The mean deviation (cut-offs −6 dB and −12 dB) stages the damage as mild, moderate or severe |
| To find a risk factor and correct the reading | Pachymetry: central corneal thickness | Goldmann applanation is accurate at 520 µm; the error is about 0.7 mm Hg per 10 µm. A thin cornea (below 500 µm) reads falsely low and is a risk factor. A thick cornea (above 570 µm) reads falsely high |
| Baseline for follow-up | Diurnal phasing; optical coherence tomography of the nerve fibre layer | A pressure swing of 8 mm Hg or more is significant. Superior and inferior nerve fibre thinning; the scan is the baseline for progression |
| Fitness for surgery | The tests the planned operation needs | Example: specular microscopy and biometry before cataract surgery in pseudoexfoliation (card G6) |

**Say it like this:**
:::say
I will proceed with gonioscopy to confirm an open angle, and a Humphrey field to confirm and stage the damage. Pachymetry will correct the pressure reading. Diurnal phasing and a nerve fibre layer scan will give the baseline for follow-up.
:::

### Ask 5 — the management ladder, in the first person
Say it in this order: aim → medical → combinations → laser → surgery → follow-up and counselling. Give the trigger that moves you up each step. On every long-case card, two tables follow the ladder: "Drugs in this case" and "Laser and surgery in this case". The full drug profiles are on the subject's toolkit card (card GX for glaucoma).

**Worked example — primary open-angle glaucoma:**
:::say
My aim is to lower the intraocular pressure to a target pressure and stop further optic-nerve damage. The target pressure is the level at which further damage is unlikely.

First, medical treatment. I will start a prostaglandin analogue, latanoprost 0.005 per cent, one drop at bedtime. It lowers the pressure by increasing uveoscleral outflow. Its side effects are conjunctival hyperaemia, longer lashes and iris pigmentation. I avoid it in uveitis.

I will review him after two months. If the target is not reached, I will switch to another drug or add one. If two drugs are needed, I prefer a fixed combination. It gives simpler dosing, better adherence and less preservative toxicity. Its disadvantage is that the two drugs may need different times of day.

If he cannot use drops reliably, I will offer laser trabeculoplasty. Selective laser trabeculoplasty targets only the pigmented meshwork cells, so the tissue around them is spared heat. It is not suitable for uveitic, neovascular or angle-recession glaucoma.

The pressure may stay above target on three drugs, or the field may progress on maximal tolerated therapy. Then I will do a trabeculectomy. Its partial-thickness flap gives uniform pressure control and less hypotony. Its risks are hypotony with a flat chamber, bleb leak, bleb infection, endophthalmitis and cataract.

I will review him every three months, or monthly if the damage is advanced. Each time I will check the pressure and his compliance. I will repeat the field every six months, or every three months if advanced. I will repeat gonioscopy every year. I will counsel him that glaucoma is chronic, so the drops and the follow-up must continue. Lost vision does not return.
:::

**The triggers to step up**, as the books give them for trabeculectomy:
- the target pressure is not reached on three topical drugs;
- the field progresses on maximal tolerated therapy;
- side effects or poor compliance;
- a baseline pressure above 40 mm Hg;
- one eye already lost, or follow-up likely to be difficult.

## How to present it in 8–10 minutes
The older scheme gave the long case 20 + 10 minutes without saying how they are split. Prepare 8–10 minutes. If stopped, go straight to the summary and diagnosis (each card's 2-minute say-it script).

@widths 13 37 50
| Minute | Section | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| 0–0.5 | Particulars and chief complaints | One sentence: complaint, eye, duration |
| 0.5–2.5 | History of presenting illness | Onset, progression, associations; positives; negatives |
| 2.5–3.5 | Past, personal, family, treatment | Seniors' checklist; drugs with strength and frequency |
| 3.5–4 | General and systemic examination | One sentence if normal; give the vitals |
| 4–7.5 | Ocular examination, RE then LE | Brisk on normal rows; slow on the key structure |
| 7.5–8 | Special tests for this case | Gonioscopy, Hertel, ptosis measurements, cranial nerves |
| 8–8.5 | Summary | Positives and key negatives |
| 8.5–9 | Complete diagnosis | "My provisional diagnosis is …", by the formula |
| 9–10 | Differentials, investigations, plan | One line each; then stop for questions |

**Saying the examination (minutes 4–7.5):**
- Go row by row, in the table's order. In each row give the right eye, then the left eye.
- Group the normal rows in one sentence: "The lids, conjunctiva and cornea are normal in both eyes."
- Slow down on the key structure. Describe each finding, then say "suggestive of …".
- Give the pressure with its method and time. Then give the special tests for this case.

### Model opening — about 1 minute
:::say
My patient is a 35-year-old man, a labourer by occupation. He came with pain, redness, watering and defective vision in the left eye for the past 10 days.

He was apparently normal 10 days back. Then dust fell into his left eye while he was travelling on a two-wheeler. He had pain in that eye, sudden in onset and progressive in nature. It was associated with redness, watering, photophobia and defective vision. He was treated at a private clinic nearby with two eye drops, and then referred to our institute.

There is no history of contact lens wear, eye surgery, steroid drops or similar episodes in the past. He is not a known case of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, tuberculosis, asthma or epilepsy. He has no drug allergy. His personal and family history are not significant.
:::

### Model closing — the last 2 minutes
:::say
To summarise, my patient is a 35-year-old labourer with pain, redness, watering, photophobia and defective vision in the left eye. It began 10 days ago, after dust fell into the eye. He has no contact lens wear, steroid use or diabetes. Visual acuity is 6/6 in the right eye. In the left eye it is 6/60, not improving with glasses or pinhole. The left eye has lid oedema and circumcorneal congestion. It has a greyish-white, 4 by 4 millimetre paracentral corneal ulcer with well-defined margins. There is surrounding stromal infiltrate. The anterior chamber is of normal depth, with no hypopyon. The right eye and both fundi are normal.

## How to answer any viva question
Examiners mark the shape of an answer as well as its content. Lead with the answer, never with background.

- **Keyword first.** Put the exact term at the start of the answer, in bold. The examiner then hears it in the first second. Say "**Bayoneting** is…", not "It is called bayoneting when…".
- **Use the keywords.** Each subject card has a line of the keywords the examiner listens for. Use each of those terms in your answers.
- **Definition first.** Start from the book's standard definition, with its key terms unchanged. Then give one line in plain words.
- **Plain English, professional register.** Short sentences, one idea each. Findings in the present tense: "The right eye shows…". Management in the first person: "I will start…". Numbers with their unit, method and time.

**The shape for each kind of question:**

@widths 26 74
| The question asks for | Shape of the answer |
|---|---|
| **A definition** ("What is…?") | 1. The standard definition, in the book's wording, key terms unchanged. 2. One line in plain words. 3. The 2–3 features that make the diagnosis |
| **A classification** | 1. Name the system. 2. Give its grades in order. 3. Say which grade this patient has |
| **Causes** | 1. Group them (congenital / acquired, ocular / systemic). 2. Most common first in each group. 3. The mnemonic, if your mnemonics file has one |
| **Symptoms and signs** | 1. Symptoms first. 2. Signs in examination order: lids → conjunctiva → cornea → anterior chamber → iris → pupil → lens → intraocular pressure → angle → fundus |

| **Prognosis** | Good factors, then poor factors |

### Two model answers
Q: What is glaucoma?
A: **Glaucoma** is a chronic progressive optic neuropathy caused by a group of disorders leading to irreversible damage to optic nerves and corresponding visual field defects with or without high intraocular pressure. In plain words: the optic nerve is slowly and permanently damaged, and the field of vision shrinks to match. The diagnosis rests on the **optic disc**, the **visual field** and the **angle**.

Q: Tell me about latanoprost.
A: **Latanoprost** is a **prostaglandin analogue**, used as 0.005% drops once at bedtime. It lowers intraocular pressure by **increasing uveoscleral outflow**. Side effects: conjunctival hyperaemia, longer lashes and iris pigmentation. Avoid it in uveitis.

The first answer has the definition shape: the book's definition, one plain line, then what makes the diagnosis. The second has the drug shape: class, strength and frequency, mechanism, adverse effects, contraindication.

### Viva on the description
Q: What does distant direct ophthalmoscopy show?
A: **Distant direct ophthalmoscopy**, at one arm's distance, shows opacities in the media against the red glow. A greyish reflex suggests retinal detachment and a black reflex vitreous haemorrhage. A white reflex is leukocoria.

Q: Where do you judge the arteriovenous ratio, and what is normal?
A: **The arteriovenous ratio** is judged after the first branching of the vessels. The normal ratio is 2:3.

Q: Why are superficial haemorrhages flame-shaped and deeper ones dot-shaped?
A: **Flame-shaped haemorrhages** lie in the nerve fibre layer and follow its architecture; they come from the superficial precapillary arterioles. **Dot haemorrhages** lie in the inner retina, whose structures run perpendicular to the retinal surface.

Q: What is a cotton-wool spot?
A: **Cotton-wool spots** are ischaemic infarcts of the nerve fibre layer. Axoplasmic flow is interrupted, so transported material builds up in the axons.

Q: Why use both the direct and the indirect ophthalmoscope?
A: The **direct ophthalmoscope** gives a 15 times magnified, erect image of a 10–15° field, up to the equator. The **indirect ophthalmoscope** gives a stereoscopic view of a 35° field, out to the ora serrata. It sees better through hazy media.

Q: Why confirm with the +90 dioptre lens?
A: **Slit-lamp biomicroscopy with a +90 dioptre (D) lens** is the best way to examine the disc and nerve fibre layer. It is done through a dilated pupil.

## Focused examination — steps and their viva
**Method:** torch, then slit lamp, so the examiner sees each sign. Pupils and iris new vessels before any drop. Tension before gonioscopy and dilatation. Each step has **Do** (how), **Record** (the department's shorthand) and 1–2 viva questions.

### Step 1 — Inspect, then the pupils before any drop
**Do:** compare the two eyes with a torch. Check the direct and consensual reflexes, then the swinging torch light test for a relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD). Look at the pupillary margin before any drop.

**Record:** "RTL (3 mm); no RAPD" (round, reacting to light).

Q: Why do you look at the pupil before dilating?
A: The **pupillary margin** carries the hallmark flecks and shows loss of the pupillary ruff. **Poor mydriasis** is itself a sign of pseudoexfoliation.

### Step 2 — Slit lamp, undilated
**Do:** grade the chamber by Van Herick. Examine the pupillary margin and iris, and retro-illuminate for transillumination defects. Look for iridodonesis and phacodonesis, and compare the chamber depth of the two eyes.

**Record:** "VH Gr IV (ND)" (Van Herick grade 4, normal depth); "PxF (pseudoexfoliation) at the pupillary margin, no iris atrophy".

Q: What are the four points to describe pseudoexfoliation?
A: The **four-point description** gives the colour of the material (white) and its nature (flaky, dandruff-like). Then its location (pupillary margin as tufts; anterior lens capsule as a disc-shaped deposit), and whether there is iris atrophy.

Q: Why compare the chamber depth of the two eyes?
A: A difference in depth, or **phacodonesis**, points to zonular dialysis. Ultrasound biomicroscopy confirms its extent before cataract surgery.

### Step 3 — Applanation tonometry
**Do:** consent, topical anaesthetic, fluorescein and a disinfected prism; both eyes (card G8).

**Record:** "Tn by GAT 24 mm Hg (RE), 16 mm Hg (LE) at 11 am". That is tension by Goldmann applanation tonometry, right eye and left eye.

Q: Why measure the pressure before gonioscopy and dilatation?
A: **Goldmann applanation tonometry** comes first. Gonioscopy presses on the cornea and opens the angle, so a later reading is falsely low. Dilatation raises the pressure by 4–5 mm Hg, so a later reading is falsely high.

### Step 4 — Gonioscopy
**Do:** after tonometry and before dilating; topical anaesthetic and a disinfected lens; every quadrant (card G9).

**Record:** one cross per eye with the deepest structure seen in each quadrant. Note the pigment and Sampaolesi's line beside it.

Q: What is Sampaolesi's line? Is it specific?
A: **Sampaolesi's line** is a dark, dense, scalloped band of pigment on or in front of Schwalbe's line. It is not specific: it also occurs in pigment dispersion syndrome and chronic inflammation.

### Step 5 — After dilatation: the lens and the disc
**Do:** dilate, since the angle is open, and note how well the pupil dilates. Examine the anterior lens capsule, then the disc with a +90 dioptre lens.

**Record:** "Three zones on the anterior lens capsule (target sign); NS-2; CDR ___" (nuclear sclerosis grade 2; cup–disc ratio).

Q: Describe the deposit on the lens.
A: The **three zones (target sign)** start with a central disc, absent in 20%. Next comes a clear zone, rubbed clean by the moving iris. Outside it lies a peripheral granular zone, seen only after dilatation.

**Method:** 3–6 first-person steps, each with its trigger. Each drug gets a one-line mechanism and its key adverse effect or contraindication. Each procedure gets one advantage and one disadvantage. Full profiles: card GX.

1. **Aim and approach.** I will treat aggressively, with frequent follow-up, because pseudoexfoliation glaucoma responds less well to drugs.
2. **Medical.** Latanoprost 0.005% at bedtime increases uveoscleral outflow; it darkens the iris and lengthens the lashes. Timolol 0.5% twice daily lowers aqueous production; I avoid it in asthma. One drug is usually not enough, so I will combine.
3. **Laser trabeculoplasty**, if the pressure is not controlled. Advantage: it is more successful than in primary open-angle glaucoma. Disadvantage: the effect does not last as long.
4. **Trabeculectomy**, if the target pressure is not reached or the glaucoma progresses; combined with cataract surgery when both are needed. Advantage: the partial-thickness flap gives uniform pressure control with less hypotony. Disadvantage: bleb leak, bleb infection and cataract.
5. **Cataract surgery with precautions.** Iris hooks or a pupil expansion ring for the small pupil. A dispersive viscoelastic for the endothelium. A capsular tension ring for weak zonules.
6. **Follow-up.** Yearly without glaucoma; every 3 months with glaucoma.

## Must know
**Method:** the book's definition first, then related terms, the classification and the key numbers.

- **Pseudoexfoliation syndrome** is "an age-related systemic syndrome where mainly ocular tissues are affected through the gradual deposition of fibrillary white flaky material from the lens, mainly on the lens capsule, ciliary body, zonules, corneal endothelium, iris and pupillary margin resulting in secondary glaucoma." In plain words: white flakes from the lens settle all over the front of the eye and clog the drainage angle.
- **Glaucoma capsulare:** glaucoma in an eye with pseudoexfoliation; usually unilateral.
- **Who:** after 50, mostly in the seventh decade; men and women equally; commonest in Scandinavians.
- **40%** develop glaucoma or ocular hypertension. The angle is occludable in **9–18%**.
- **Hallmark:** flecks on the pupillary margin. **Most consistent sign:** the whitish powdery ring on the anterior capsule.
- **Pseudoexfoliation glaucoma versus primary open-angle glaucoma:** it starts later (over 60) and usually in one eye. The pressure is higher, often above 40 mm Hg, with wider swings. It responds less well to drugs.

## Viva questions
**Method:** a real short-case card has 12–18, basics first. Here are four that come up in every pseudoexfoliation case.

Q: What is true exfoliation?
A: **True exfoliation** is a lamellar delamination of the anterior lens capsule into thin, clear scrolls. It follows heat or infrared exposure, as in glassblowers. Glaucoma is infrequent.

Q: Why are the zonules weak?
A: **Zonular weakness** comes from the material invading the zonular attachments; its proteolytic enzymes break them.

Q: What precautions will you take at cataract surgery?
A: For the small pupil, iris hooks or an expansion ring. For the endothelium, a dispersive viscoelastic. For weak zonules, a chop technique and a **capsular tension ring**. After surgery, steroids given aggressively and for longer.

Q: How does pseudoexfoliation glaucoma differ from primary open-angle glaucoma?
A: **Pseudoexfoliation glaucoma** starts later and is usually one-sided. The pressure is higher, with wider daily swings. It progresses faster and responds poorly to drugs.

## Quick recall
:::recall
- **Order:** pupils before drops → slit lamp undilated → tonometry → gonioscopy → dilate → lens and disc.
- **Four points:** colour · nature · location · with or without iris atrophy.
- **Three zones:** central disc (absent in 20%) · clear zone · peripheral granular zone (after dilatation).
- **Sampaolesi's line:** on or in front of Schwalbe's line; not specific.
- **40%** get glaucoma or ocular hypertension; occludable angle in 9–18%.
:::
