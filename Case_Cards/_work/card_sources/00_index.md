## Particulars and chief complaints
**Particulars:** name (Mr X), age, sex, occupation, place; the medical record number goes on the written sheet.

**Write the opening line like this:** "Mr X, 35-year-old male, labourer by occupation, came with c/o pain, redness, watering and defective vision (LE) for the past 10 days." For a review patient: "Mr X, 70-year-old male, has come for regular follow-up."
- The eye in brackets after the complaints; the duration as "for the past …".
- Several complaints: in chronological order, each with its own eye and duration.
- The patient's complaint ("prominence of both eyes", "drooping of the left upper lid"), never your diagnosis.

## History of presenting illness
**Template:** "Patient was apparently normal ___ (days / months / years) back, then he noticed ___ in the ___ eye, which was:"
- insidious / sudden / gradual in onset;
- progressive / stationary in nature;
- associated / not associated with ___ (pain, redness, watering, photophobia, headache).

"He visited an ophthalmologist at his place, was found to have ___ and was started on ___. Then he came to our institution for further management." Then the positives ("H/o ___ ⊕"), then the negatives ("No h/o ___"); the department stars each line.

**Elaborate each complaint:**
@widths 22 78
| Complaint | Ask |
|---|---|
| Defective vision | Eye, onset, progression; distance or near; pain; night blindness; change of glasses; coloured haloes |
| Pain | Onset, site, character, radiation; aggravating and relieving factors; eye movement; time of day |
| Redness, watering | Onset; pain, photophobia, discharge; foreign-body sensation; lid swelling |
| Double vision | Goes when either eye is closed?; horizontal or vertical; worse in which gaze; diurnal variation |
| Prominence of the eye | One or both; onset, progression; intermittent?; change with posture or straining; swishing sound |
| Drooping lid, squint | Congenital or acquired; progression; constant or intermittent; diurnal variation; glasses, patching |

## Negative history
- Each "No h/o …" line rules out a named differential. Examiners ask "Why did you ask about …?"; each card gives the reasons.
- The department's glaucoma screen: No h/o headache with vomiting and pain · coloured haloes · redness, watering, pain · trauma to the eye · frequent change of glasses.

## Past, personal, family and treatment history
- **Past** (department checklist): "K/c/o DM / SHTN / TB / asthma / epilepsy for ___ years, on treatment ___" · similar complaints in the past · ocular surgery or trauma: eye, what, when, where.
- **Seniors' drug and systemic checklist, every case:** sulpha allergy · other drug allergy · medical history · head injury · asthma · "CVD" (heart disease or stroke: ask both) · migraine · Raynaud's phenomenon · peripheral vascular disease · steroid use (type, duration).
- **Personal:** "Takes mixed diet. Smoker / alcoholic for ___ years. Normal bowel and bladder habits."
- **Family:** "No h/o similar illness in the family members"; ask about glaucoma, night blindness and consanguinity.
- **Treatment:** drug, strength, frequency, eye, duration: "E/D timolol 0.5% BD (BE) for the past ___ years". Or: "Treated at a private clinic nearby with two eye drops, then referred to our institute."

## General and systemic examination
- **General:** "Patient is conscious, oriented, afebrile; moderately built and nourished; not anaemic, not icteric; no cyanosis, clubbing, generalised lymphadenopathy or pedal oedema."
- **Vitals:** "PR ___/min, regular in rhythm, normal in volume · BP ___/___ mm Hg, measured in the left arm in sitting posture · RR ___/min."
- **Systemic:** "CVS: S1 S2 heard, no murmurs · RS: NVBS, no added sounds · P/A: soft, no organomegaly · CNS: NFND."
- **Blocks added before the eye:** neck (thyroid swelling moving with deglutition); local examination of the eye for an ulcer (lid closure, Bell's phenomenon, lagophthalmos, trichiasis, entropion, ectropion, blepharitis, meibomitis, foreign body in the fornices); head posture and facial symmetry; cranial nerves I–XII for palsies.

**Tension:** before gonioscopy (which lowers the reading) and before dilating (which raises it); always the method and the time. The department's tables list "Tn by GAT" straight after visual acuity, which is fine on paper if you measured it first.

**Van Herick:** a thin slit 1 mm inside the temporal limbus at 60°; compare the peripheral chamber depth with the corneal thickness. **Grade 4** ≥ 1 (normal depth) · **3** ¼–½ · **2** ¼ · **1** < ¼ (potentially occludable). **No slit lamp — pen-torch method:** torch from the temporal side; whole iris lit = grade 4, normal depth; two-thirds lit = grade 2, shallow.

**Gonioscopy:** draw one cross per eye, write the deepest structure seen in each quadrant, and name the system ("by modified Shaffer's grading", as the department writes). Last year's exam sheet:

:::gonio
RE | S=SS | T=SS | I=SS | N=SS
LE | S=SS | T=SS | I=SS | N=SS
caption: Deepest structure seen in each quadrant — scleral spur all round in both eyes: open angles (last year's exam sheet)
:::

**Fundus and fields:** describe the fundus by card B; in a long case a normal fundus is written "Media clear; disc and vessels appear normal; macula FR ⊕". Confrontation: face to face, 60 cm apart; his right eye fixes your left eye, the other two eyes closed; bring your finger in from the periphery, midway between you, in each quadrant, and compare with your own field.

## Summary
"Mr ___, a ___-year-old ___, came with ___ in the ___ eye for the past ___, ___ in onset, ___ in nature, associated with ___; no history of ___; known case of ___. On examination, visual acuity ___ / ___; ___ [positives]; ___ [key negatives]; intraocular pressure ___ / ___ mm Hg by ___ at ___." Only the positives that make the diagnosis and the negatives that exclude the differentials.

## Complete diagnosis
**Formula: eye → disease → type / stage / grade → aetiology → complications or status → other eye.** Begin "My provisional diagnosis is …"; say "probably of … aetiology" when the cause is not proven.

@widths 22 26 26 26
| Slot | Glaucoma sheet | Corneal ulcer sheet | Third nerve model sheet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eye | Both eyes | Left eye | Right eye |
| Disease | primary open-angle glaucoma | corneal ulcer | third nerve palsy |
| Type / stage / grade | — | paracentral | pupil-sparing, infranuclear, incomplete |
| Aetiology | — | probably of bacterial aetiology | due to ischaemic microangiopathy |
| Complications or status | with pseudophakia, with intraocular pressure under control on medical management | in healing stage | — |
| Other eye | (both eyes) | state it if abnormal | state it if abnormal |

**More examples:**
- "Bilateral axial proptosis, probably of thyroid-related orbitopathy, in active stage" (active or inactive by the signs).
- "Right eye: primary open-angle glaucoma, status post trabeculectomy with failed bleb and grade 2 nuclear sclerosis. Left eye: primary open-angle glaucoma and grade 2 nuclear sclerosis."

## Differentials, investigations and management
- **Differentials:** 2–4, most likely first, each with one point for and one against.
- **Investigations:** to confirm → to stage or grade → to find the cause → baseline before treatment. CCT goes here, not in the clinical table.
- **Management:** aim → medical (drug, strength, frequency, eye) → laser → surgery → follow-up and counselling.

## Seniors' corrections — do these every time
:::trap
- **EOM** row straight after visual acuity.
- **Van Herick grade** for every anterior chamber ("VH Gr IV (ND)"); the seniors circled it.
- **CCT out of the clinical table**: they struck the row out; it is an investigation.
- **"Fields (confrontation test)"** after the fundus, before the diagnosis.
- **The drug and systemic checklist** in every case; **complete the negative:** "No h/o headache **with vomiting and pain**".
- **Gonioscopy:** name the grading ("by modified Shaffer's grading") and draw the cross.
- **Corneal ulcer:** site, size, shape, depth, colour, margin, floor, vascularisation, KPs, stromal infiltrate; then sensation, staining, syringing, digital tension.
- **Cranial nerves:** name the muscles: the fifth nerve's mandibular division supplies masseter, temporalis, medial and lateral pterygoids.
:::

## How to present it in 8–10 minutes
The older scheme gave the long case 20 + 10 minutes without saying how they are split. Prepare 8–10 minutes; if stopped, go straight to the summary and diagnosis (each card's 2-minute say-it script).

@widths 13 37 50
| Minute | Section | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| 0–0.5 | Particulars and chief complaints | One sentence: complaint, eye, duration |
| 0.5–2.5 | History of presenting illness | Onset, progression, associations; positives; negatives |
| 2.5–3.5 | Past, personal, family, treatment | Seniors' checklist; drugs with strength and frequency |
| 3.5–4 | General and systemic examination | One sentence if normal; give the vitals |
| 4–7.5 | Ocular examination, RE then LE | Brisk on normal rows; slow on the key structure |
| 7.5–8 | Special tests for this case | Gonioscopy, Hertel, ptosis measurements, cranial nerves |
| 8–8.5 | Summary | Positives and key negatives |
| 8.5–9 | Complete diagnosis | "My provisional diagnosis is …", by the formula |
| 9–10 | Differentials, investigations, plan | One line each; then stop for questions |

### Model opening — the first 2 minutes
:::say
My patient is a 35-year-old man, a labourer by occupation, who came with complaints of pain, redness, watering and defective vision in the left eye for the past 10 days.

He was apparently normal 10 days back, when dust fell into his left eye while he was travelling on a two-wheeler. Then he had pain in that eye, which was sudden in onset, progressive in nature and associated with redness, with watering, photophobia and defective vision. He was treated at a private clinic nearby with two eye drops and then referred to our institute.

There is no history of contact lens wear, eye surgery, steroid drops or similar episodes in the past. He is not a known case of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, tuberculosis, asthma or epilepsy, and he has no drug allergy. His personal and family history are not significant.
:::

### Model closing — the last 2 minutes
:::say
To summarise, my patient is a 35-year-old labourer with pain, redness, watering, photophobia and defective vision in the left eye for 10 days after dust fell into it, with no contact lens wear, steroid use or diabetes. Visual acuity is 6/6 in the right eye and 6/60 in the left, not improving with glasses or pinhole. The left eye has lid oedema, circumcorneal congestion and a greyish-white, 4 by 4 millimetre paracentral corneal ulcer with well-defined margins and surrounding stromal infiltrate; the anterior chamber is of normal depth, with no hypopyon. The right eye and both fundi are normal.

My provisional diagnosis is left eye paracentral corneal ulcer, probably of bacterial aetiology, in healing stage. The main differential is a fungal ulcer, which would show feathery margins and satellite lesions.

I would take a scraping from the base and margins of the ulcer for Gram stain, a 10 per cent potassium hydroxide mount and culture, treat according to the result, with atropine for the ciliary spasm, and follow the ulcer until it heals.
:::

## The order
1. **Particulars and chief complaint** (shorthand as on card A), each eye with its own duration; history jotted beside it: diabetes, hypertension (duration, treatment), past surgery, injections or laser, night blindness, consanguinity.
2. **Vision with pinhole**, right eye (RE) above left eye (LE): "6/24 → PH 6/18".
3. **Tonometry**: method, value, time (the department uses the non-contact tonometer).
4. **Anterior segment**: the four points below, before dilating.
5. **Fundus description**, aloud, in the order below.
6. **Drawing**, colour-coded, every lesion labelled.
7. **Diagnosis** with grade or stage and the lens status, one line per eye.
8. **Plan**: eye + treatment, and the investigation that decides it.

### Say it — a normal fundus
:::say
Fundus examination of the right eye. On distant direct ophthalmoscopy at one arm's distance, there is a good red glow. Direct ophthalmoscopy close to the face reveals clear media. The disc is normal in size, vertically oval and pink, with well-defined margins, a cup–disc ratio of 0.3 and a healthy neuroretinal rim. The vessels arise from the centre of the disc and branch dichotomously, maintaining an arteriovenous ratio of 2 is to 3, with no attenuation, crossing changes or sheathing. The background retina is normal, with no haemorrhages, exudates or pigment. The macula is normal, and the foveal reflex is present. On indirect ophthalmoscopy with a 20 dioptre lens, the peripheries are normal. On slit-lamp biomicroscopy with a plus 90 dioptre lens, the above findings were confirmed. The left fundus is the same.

## Diagnosis and plan
- **"Diagnosis:"**, one line per eye and condition, with the lens status and the grade the sheets leave out: "Right eye high-risk proliferative diabetic retinopathy with clinically significant macular oedema · right eye pseudophakia".
- **Plan**, eye + treatment: "(BE) panretinal photocoagulation (PRP)", "LE sectoral PRP", "LE intravitreal bevacizumab, 3 doses" (written "Inj Avastin").
- **Investigations:** macular optical coherence tomography, fundus fluorescein angiography, B-scan if the media are hazy.

## Common fundus-description mistakes
- Naming a lesion without describing it (colour, shape, size, margins, number, location, then "suggestive of").
- Skipping the red glow and the media; a cup–disc ratio without the rim; no AV ratio.
- "Macula normal" without the foveal reflex; no periphery; no 90 dioptre confirmation.
- Pupil and iris checked after dilating, so an RAPD or iris new vessels are missed.
- A diagnosis without its grade, stage or lens status; unlabelled circles, eyes swapped; abbreviations aloud ("NVE" for new vessels elsewhere).

## The method, in order
1. **Hear the instruction** and do exactly that ("Examine the anterior segment of this patient").
2. **Introduce yourself and take consent**; ask the age and complaint only if the examiner allows.
3. **Inspect before you touch**: head posture, face, both eyes compared, lids, deviation.
4. **Focused examination**, torch then slit lamp, so the examiner sees each sign: pupils (relative afferent pupillary defect) and iris new vessels before dilating; tension before gonioscopy.
5. **Describe aloud**, right eye then left (always the other eye too): each finding described, then "suggestive of …"; no abbreviations.
6. **Complete diagnosis line**: eye → disease → type / stage / grade → aetiology → complications or status → other eye.
7. **Differentials**: 2–3, each with one distinguishing point.
8. **Investigations**: the one or two that decide.
9. **Management**: one line.

**Timing:** minutes 0–1 instruction, consent, inspection · 1–7 examination · 7–10 description to management · then 5 minutes of questions. The split is not official: if asked for the diagnosis early, give it at once.

## Say it — a short-case model
**Instruction:** "Examine the anterior segment of this patient and measure the intraocular pressure."

:::say
On slit-lamp examination of the right eye, the lids and conjunctiva are normal and the cornea is clear. The anterior chamber is of normal depth, Van Herick grade 4. There is white, flaky, dandruff-like material at the pupillary margin as tufts and on the anterior lens capsule as a disc-shaped deposit, with no iris atrophy, suggestive of pseudoexfoliation. The pupil is round and reacting to light, and the lens shows grade 2 nuclear sclerosis. The left anterior segment is normal. Intraocular pressure by Goldmann applanation tonometry is 24 millimetres of mercury in the right eye and 16 in the left, at eleven in the morning.

My diagnosis is right eye pseudoexfoliation syndrome with raised intraocular pressure and grade 2 nuclear sclerosis. The differentials are pigment dispersion syndrome, with mid-peripheral iris transillumination defects and a Krukenberg spindle, and true exfoliation, a delamination of the lens capsule after heat or infrared exposure.

I would do gonioscopy and a dilated disc examination to decide whether this is pseudoexfoliation glaucoma, with a Humphrey field and central corneal thickness. If it is, I would start medical treatment; at cataract surgery I would plan for a poorly dilating pupil and weak zonules.
:::
