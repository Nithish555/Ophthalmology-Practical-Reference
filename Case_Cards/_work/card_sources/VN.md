## What this session is and how to use this guide
- **What it is:** a 4 h 05 min live mock exam. Seven postgraduate candidates presented long cases to senior examiners, who interrupted, criticised and asked viva questions. Closing advice from the examiners and a short glaucoma talk followed. The moderator called it a "survival kit": it teaches the technique of answering and how to take constructive criticism, not only knowledge. The planned format was a 5-minute presentation, about 20 minutes of viva and 5 minutes of criticism.
- **What is in these five cards:** VN1 (this card) is the overview and timed summary. VN2 holds every survival tip, de-duplicated, with timestamps. VN3 covers the seven cases, what was criticised and the preferred diagnosis line. VN4 is the viva question bank with the answers the examiners expected. VN5 lists the slides and images mentioned and a plan for your last week.
- **How to use it:** read VN2 and VN3 first, then cover the answers in VN4 and test yourself. The session answers are what the examiners said on the day, not a textbook. Glaucoma answers were compared with your glaucoma cards, and every difference is flagged for you to settle with your seniors. Use the timestamps to jump to the exact moment on the recording.

## Every distinct tip, grouped by the stage of the exam
Repeated tips from the four parts of the recording are merged into one line, and a merged tip keeps all its timestamps. Some tips were given in words and some are clear from how the examiners behaved; the second kind is marked "(implied)". Numbering restarts in each group. Where a tip uses a technical term, the matching question in VN4 gives the examiners' answer.

### Before you start
1. The moderator framed the session as a survival kit: it teaches the technique of answering and how to take constructive criticism, not only knowledge. [02:23–02:34]
2. Start preparing from day one, not on the eve of the exam. There is no replacement for hard work, practice and perfection. [3:39:10, 3:43:39–3:43:47]
3. Know which phase of training you are in. Ophthalmology needs about three months before you start to love it, because undergraduate exposure is small. [3:43:56–3:44:20]
4. In the first six months acquire every basic clinical skill and instrument technique. Categorise yourself, learn the terminology and the newer procedures, and master the equipment (gonioscopy, slit lamp, indirect ophthalmoscope, 90 dioptre lens) and the tests. [3:39:30–3:39:38, 3:47:27–3:47:46]
5. In the first six months write down every new term each day and read about it for half an hour every evening. [3:44:20–3:44:37]
6. Do one refraction every day and read up retinoscopy and refraction, so that you are proficient by six months. [3:47:46–3:47:56]
7. Read up every investigation that comes with your cases. [3:47:56–3:48:03]
8. In the second six months pick up clinical signs, make diagnoses and miss nothing. In the second year learn to advise patients, plan investigations and surgery, and master cataract surgery and its complications. In the third year miss nothing and discuss the differentials of each case. [3:48:03–3:48:45]
9. Choose one textbook that suits you and add your own simplified, categorised notes (sticky notes) to it, to revise in the final month. [3:44:37–3:45:15]
10. Treat every case as your Bible and every patient as your encyclopedia. The more you see, the more you learn, so do not complain about workload. [3:39:24, 3:45:17–3:45:48]
11. After each patient ask yourself the take-home message. [3:46:00–3:46:10]
12. Document every case fully and exactly. Write the real findings every day, not notes copied forward from the day before. [3:39:53–3:40:18]
13. Carry a small notebook, record the cases you see and discuss them with seniors. Call patients back after a couple of days and re-examine them once you have read up. [3:40:24–3:40:42]
14. Do not use a phone or the internet as a substitute for knowledge. Build your own memory. [3:40:44–3:41:15]
15. Re-read the same basic clinical books again and again (two of the books named were Parsons and Kanski; the third title is garbled). Revisit each day's cases and ask the faculty about every doubt. [3:41:17–3:41:35]
16. Carry a pocket clinical-methods book (a second-edition "Clinical Methods" text was recommended [?]) and a second recommended book (title garbled [?]) every day. Look things up in front of the patient or in the next room. [3:41:56–3:42:40]
17. Keep colour pencils, a circle template (a bangle works) and a transparent scale in your pocket or purse, so that you do not run around when a ptosis or similar case is given. Also carry a basic armamentarium: a tuning fork, and a stethoscope and hammer [?]. [3:42:42–3:43:13]
18. Protect your study time. Treat the three years (about a thousand days) as the time to study and skip worldly distractions. Cope with stress by loving your work, and do not use family or children as an excuse for not studying; make them your strength. [3:46:13–3:46:45, 3:47:02–3:47:20]
19. Present cases as early and as often as possible, at least once or twice a week, so that exam day is not the first time. Practise oral presentation a lot, because fluency comes out automatically under exam tension. [3:39:41–3:39:53, 1:45:59–1:46:19, 3:48:45–3:48:58]
20. Form small discussion groups and discuss cases and signs as often as you can. This reduces the fear of scary cases. [3:49:00–3:49:25]
21. Ophthalmology books are not reference books. Understand the natural course of each disease (for example how a retinal detachment behaves) and answer from understanding, not from lookup. [3:49:33–3:49:56]
22. Revise the basics that examiners keep asking. These are synechial closure versus iris processes, and Gunn sign with the arteriovenous-crossing changes of hypertensive retinopathy. Also learn the two reasons why branch retinal vein occlusion is commonest in the superotemporal quadrant: the branching pattern with a direct thrust, and the many arteriovenous crossings. [1:00:28–1:00:31, 1:01:48–1:02:13, 1:05:20–1:05:30]
23. Keep revising high-yield topics such as third nerve palsy, and make notes of all the points, because it has many dictums. One example: an isolated medial rectus palsy or an isolated mydriasis is not called a partial third nerve palsy. [2:08:58–2:09:36, 2:12:15–2:12:25]
24. Be proficient in uveitis basics: the telltale signs of old uveitis, the signs of recurrent anterior uveitis, old versus fresh keratic precipitates, and the types of keratic precipitates. [3:09:19–3:09:41]
25. Learn to tailor and present whatever case you are given, and never miss the importance of any finding. [3:09:41–3:09:51]
26. Know exactly which peripheral retinal degenerations need treatment and which do not. It is a very common question, because every postgraduate examines high myopes with the indirect ophthalmoscope. Be ready to explain to the patient which lesions need intervention. [3:28:40–3:29:29]
27. Check your slides before you present. Put in every key picture (a primary-position photograph and the anterior-segment pictures were missing in two cases), and know how to enlarge and share them in full-screen mode. Examiners will ask to see them. [37:33–39:00, 1:48:50–1:48:58, 3:19:16–3:19:40]
28. While a colleague presents, listen critically for mistakes. The examiner invited the other postgraduates to spot the error (implied). [1:58:57–1:59:03]
29. Expect ordinary cases. A perforated ulcer is not a normal long case. Keratitis is usually a short case, not perforated, often healing and often fungal, because acute patients should not be inconvenienced. [2:24:48–2:25:25]

### Presenting the history
1. Give exact durations in days, weeks or months, and keep the time-line consistent. Do not write "sudden" and "progressive" without saying when each applied. [56:56–57:45]
2. Describe the history fully, especially the decrease of vision. Do not give a fragmented, telegraphic history. [12:56–13:09]
3. Present the presenting complaint in the patient's own words, for example "coloured rings around the light", and not in medical terms such as "rainbow halos". Use lay language for symptoms and keep sign-words for the examination, because sign-words in the history sound tutored. [13:43–14:09, 1:42:14–1:42:25]
4. Give the optical (spectacle) history under its own heading, and always include a drug history. Link every past-history question to management: steroid exposure, and drugs that are contraindicated in asthma or cardiac disease. [14:16–14:38, 18:09–18:13, 24:51–25:08]
5. Form a provisional diagnosis or differential from the history alone before you move to the examination. For gradual loss of vision in both eyes in an elderly person, run through all the causes (lens, glaucoma, retina, systemic and diabetic, cornea) before you settle. [14:52–14:59, 15:38–16:10]
6. Let each history point and each examination step narrow the differential, so that the next question follows from the last answer. Do not tailor the history backwards to the final diagnosis. Present it as a prospective history in which the diagnosis emerges. [25:24–26:14, 26:40–26:50]
7. Every question you ask must serve a purpose. Know what each negative history item is ruling out. [21:19–21:22, 21:36–21:51]
8. Leave out negative history that does not belong to the case. If you ask about seizures because of headache, you should also be asking about other neurological deficits. [23:25–23:29, 27:11–27:21]
9. Be ready to defend anything you say or write. Ask yourself "why have I said this?" [27:21–27:33]
10. State clearly whether a past procedure such as laser in situ keratomileusis (LASIK) was done in one eye or both. A vague history is the first thing examiners probe. [2:57:07–2:57:15]
11. Patients call any corneal refractive procedure "LASIK", so do not accept the label without evidence. Cross-check the history, for example by asking whether the patient saw well without glasses after the surgery. Records are rarely available, so examination is how you corroborate the history. [2:59:44–2:59:54, 3:06:33–3:06:54]
12. After trauma, elaborate the mechanism of injury (what the patient was doing) and try to get the patient's records. [3:19:55–3:20:14, 3:20:29]
13. Reason from the time-line. Normal vision for 10 years after a penetrating injury argues that the entry was not in the cornea or lens but further back, probably in the sclera. [3:20:45–3:21:05]
14. In orbital inflammation ask about pets and parasite exposure. [1:36:25–1:36:40]

### Examination and findings
1. Be ready to say what you looked for in each part of the examination, and tie it to the findings already presented. For example, with a blood pressure of 220/110, look first for hypertensive changes in the fundus, not only for papilloedema. [1:59:15–2:00:20]
2. Know the textbook grading of signs cold, for example mild, moderate and severe ptosis with the millimetre values. [2:00:35–2:00:55]
3. Label the neurological examination "neurological evaluation" and give a one-line gross summary (no sensory-motor deficit, deep tendon reflexes grossly normal). Do not use an odd heading such as "muscular skeletal system". [2:02:55–2:03:15]
4. State the lens grading, for example the grade of nuclear sclerosis. [32:20–32:23]
5. When you say the lids and globe are normal, know what you are excluding, for example xanthelasma and proptosis. Mention the relevant adnexal and conjunctival signs. [51:57–52:26]
6. In a vascular occlusion look for cells and flare in the anterior chamber, to pick up inflammatory causes. [53:12–53:36]
7. Link the presenting event to a mechanism. If the intraocular pressure (IOP) is high, check the cornea for epithelial oedema and bullae and say what you looked for. [58:56–59:10]
8. In gonioscopy, say that you did indentation gonioscopy and state whether the closure was appositional or synechial. [59:46–1:00:20]
9. In any vein occlusion, examine the disc and know why: risk factors, glaucoma, and collaterals versus new vessels. [1:03:00–1:03:15]
10. Be able to describe artery versus vein and point them out on a picture. [1:04:56–1:05:00]
11. Describe the fundus in an order: disc in detail, macula, vessels, then background. Do not jump to the pathology and skip the rest. [1:05:35–1:05:54]
12. Know how to describe each type of haemorrhage and a microaneurysm, how to tell a dot haemorrhage from a microaneurysm, and which conditions give red lesions in the fundus. [1:06:21–1:06:50]
13. Say "resistance to retropulsion" rather than just "positive" or "negative". [1:26:35–1:26:44]
14. Know the clinical signs of thyroid eye disease. The examiner has a list of about 28 and named Möbius sign (difficulty in convergence) as one of the earliest. [1:25:11–1:25:36]
15. Know a simple bedside test for minimal proptosis, the ruler test. [1:31:15]
16. Know several exophthalmometers: Hertel, Luedde, and one for use after an orbitotomy. [1:32:36]
17. When you suspect optic nerve compression, test colour desaturation with a red or green object, not only with plates. [1:39:56–1:40:24]
18. In the corneal diagram, draw cross-sections at three areas: the oedematous stroma, the apparently normal cornea and the site of perforation. [2:29:13–2:29:35]
19. Draw the lids with lashes and the tear film. Draw a normal tear meniscus as a triangle at the lower lid margin, and a dry-eye meniscus as concave with small marks for debris. Blue with hatching for stromal oedema was approved. Examiners say this gives the impression that you examined well. [2:30:39–2:31:35]
20. Describe a corneal lesion cornea first, then the lesion, precisely and not vaguely ("two-thirds of the cornea"). Concentrate on the cornea and mention the pre-corneal tear film, lids and lashes. [2:29:40–2:30:12]
21. In a corneal ulcer examine and mention the adnexa, not only the lacrimal sac: lid margin, pre-corneal tear film, Meibomian glands, lash and lid-margin position, and pre-existing dry eye. In the other eye mention the tear meniscus, and think of dry eye when the picture looks like peripheral ulcerative keratitis. [2:30:04–2:30:24, 2:32:46–2:33:17]
22. Do not perform or mention digital tonometry in a perforated cornea. It can make the condition worse. [2:32:23–2:32:41]
23. If you blame an orbital inflammatory cause for limited movements, examine for it. Evert the upper lid, retract it laterally and ask the patient to look down. Then look at the lacrimal gland for injection or tenderness. [3:01:23–3:01:40]
24. When a patient reports LASIK, look for a flap scar. You cannot see it unless there is gross thinning, so press on the cornea with a blunt instrument or a cotton-tip applicator (after topical anaesthetic [?]) to bring out a scar. After photorefractive keratectomy (PRK) there may be nothing to see. [2:59:56–3:00:30]
25. In a centre without topography, use keratometry to check for earlier refractive surgery: a flat reading, or trouble getting a reading, suggests it, and a keratometry of 44–45 suggests none. Reason from the finding: minus 12 dioptres with flat keratometry means regression, partial correction or no correction, and the diagnosis must be revised. [3:05:21–3:06:30, 3:06:59–3:07:17]
26. Use exact descriptive terms, for example circumcorneal congestion and not just "hyperaemia". [3:07:25–3:07:47]
27. When you have few findings, elaborate fully on the ones you have; "tessellated" alone was not enough. In a myopic fundus describe the disc size and colour, cup–disc ratio, tilt, vessels, peripapillary atrophy, chorioretinal degeneration at the peripapillary region and macula, and posterior staphyloma. Examiners want the basics described well so that they can take you forward. [3:03:56–3:04:04, 3:08:19–3:09:15]
28. After any trauma or surgery, look around the limbus and over the conjunctiva and sclera for scars and report them. After a foreign-body history, a posterior removal leaves a scar, possibly a blackish discolouration. [3:21:12–3:21:22, 3:37:25–3:37:55]
29. Identify the type of cataract surgery from the scar and state it definitely. Say the lens is in the bag if you can see the capsulorhexis rim in front of the lens. [3:21:36–3:21:57, 3:22:04–3:22:08]
30. When you cannot see a break, still say where it is likely to be (the laser-mark area, or a pigmented patch from the foreign-body entry). Use indentation indirect ophthalmoscopy and search again and again; take a break and search again. [3:22:56–3:23:45, 3:38:03–3:38:23]
31. Pigment or cells in the anterior vitreous are a Shaffer sign only in an eye that has not been operated. In an operated eye they may reflect the intervention. [3:26:39–3:27:08]
32. For IOP measurement, seat the patient comfortably, use the correct technique, calibrate the tonometer and disinfect it. [3:55:29–3:55:42]

### The diagnosis line
1. Re-read your diagnosis slide before you present. The examiners go straight to it and check every grade and finding written there. [1:58:49–1:59:10]
2. Be specific in every term: "senile cataract", not "cataract". State the type that applies to your patient, for example "neurogenic ptosis", and not a general list. [15:15–15:20, 2:02:08–2:02:19]
3. Make the label match what you can show. An "ischaemic" label with no cotton-wool spots will be noticed. [1:05:56–1:06:07]
4. Be able to name the refractive error when asked, not only quote the numbers, even though refraction is a separate case in the exam. [50:06–50:21]
5. State clearly why a leading diagnosis is unlikely. For thyroid orbitopathy, the points were no inflammation, no lid retraction and full movements. [1:25:36–1:26:11]
6. Be ready to give both an aetiological diagnosis and a morphological diagnosis. [2:25:27–2:25:39]
7. When the picture is unclear, keep a spread of differentials in mind: peripheral ulcerative keratitis (PUK), Mooren's ulcer, vasculitis-related immune keratitis, and infection by fungus or virus. [2:33:19–2:33:40]
8. Be ready for "what if it were not isolated?" and have the associated syndromes ready. [2:03:21–2:03:35]
9. Never use a label such as pseudoproptosis unless you can define it. Proptosis is forward protrusion of the globe in primary gaze; pseudoproptosis is due to a shallow orbit or a large globe. Exclude orbital and facial causes and measure the axial length. [2:58:09–2:58:44]
10. Rank the diagnosis as primary, secondary and tertiary. Being told "it is a uveitis case" does not make uveitis the primary diagnosis. Even when tipped off that "this is a cataract case", place each diagnosis by its importance. [3:02:30–3:02:50, 3:07:50–3:08:14]
11. Carry your own measurements into the diagnosis. A 30 mm globe in both eyes means axial myopia. [3:03:31–3:03:39]
12. Avoid "senile" for a 49-year-old (the cut-off quoted was about 45). With high myopia call the cataract a complicated cataract, that is, a cataract with an associated morbidity. [3:04:26–3:05:00]
13. Name the factors that predispose an operated eye to detachment (vitrectomy, cataract surgery and capsulotomy with the neodymium:yttrium–aluminium–garnet (YAG) laser). Always list the associated risk factors for rhegmatogenous retinal detachment: YAG capsulotomy, trauma, surgery and high myopia. [3:22:25–3:22:40, 3:25:36–3:26:04]

### Investigations and management
1. Bring the relevant investigation reports with a glaucoma case, such as the visual field and glaucoma imaging. A glaucoma diagnosis needs a documented field defect. [34:22–35:11]
2. Learn how to interpret a visual field report, because most examiners ask it. The examiner recommended a journal article on presenting a visual field report. [35:13–35:32]
3. Know the target IOP and the precautions during surgery. [35:45–36:10]
4. A stepwise plan is acceptable: control the pressure medically first, then plain cataract surgery, then check the IOP, add medicines and do glaucoma surgery later if needed. Frame it as a first aim with several options depending on the response. [33:51–34:13, 36:56–37:10]
5. Tailor the investigations to rule out the risk factors that have not already been detected. [49:43–49:53]
6. Back a claim of macular oedema with the confirming test, optical coherence tomography (OCT), and its numbers (central thickness above about 270–280 microns), plus the fundus sign of a lost macular reflex. [1:12:57–1:13:30]
7. Tie the prognosis and plan to what the patient wants, which is better vision, and not only to the laboratory diagnosis. [1:07:14–1:07:24]
8. Say that the systemic evaluation is done and controlled, and move to ocular management when you are told. [1:07:34–1:07:42]
9. Use generic drug names with the correct dose, and no brand names. [1:11:57–1:12:10]
10. When you use steroids, state that you will monitor the IOP. [1:14:34–1:14:44]
11. Computed tomography (CT) is often enough for proptosis, even when vision is reduced. Do not insist on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) unless it is needed. [1:28:50–1:29:30]
12. Know the red flags that make a third nerve palsy an emergency: pupil involvement, acute onset, severe headache, meningeal signs and hemiparesis. They mean immediate neurosurgical referral, and recognising them can save a life. [2:15:51–2:17:15]
13. Scrape the ulcer margin and base, remove necrotic debris and avoid thinned areas to prevent perforation. Do a smear straight away and look for fungal hyphae; if none is found, start empirical treatment guided by severity. [2:35:36–2:36:05, 2:35:41–2:35:49]
14. Order an ultrasound (B-scan) of the posterior segment when you cannot see in, especially if surgery is likely, to define the extent of infection. [2:36:36–2:36:57]
15. Antibiotic monotherapy is "not indicated" in general. The examiner allowed it only where fortified drops are not accessible and the lesion is mild or moderate, off the visual axis and responding. [2:37:25–2:37:57]
16. Do syringing in all cases of infectious keratitis; do not rely on the test of regurgitation on pressure over the lacrimal sac (ROPLAS) alone. Treating a lacrimal block helps the ulcer heal. [2:39:55–2:40:30]
17. Control the infection first and only then think of steroids, even in PUK. [2:41:30–2:41:36]
18. The goal of treating infectious keratitis is to eliminate the infection and prevent complications. Restoring vision is not the goal at this stage. [2:43:44–2:44:26]
19. For a large perforation do not offer glue. Plan therapeutic keratoplasty as early as possible once the infection is under treatment. [2:45:34–2:45:50]
20. Brush up on the applications of recent investigations, such as anterior segment OCT for corneal thinning and OCT in posterior segment cases. Examiners ask about them. [2:47:15–2:48:03]
21. Keep "seek the hole and seal the hole" at the centre of every retinal detachment answer. [3:36:07–3:36:12]
22. Classify detachment procedures in a simple way: external (scleral buckles) versus internal (silicone oil and gases). Know everything about the gases. [3:36:16–3:36:47]
23. In a vitrectomised eye a buckle is preferable but behaves differently. The final choice is the surgeon's preference. [3:38:30–3:38:42]
24. When several drops are suspected in a drug allergy, stop all of them and reintroduce them one by one. [4:01:21–4:01:25]
25. For the first-line drug choice in glaucoma, use a prostaglandin analogue if affordable and a beta-blocker if cost is an issue. Use carbonic anhydrase inhibitors only for mild-to-moderate lowering. Add a second drug if the first works, and switch class if the first response is poor. [4:01:43–4:02:11]
26. Treat the person, not the disease. Glaucoma is chronic, so build a good rapport. [4:03:16–4:03:26]

### Behaviour in the viva
1. Stay relaxed, cool and confident. If the examiner catches you out, stay calm. Smile, hide the tension, and answer with good articulation. Examiners praised a candidate despite visible tension and told the candidate to relax. [1:41:55–1:41:58, 1:43:24–1:43:41, 2:05:09, 2:06:07, 2:58:04]
2. Examiners differ, so do not be rattled by a harsh style. If you prepare every day, presenting is no problem: the presentation stays the same and only the examiners change. [1:43:15–1:43:24, 3:43:15–3:43:26]
3. Use simple English with scientific terms. Flamboyant English is not needed. [1:42:02–1:42:09]
4. "The ball is in your court": examiners do not come with a fixed list, so your presentation leads them. Lead them to the areas you know well, and mention only what you can answer questions on. Avoid negatives you cannot defend, because what you say steers the questions, and do not tie yourself in a knot. [25:08–28:12, 1:42:27–1:42:45, 1:45:51–1:45:57]
5. If you do not know, do not guess badly. The examiner will see your gap and keep pressing. Say "pardon, sir" or "pardon, madam" and pause; never guess. [1:42:55–1:43:12, 1:45:34–1:45:43]
6. Never try to outsmart the examiner. [1:45:43–1:45:49]
7. Answer exactly what is asked. The examiner said "not answering my question" when a related detail was given instead. [1:45:47–1:45:51, 2:43:52–2:43:58]
8. When asked about your case, discuss your case and not an assumed scenario. Answer for this patient. The examiner cut in twice with "pertaining to this particular case only". [32:02–32:10, 2:02:13, 2:45:00–2:45:04]
9. Do not "disappear" when you are asked a question. Answer it. [3:35:56]
10. Do not agree with a suggested diagnosis that your own findings contradict. The candidate said no to "nuclear lesion?" and the examiner approved (implied). [2:12:09–2:12:20]
11. Examiners catch one glaring mistake and then forget everything else you said, so double-check your terms before you use them. [2:57:52–2:58:02]
12. The examiner's questions come straight from the standard text ("I am not asking anything out of the book"). Answer from the standard definition. [2:01:12–2:01:20]
13. Structure your lists. Categorise risk factors (systemic vascular, thrombophilia, inflammatory, blood dyscrasias) rather than listing them at random. Localise a lesion in a fixed anatomical order, here from the orbit to the nucleus, which is easier to follow. [48:19–48:27, 2:05:19–2:06:07]
14. Depth beyond what a postgraduate is expected to know is not demanded. When a candidate stalled on a retina-specialist "why", the examiner supplied the answer and said so. [55:41, 56:37–56:44]
15. If a picture or slide will not load, keep talking and keep answering. [1:34:29]
16. The exam tests the basics. Missing a rare question will not fail you, but a basic blunder will, for example writing a retinoscopy reading of plus 4 and calling it myopia. [1:46:34–1:46:51]
17. The examiners test the flow of your reasoning and how you interpret the findings, not only the final diagnosis. [1:46:56–1:47:06]
18. Many examiners are subspecialists and not general ophthalmologists, so you may be better read than they are in some areas. Be ready for basic questions even from examiners outside the case's subspecialty [?]. [1:46:19–1:46:34, 3:24:14–3:24:44]

### Time management
1. Keep to time. The planned format was a 5-minute presentation, about 20 minutes of viva and 5 minutes of criticism, 30 minutes in all. A presentation of 8 minutes was flagged as too long, and each case was later cut to about 15 minutes in total. [02:11–04:21, 12:22, 25:08–28:28]
2. Keep the history brief. Leave out normal items and write "nothing contributory" for the systemic examination. [1:17:11–1:17:28]
3. The long-case presentation normally finishes with the clinical examination, and the discussion then takes over. [47:12–47:17]
4. Prepare a template for each common case type (proptosis, uveitis, open-angle glaucoma, neuro-ophthalmology and so on) with the examination columns ready. At the bedside write only the positive findings, and fill in the normal findings later, outside the room. [1:43:52–1:44:20, 1:44:22–1:44:44]
5. In a real-patient exam you have time, and you may be asked to elicit signs in front of the patient. Stay relaxed. [1:45:24–1:45:29]

## The commonest mistakes the examiners criticised
:::trap
- A fragmented history with the complaint in medical words ("rainbow halos") and a muddled time-line without exact durations.
- Negative history items that the candidate could not defend (seizures, diplopia), a history back-fitted to the diagnosis, and no optical or drug history.
- No differential voiced from the history, and vague terms: "cataract" for "senile cataract", "hyperaemia" for "circumcorneal congestion", "senile" for a 49-year-old.
- A diagnosis list in the wrong order, with the presenting problem first (uveitis, or cataract) when the primary diagnosis was axial myopia. Also a label that did not match the findings ("ischaemic" with no cotton-wool spots).
- Labels used without proof or definition: pseudoproptosis with no axial length, LASIK accepted from history without keratometry, "partial" palsy without the rules.
- Basic grading and definitions not known by heart: ptosis grades, Gunn sign, retropulsion called "positive", haemorrhage types.
- Gonioscopy reported without saying indentation was done, and no statement of appositional versus synechial closure.
- A thin fundus description ("tessellated" only), jumping to the pathology, or a generic purpose for the fundus examination (papilloedema when the blood pressure was 220/110).
- Missing material: no visual field report with a glaucoma case, no primary-position photograph, no anterior-segment pictures, no scars looked for after trauma or surgery, and no search for the break.
- Unsafe or wrong management: digital tonometry in a perforated cornea, glue for a large perforation, steroids started before the infection was controlled, no syringing, antibiotic monotherapy, and no pressure monitoring with steroids.
- Answering a different question, drifting to assumed cases, guessing instead of saying "pardon", or going quiet when asked.
- Overrunning the time: 8 minutes against 5 for the presentation.
:::

### Case 1 — Primary angle-closure glaucoma with cataract, both eyes [04:24–37:14]
- **Patient:** a 60-year-old woman. She has had decreasing vision in both eyes for 3 years, with intermittent left-sided eye pain and headache for 2 years.
- **Candidate's diagnosis [11:59–12:13]:** both eyes primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG); both eyes compound hypermetropic astigmatism, against the rule; both eyes immature senile cataract; type 2 diabetes mellitus; systemic hypertension.
- **Examiners' preferred line:** no model line was dictated. The second examiner restated the diagnosis as below and asked the candidate to assume nuclear sclerosis grade 2–3 [31:08, 32:15–32:27]. The first examiner reminded the candidate that a glaucoma diagnosis needs a documented field defect [34:44–35:07].

:::say
Bilateral primary angle closure glaucoma with senile cataract.
:::

**What was criticised**
- The history was fragmented. The decrease of vision should have been described more fully and clearly. [12:56–13:09]
- The complaint was reworded into medical terms ("rainbow halos"). It should have been in the patient's words, such as "coloured rings around the light". [13:43–14:09]
- There was no separate optical (spectacle) history, which is essential in a long case. Add the optical history and the drug history. [14:16–14:38, 18:09–18:13, 25:04–25:08]
- No differential diagnosis was voiced from the history. Reach a provisional conclusion before you start the examination. [14:41–14:59]
- The term "cataract" was too vague. Say "senile cataract". [15:15–15:20]
- The negative history items could not be defended: redness, diplopia, nausea, vomiting and photophobia, seizures, and trauma. Seizures were called not relevant. [21:36–24:00, 23:25–23:27]
- The past history lacked a drug history. Asthma and cardiac disease matter for steroid-induced cataract and for contraindicated drugs. [24:03–25:00]
- The history was not driven by differentials. The examiner said it had "gone retrospective", tailored to PACG. [25:12–26:14, 26:24–26:50]
- The candidate listed negatives unrelated to the case. [27:11–27:21]
- The lens grading was not given. [32:20–32:23]
- No visual field report, or other glaucoma report, was shown with the case. A glaucoma diagnosis should be backed by a documented field defect. [34:22–35:20]
- The presentation took 8 minutes against the 5 allotted. [12:22]
- Two inconsistencies were not raised by the examiners, but check your own case for the same. The complaint said 3 years while the history of present illness said one year. Closure was described as more than 180° in the summary but more than 270° in the viva. [04:24–12:22, 32:59–33:05]

**On your cards:** G4 (primary angle-closure disease), with G9 (gonioscopy), G10 (Humphrey visual field), G8 (applanation tonometry) and GX (treatment toolkit).

### Case 2 — Left central retinal vein occlusion with iris neovascularisation and neovascular glaucoma [39:08–1:16:30]
- **Patient:** a 67-year-old man. He has had defective vision in the left eye for one week, on top of a sudden loss 8 months earlier that was never worked up.
- **Candidate's diagnosis [46:40–46:50]:** both eyes senile immature cataract; left eye ischaemic central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO) with macular oedema and neovascular glaucoma. In the discussion the candidate called the glaucoma "secondary closed-angle glaucoma due to neovascularisation", and the examiner accepted that wording [1:10:15].
- **Examiners' preferred line:** an examiner restated the diagnosis without correcting it [47:41–47:52]. The second examiner felt the eye was probably non-ischaemic at the start and became ischaemic with time, which explains the few cotton-wool spots [1:16:08]. The vessels on the disc were judged to be collaterals, not new vessels [1:11:37].

:::say
Left eye central retinal vein occlusion with neovascularisation of the iris and neovascular glaucoma. The glaucoma is secondary closed-angle glaucoma due to neovascularisation.
:::

**What was criticised**
- The refractive error was given only as numbers. It should be named when asked. [49:57–50:21]
- The lids, globe, conjunctiva and anterior chamber findings relevant to systemic causes were not volunteered, so the examiner prompted for them. [51:30–53:36]
- No retinal neovascularisation was mentioned, and the candidate could not explain why the iris is involved without new vessels on the disc or elsewhere. [54:35–56:44]
- The history time-line was muddled: "sudden onset 8 months", then "progressive", then "a few days". Durations were vague. [56:56–57:45]
- The candidate did not volunteer the corneal and pressure-related reasons for the sudden drop in vision. [58:00–59:05]
- The gonioscopy report was incomplete. Say that indentation gonioscopy was done and whether the closure was appositional or synechial; the candidate wavered. [59:34–1:00:25]
- The candidate could not define Gunn sign, and knew the arteriovenous-crossing changes poorly. [1:05:08–1:05:20]
- The candidate could not at first show artery and vein on the picture. [1:04:44–1:04:58]
- The candidate was slow to say why the disc matters in a vein occlusion (risk factors, collaterals versus new vessels). [1:03:00–1:04:30]
- Haemorrhage descriptions lacked the basics (flame, dot, blot, microaneurysm), and the "ischaemic" label did not match the few cotton-wool spots. [1:05:56–1:06:50]
- The candidate gave no clear generic name with a dose for the anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) injection. [1:11:57–1:12:20]
- The investigation list was cut short before discussion began. This is not a criticism, only how the long case ran. [46:55–47:17]
- Praise: the presentation was "very nice and vivid" [47:34–47:36], and the sequence of the fundus description (disc, macula, vessels, background) was approved [1:05:32–1:05:45].

**On your cards:** G5 (neovascular glaucoma), G7 (CRVO) and G9 (gonioscopy with indentation). The retinal side of CRVO is not yet built — RETINA.

### Case 3 — Axial proptosis of the right eye with disc oedema [1:17:29–1:41:20]
- **Patient:** a 34-year-old man. He has had painless, gradual forward protrusion of the right eye for 1 year and a mild dull ache behind the eye for 3 months.
- **Candidate's diagnosis [1:22:00–1:22:49]:** axial proptosis with compressive optic neuropathy. The first differential was thyroid orbitopathy, then intraconal masses (cavernous haemangioma, optic nerve meningioma, optic nerve glioma or schwannoma [?], circumscribed lymphangioma, orbital lymphoma).
- **Examiners' preferred line:** no model line was dictated. The examiner did not accept thyroid disease [1:25:36–1:26:11, 1:33:06]. The picture was an intraconal mass causing axial proptosis in a young man, with normal vision, no postural variation and no inflammation. The scan showed a globular, homogeneous intraconal mass [1:35:44–1:37:32]. Lymphoma was thought unlikely [1:38:00].

**What was criticised**
- The history should have been briefer. Skip the normals and write "nothing contributory" for the systemic examination. [1:17:11–1:17:28]
- Retropulsion was called "positive" and defined as the globe going in easily. The correct wording is resistance to retropulsion. [1:26:11–1:26:44]
- No thyroid eye sign was seen, and the examiner added Möbius sign (difficulty in convergence) as an early sign to look for. [1:25:11–1:25:36]
- The candidate had not asked about pets or parasite exposure. [1:36:25–1:36:34]
- Thyroid was the natural first thought, but the history and examination did not fit it: no inflammation, no lid retraction, axial proptosis and full movements. [1:25:36–1:26:11, 1:27:23–1:27:41]
- The candidate preferred magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), but the examiner said computed tomography (CT) was enough. [1:28:50–1:29:30]
- The candidate did not know the simple Luedde exophthalmometer. [1:32:08–1:32:35]
- For colour vision the candidate named a chart-based test (probably Ishihara [?]). The examiner wanted colour desaturation with a red or green object. [1:39:24–1:40:24]
- The CT would not share on screen. The examiner told the candidate to keep answering while it loaded. [1:34:24–1:34:33]
- Overall, the candidate "answered almost all the questions very well". [1:41:26–1:41:33]

**On your cards:** not yet built — OCULOPLASTY.

### Case 4 — Left third nerve palsy [1:48:14–2:17:27]
- **Patient:** a 73-year-old man. He has had sudden painless double vision for 7 days and severe drooping of the left upper lid for 3 days.
- **Candidate's diagnosis [1:58:15]:** isolated, pupil-sparing, partial oculomotor palsy of the left eye, probably due to microvascular ischaemia from uncontrolled diabetes and hypertension, with ptosis. The candidate added senile cataract and compound hypermetropic astigmatism, against the rule, in both eyes.
- **Examiners' preferred line:** no model line was dictated. They accepted the diagnosis, asked for the ptosis to be graded as severe and typed as neurogenic, and accepted the cause as an infarct secondary to ischaemia [2:01:42, 2:02:08–2:02:19, 2:14:58]. The wording they accepted:

:::say
Left eye isolated, pupil-sparing, partial oculomotor palsy, probably due to microvascular ischaemia from uncontrolled diabetes and hypertension, with severe neurogenic ptosis.
:::

**What was criticised**
- The purpose of the fundus examination was given as "rule out papilloedema". With a blood pressure of 220/110, the first thing to look for is hypertensive change. Tie the fundus to the findings already presented. [1:59:15–2:00:20]
- On the diagnosis slide the examiner challenged "severe ptosis" and asked how ptosis is graded. The candidate asked what the 2 mm figure meant instead of answering, and lost marks for not knowing the grading by heart. The second examiner restated the grades and the numbers (margin reflex distance 1 of −1 mm, fissure height difference of 5 mm) and accepted "severe". [2:00:22–2:01:56]
- The type of ptosis was not stated. The answer is neurogenic ptosis. [2:01:58–2:02:19]
- A slide headed "muscular skeletal system" was out of place. Use "neurological evaluation: no sensory-motor deficit, deep tendon reflexes grossly normal". [2:02:55–2:03:15]
- The primary-position photograph was missing from the slides. This was the candidate's own apology, and the examiners did not comment. [1:48:50]
- Observation, not a criticism: the candidate refused to agree to the examiner's bait of a nuclear lesion, and the examiner approved. [2:12:09–2:12:20]
- Praise: "a super first presentation" and "excellent answering". [2:15:51–2:17:15]

**On your cards:** not yet built — NERVES.

### Case 5 — Perforated right corneal ulcer: peripheral ulcerative keratitis or fungal keratitis [2:17:49–2:48:08]
- **Patient:** a 55-year-old woman. She has had redness, pain, watering and photophobia in the right eye for 2 weeks. That morning she injured the eye with her own finger and lost vision suddenly, with severe pain.
- **Candidate's diagnosis [2:24:21]:** right eye corneal ulcer with perforation, and systemic hypertension. When pushed, the candidate said the aetiology could be infective or inflammatory (autoimmune) and the perforation could be traumatic or from the ulcer itself [2:25:44–2:26:58].
- **Examiners' preferred line:** the second examiner read it as shown below [2:27:26–2:27:50]. The first examiner said to keep keratomycosis (fungal keratitis) in mind and later treated it as more likely [2:31:43–2:32:15, 2:41:28]. For management the working frames became "peripheral ulcerative keratitis with secondary bacterial infection" or "keratomycosis with secondary bacterial infection" [2:40:37–2:40:55]. Both said the picture was not clear-cut and would not be set in a real exam [2:33:43–2:34:05].

:::say
Peripheral ulcerative keratitis with a perforation, possibly with a secondary bacterial infection.
:::

**What was criticised**
- A perforated ulcer is not a usual long case, and keratitis is normally a short case. [2:24:41–2:25:25]
- The candidate must give both an aetiological and a morphological diagnosis. The aetiology was vague (infective versus immune). [2:25:27]
- The description of the ulcer was inaccurate ("two-thirds of the cornea" is vague). Describe the cornea first, then the lesion, and concentrate on the cornea, including the pre-corneal tear film, lids and lashes. [2:29:40–2:30:12]
- The diagram needed cross-sections at three areas (oedematous stroma, apparently normal cornea, site of perforation). The lids, lashes and tear film were missing. Blue colour with hatching for stromal oedema was praised. [2:29:10–2:31:38]
- Digital tonometry in a perforated cornea should not be done or mentioned. [2:32:23–2:32:41]
- The adnexa were neglected: lid margin, Meibomian glands, lash and lid-margin position, pre-existing dry eye. [2:32:46–2:33:17]
- The history was somewhat inadequate (delayed presentation, few vessels, circumferential perforation), so the case was not a clear-cut textbook picture. [2:33:43–2:33:55]
- Only the test of regurgitation on pressure over the lacrimal sac (ROPLAS) was done and syringing was not. A third examiner said to syringe in all infectious keratitis. [2:39:55–2:40:30]
- The candidate planned to start steroids after 48 hours of antibiotics in peripheral ulcerative keratitis and was told to control the infection first. [2:41:25–2:41:36]

## How to use this session in your last week
- **Rehearse the opening aloud, in your department's format.** The examiners wanted a full but brief history: exact durations, the complaint in the patient's own words, an optical and drug history, and a differential before the examination (VN2, "Presenting the history"). Say your opening against a timer, using the master proforma card (00_index), and keep your long-case time of 20 + 10 minutes in mind.
- **Be ready to defend every question you ask.** For each glaucoma case, take the negative-history lines on your cards (G4, G1, G5) and say why you ask each one. The first case in this session was picked apart line by line (VN4, "General and presentation").
- **Fundus description is a weak area, so drill it.** Describe in order and never jump to the pathology. The examiner here approved disc, macula, vessels, then background, but your department has its own fundus formula on the master proforma card, so follow that and ask your seniors if the two orders differ. Know the haemorrhage types, artery versus vein, and the full description of a myopic fundus (VN2, "Examination and findings", tips 11, 12 and 27).
- **Retinoscopy and refraction are weak areas, so do one every day.** The examiners said to name the refractive error and not only give the numbers, and that a basic blunder such as calling a plus 4 reading myopia is what hurts (VN2). Say the refractive diagnosis aloud each time, for example "compound hypermetropic astigmatism, against the rule".
- **Rehearse the diagnosis line.** Be specific, rank it as primary, secondary and tertiary, and make the label match what you can show (VN2, "The diagnosis line"). Your thesis is on angle closure, so read the first case in VN3 and the glaucoma group in VN4 against card G4.
- **Settle the seven "Your card says" differences before Friday.** They are in VN4. They cover nausea in the history (G4), pain in open-angle glaucoma (G1), Van Herick grade 3 (G4), the plan for angle closure with cataract (G4), drugs to avoid in neovascular glaucoma (G5), the timing of panretinal photocoagulation (G5), and switching versus adding a drug (G1). Take them to your seniors in the next few days.
- **Practise the bedside habits.** In a real-patient exam you may be asked to elicit signs in front of the patient. Carry colour pencils, a circle template and a transparent scale for drawings, keep a template for each common case type, and write only positives at the bedside (VN2, "Time management").
- **Keep your behaviour simple under pressure.** Answer exactly what is asked, say "pardon" instead of guessing, stay on your own case, and lead the examiner to what you know. The first twenty-one tips under "Before you start" describe a three-year training plan, so skim them and spend the last days on the rest of VN2.
