## Case as presented
**Mrs X, 60, a tailor.** Presentation took **8 minutes** (the examiner timed it).
- **Complaints:** decreased vision in both eyes for 3 years; intermittent left-sided eye pain with headache for 2 years.
- **History of presenting illness:** gradual, painless, progressive blurring in both eyes, worse for near work (newspaper, stitching) with her present glasses. Left-sided headache and eye pain after long hours of work and while watching television at night, with coloured haloes, relieved by rest. Distance tasks (bus boards, recognising people) were still easy.
- **Negative history given:** no redness, diplopia, nausea, vomiting, photophobia, seizures or trauma.
- **Past and family history:** type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM) for 7 years on oral hypoglycaemic agents, well controlled; systemic hypertension (HTN) for 3 years, not on regular treatment. Sister had glaucoma treated surgically.

## Think like this
The examiner rebuilt the case the way he wanted it presented: **form the differential diagnosis from the history before you examine**, then let each step confirm or exclude.
1. **Chronic, gradual, painless loss of vision in both eyes at 60:** senile cataract → chronic simple glaucoma → diabetic retinopathy or maculopathy (she is diabetic) → age-related macular degeneration.
2. **Intermittent one-sided eye pain and headache that began at 58:** uncorrected or under-corrected **hypermetropia** (check whether the glasses are worn all the time — she is a tailor doing long near work) → **systemic hypertension** → intermittent angle closure → **lens-induced glaucoma** → end-stage chronic glaucoma (absolute glaucoma) → corneal causes in this age group (the examiner prompted for the common corneal degeneration). Migraine is unlikely at this age.
3. **Why each negative history:**
   - Redness → uveitis, acute congestive angle closure.
   - Diplopia → intracranial space-occupying lesion (she has headache).
   - Nausea, vomiting, photophobia → migraine (unlikely) and an acute attack.
   - Trauma → traumatic cataract, angle-recession glaucoma, traumatic subluxation of the lens.
   - Bronchial asthma or heart disease → steroid use (cataract, raised pressure) and **contraindication to topical beta-blockers**.
4. **Management in this order:** control the pressure medically first → judge how much the lens adds to the shallow chamber (lens thickness) → decide cataract surgery alone, combined surgery, or sequential surgery from the pressure response.

## Viva: questions and expected answers
Q: From the history alone, what is your differential diagnosis? (0:14:41)
A: For the visual loss — senile cataract, chronic simple glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, age-related macular degeneration. For the pain and headache — uncorrected hypermetropia, systemic hypertension, angle closure, lens-induced glaucoma, absolute glaucoma, corneal disease.
Q: Why did you ask about redness? (0:21:40)
A: To exclude uveitis and an acute congestive attack of angle closure.
Q: Why did you ask about diplopia? (0:22:23)
A: She has one-sided headache: diplopia would point to an intracranial space-occupying lesion.
Q: Why did you ask about trauma? (0:23:31)
A: Traumatic cataract, angle recession with secondary glaucoma, and traumatic lens subluxation can all cause this picture.
Q: Why does a history of bronchial asthma matter in a patient with decreasing vision? (0:24:05)
A: Steroids for asthma cause cataract (and raise pressure); and topical beta-blockers are contraindicated in asthma and some heart disease, which changes your glaucoma treatment.
Q: How do you assess a shallow anterior chamber clinically? (0:28:48)
A: **Torch test:** light from the temporal side; in a shallow chamber the convex iris throws a shadow on the nasal iris. **Van Herick test at the slit lamp:** a thin optical section at the temporal (or nasal) limbus; compare the peripheral chamber depth with corneal thickness — grade 4 ≥ 1 corneal thickness, grade 3 = ¼–½, grade 2 = ¼, grade 1 < ¼. Grades 2 and 1 need gonioscopy; grade 1 is likely to close. (Accepted.) (added) Grade 0 = iridocorneal contact; grades 0–2 are suspicious of angle closure.
Q: She has bilateral PACG and a significant cataract. How will you plan treatment and justify it? (0:31:14)
A: Control the pressure medically first. Assess whether the lens is adding to the angle crowding (lens thickness). If the pressure is controlled, do **cataract surgery alone** and watch how much the pressure falls; add drugs if needed; do glaucoma surgery later as a **sequential** procedure. Consider combined surgery when the pressure is not controlled and synechial closure is extensive (here more than 270°). Know the success rates of combined versus sequential surgery. The examiner's final advice for this patient: plain cataract extraction first, then follow-up.
Q: What is your target pressure? (0:35:40)
A: Moderate damage (CDR 0.8 with field loss) — aim for the **low to mid teens** so that cupping and field loss do not progress.
Q: What precautions will you take during surgery? (0:36:09)
A: The examiner wanted you to name the risk of losing the remaining central field in advanced disease ("wipe-out"). (added) Bring the pressure down before surgery (with intravenous mannitol if needed), decompress the eye slowly, avoid a long hypotony, and watch for aqueous misdirection after surgery in angle-closure eyes.

:::recall
- Differentials come **from the history** — before the examination.
- Pain in a 58–60-year-old: hypermetropia, hypertension, angle closure, lens-induced, absolute glaucoma — not migraine.
- Every negative history = the differential it rules out.
- Bring the **field report**. Target IOP for moderate damage: low to mid teens.
- PACG + cataract: medical control → cataract surgery alone → sequential glaucoma surgery if needed.
