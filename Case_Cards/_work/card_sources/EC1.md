## At a glance
- **Keratoplasty** replaces diseased host cornea with donor cornea: full thickness (**penetrating keratoplasty, PK**; written PKP on the list) or partial thickness (**deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty, DALK**; **endothelial keratoplasty, EK**).
- On the list "KP" means keratoplasty and "TKP" therapeutic keratoplasty. On this card keratic precipitates are always written in full.
- **Rejection** is an immune attack on a graft that was clear and is often reversible; **failure** is permanent loss of clarity from any cause. A quiet opaque graft is a failed graft.
- Why kept: stable signs; one patient covers graft examination, rejection, failure, eye banking and regrafting.
- Diagnosis lines:
  - S.No 10, 32, 33, 50: "Left (or right) eye failed penetrating keratoplasty with an opaque, oedematous graft, sutures in situ or removed, vascularised in __ quadrants; probably late endothelial failure (or after rejection)."
  - S.No 12: "__ eye failed graft after therapeutic keratoplasty for infective keratitis; eye quiet, no active infection."
  - S.No 17: "Left eye after PK and Descemet's stripping endothelial keratoplasty (DSEK), probably DSEK for a failed PK; graft clarity grade __; right eye pseudophakia (posterior chamber intraocular lens, PCIOL)." Confirm the order of surgeries from the records.

## What to look for and present
@widths 32 68
| Look for | What to say or record |
|---|---|
| Original disease | Scar after keratitis or trauma, bullous keratopathy, keratoconus, dystrophy (can recur), herpes simplex (recurs; needs oral acyclovir) |
| Course after surgery | Never cleared = **primary failure**; cleared, then slow painless haze = **late endothelial failure**; cleared, then sudden red, painful eye = **rejection** |
| Number of grafts; drops | Each regraft does worse; rejection often follows stopping or tapering steroid |
| Lids, lashes, tears, sensation | Trichiasis, exposure and dry eye cause epithelial defects and graft infection |
| Graft size and centration | Measure with the slit beam; a graft over 8 mm or an eccentric graft lies near limbal vessels |
| Host–graft junction | Apposition, step or override, gape, leak (Seidel test), vessels or synechiae reaching it |
| Sutures | Interrupted (usually 16), continuous or combined; loose, broken, exposed knots, mucus, vessels along a suture, abscess |
| Graft clarity | Grade 4 (clear) to Grade 1 (opaque); thickness on slit section; Descemet's folds |
| Vascularisation | Superficial or deep, quadrants, reaching the graft; deep vessels in 2 or more quadrants = high risk |
| Rejection signs | Ciliary flush, epithelial rejection line, subepithelial infiltrates, **Khodadoust line** with oedema behind it, anterior chamber cells |
| Epithelium (fluorescein) | Persistent defect, dendrite (herpes), infiltrate (infection) |
| Endothelium; DSEK lenticule | Keratic precipitates, guttae; lenticule edge, attachment, interface haze |
| Anterior chamber, iris, lens | Depth, peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS) to the junction, iridectomy, aphakia or intraocular lens (IOL) type, haptic or vitreous touching the graft |
| Intraocular pressure (IOP) | Tono-Pen or digital; Goldmann is unreliable on an irregular graft; glaucoma is a major cause of failure |
| Visual potential | Projection of rays in four quadrants; B-scan ultrasonography if no fundal view |
| Fellow eye | Keratoconus, Fuchs' guttae or a dystrophy confirms the original disease |

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
"Mrs X, a 45-year-old woman, had penetrating keratoplasty in the left eye 4 years ago for a scar after a corneal ulcer. She saw well for about 2 years and then noticed gradual, painless blurring over 6 months, without redness or photophobia. The left eye has a central 7.5 mm graft with all sutures removed, a well-apposed junction, diffuse stromal oedema with Descemet's folds, a grade 1 opaque graft and superficial vessels reaching the junction in two quadrants. There is no epithelial defect, rejection line or keratic precipitate; the anterior chamber is quiet and IOP is 18 mm Hg by Tono-Pen. Projection of rays is accurate in all quadrants and the right eye is normal."

**Diagnosis: Left eye failed penetrating keratoplasty (late endothelial failure) with an opaque, oedematous graft and two-quadrant superficial vascularisation; good projection; high-risk candidate for regraft.**

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: What is keratoplasty and what are its types?
A: Replacement of diseased host cornea by donor cornea. PK replaces the full thickness; lamellar keratoplasty replaces only the diseased layer: anterior (superficial lamellar, and DALK down to Descemet's membrane) or posterior, that is EK (DSEK, Descemet's stripping automated endothelial keratoplasty (DSAEK), Descemet's membrane endothelial keratoplasty (DMEK)).

Q: What are the indications for keratoplasty?
A: Optical: scars, bullous keratopathy, keratoconus, dystrophies, failed grafts. Tectonic: perforation, descemetocele, severe thinning. Therapeutic: infection not responding to maximal medical treatment. Cosmetic: a white scar in a blind eye (rare). In India the commonest indication is a scar after keratitis; in the West, endothelial disease.

Q: Why does a corneal graft survive better than other allografts?
A: Immune privilege: no blood vessels or lymphatics, few antigen-presenting cells centrally, Fas ligand on the endothelium, and anterior chamber-associated immune deviation (ACAID).

Q: How do DSEK, DSAEK and DMEK differ?
A: In all three the host Descemet's membrane and endothelium are stripped. DSEK transplants a thin layer of posterior stroma with Descemet's membrane and endothelium, cut by hand; DSAEK cuts it with a microkeratome; DMEK transplants Descemet's membrane and endothelium only (about 15 µm): best vision, no hyperopic shift, least rejection, but harder to unfold and detaches more often.

Q: What is DALK and the big-bubble technique?
A: DALK replaces all the stroma and keeps the host endothelium, for keratoconus and stromal scars with a healthy endothelium. In Anwar's technique, after trephining to 75–80% depth, air is injected into the deep stroma to separate Descemet's membrane. No endothelial rejection; risks are Descemet's perforation (convert to PK), double anterior chamber and interface haze.

Q: How do you choose the donor size?
A: Usual graft 7.5–8 mm, punched from the endothelial side 0.25–0.5 mm larger than the host bed (better seal, fewer PAS, more endothelium); same size in keratoconus (less myopia); 0.75–1.0 mm larger in therapeutic, aphakic and paediatric grafts.

### Examination and signs
Q: How do you grade graft clarity?
A: Grade 4: optically clear, fine iris detail seen; Grade 3: faint haze, iris detail visible; Grade 2: marked haze, iris detail only faintly seen; Grade 1: opaque, no view of anterior chamber structures.

Q: What is graft rejection and when can you diagnose it?
A: A cell-mediated (type IV) reaction against donor antigens, with inflammation and oedema in a graft that had been clear for at least 2 weeks (1 week in a regraft). About 30% of PK eyes have at least one episode, mostly within the first year.

Q: Describe the types of rejection and their signs.
A: Epithelial: a raised rejection line (Krachmer's line) moving in from the junction, staining with fluorescein, in a quiet eye. Subepithelial: small white nummular infiltrates in the donor only (Kaye's dots in Baidya; Krachmer spots in Kanski). Stromal: limbal flush, stromal haze and oedema. Endothelial: flush, keratic precipitates scattered or in a Khodadoust line, cells and graft oedema; the most serious type.

Q: What is the Khodadoust line?
A: A line of keratic precipitates (lymphocytes) on the donor endothelium that starts at a vascularised edge, loose suture or synechia and moves centrally over days; the graft behind it is oedematous and the graft ahead clear. It marks endothelial rejection.

Q: How do you tell rejection from failure and other causes of a hazy graft?
A: Rejection: graft clear first, then sudden symptoms, flush, keratic precipitates on the donor only; reverses with steroid. Late failure: gradual, painless, quiet. Herpes: keratic precipitates on host and donor, reduced sensation. Infection: infiltrate, hypopyon. Epithelial downgrowth: grey membrane, raised IOP.

Q: What are interrupted, continuous and combined sutures?
A: All are 10-0 nylon, after four cardinal sutures at 12, 6, 3 and 9 o'clock. Interrupted (16): easier, allow selective removal; used in vascularised, inflamed, therapeutic and paediatric grafts. Continuous: faster, even tension, less inflammation, adjustable, but a break loosens the whole wound. Combined: both.

Q: What do loose sutures signify and when are sutures removed?
A: A loose or broken suture attracts vessels, mucus and bacteria and can trigger rejection or an abscess: remove it at once under antibiotic and steroid cover. Tight interrupted sutures may be removed selectively from about 3 months to reduce astigmatism; all are usually out by 12–18 months (by about 6 months in children).

### Investigations
Q: Which investigations do you do in a failed or rejecting graft?
A: Pachymetry (rising thickness is the earliest sign of endothelial failure and tracks treatment); specular microscopy, or confocal microscopy if oedematous; anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) for lenticule attachment and angle; topography; B-scan; scraping if infection is suspected.

Q: Which donors are not used?
A: Death of unknown cause; rabies, Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease and other slow-virus disease; human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis B and C, syphilis; septicaemia; leukaemia, lymphoma, retinoblastoma and other intraocular tumours; donors under 1 year. Remove the eye within about 6 hours of death.

Q: What storage media do you know?
A: Moist chamber at 4°C, up to 48 hours; McCarey–Kaufman (MK) medium (TC-199, 5% dextran, gentamicin) at 4°C, 2–4 days; Optisol GS at 4°C, up to 14 days; organ culture at 31–37°C, up to 4 weeks; glycerol or gamma-irradiated corneas for tectonic use. Pink-red MK medium is normal; yellow or turbid means contamination.

Q: What endothelial count do you want in donor tissue?
A: At least 2,000 cells/mm² for optical PK and about 2,200–2,500 for EK (handling loses cells). A normal adult has 2,500–3,000; the cornea decompensates below about 400–500. DALK and tectonic tissue need no good endothelium.

### Management
Q: How do you treat endothelial rejection?
A: Admit; prednisolone acetate 1% hourly, dexamethasone ointment at night, a cycloplegic; subconjunctival dexamethasone; if severe, a single pulse of intravenous methylprednisolone 500 mg or oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day; lower IOP; taper over months. Treatment within 8 days of onset gave about 92% survival against 55% when delayed. Oedema beyond about 2 months means failure.

Q: How do you treat epithelial and subepithelial rejection?
A: Prednisolone acetate 1% about 3-hourly, halved every 3 days. They usually settle but show the host is sensitised, so watch for endothelial rejection.

Q: What are the risk factors for rejection?
A: Host: young age, deep vessels in 2 or more quadrants, regraft or previous rejection, herpes simplex, inflammation, atopy, glaucoma, PAS, surface disease. Technical: large or eccentric graft, therapeutic graft, loose or exposed sutures, suture removal, combined surgery. After surgery: stopping steroid early, poor compliance.

Q: How do you protect a high-risk regraft?
A: Quieten the eye and control IOP first; ABO-matched donor; central graft under 8 mm with buried knots; topical steroid for 12–18 months, then low dose; oral cyclosporine (15 mg/kg/day for 2 days, then 7.5 mg/kg/day, adjusted to trough level) or mycophenolate mofetil 750 mg twice daily with blood monitoring; DALK or EK where the disease allows.

Q: What are the causes of graft failure?
A: Primary failure, oedema from day one that never clears: poor donor endothelium, storage problems, rough handling, faulty trephination, early infection. Late failure: rejection (about 30%), slow endothelial loss, glaucoma, infection, persistent epithelial defect, recurrence of disease, trauma.

Q: What causes glaucoma after keratoplasty and how do you treat it?
A: Angle collapse (tight sutures, aphakia), PAS, retained viscoelastic, pupillary block, steroid response, pre-existing glaucoma. Use beta-blockers and alpha-agonists; avoid topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitors in a borderline graft (they weaken the endothelial pump); prostaglandins with care. Surgery: trabeculectomy with mitomycin C, a tube (speeds endothelial loss) or cyclophotocoagulation.

Q: How do you manage astigmatism and visual rehabilitation after PK?
A: Astigmatism averages 4–5 D. With sutures in: topography-guided removal of tight interrupted sutures in the steep meridian, or adjusting a continuous suture. Afterwards: spectacles; rigid gas-permeable (RGP) contact lens, best for irregular astigmatism; relaxing incisions; wedge resection; toric IOL; excimer laser. Treat amblyopia in children.

Q: What do you do for a failed graft?
A: Treat the cause (IOP, inflammation, surface, herpes prophylaxis), then regraft when quiet: repeat PK if the stroma is scarred, or EK under the failed PK if only the endothelium has failed (as at S.No 17). After a therapeutic graft, wait until the eye has been quiet for some months. After two or three failures, consider a keratoprosthesis.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: List the complications of PK.
A: Early: wound leak, flat chamber, epithelial defect, raised IOP, primary failure, endophthalmitis (0.2–0.7%). Late: rejection, astigmatism, glaucoma, suture problems, keratitis, recurrence, wound dehiscence after trauma (the junction stays weak for life), cataract, late endothelial failure.

Q: Which diseases recur in a graft?
A: Herpes simplex (oral acyclovir 400 mg twice daily for at least a year after grafting), fungus after a therapeutic graft (junction, anterior chamber or vitreous), and stromal dystrophies such as Reis–Bücklers, lattice, granular and macular.

Q: What are the complications of DSEK and DMEK?
A: Graft detachment (commonest; rebubble with air), pupillary block from air (inferior iridectomy, partial air release), primary graft failure, endothelial rejection, interface haze, epithelial ingrowth, hyperopic shift of about 1 D after DSEK, glaucoma.

Q: What is Brightbill's prognostic grading?
A: Grade I excellent: keratoconus, central avascular scars, lattice and granular dystrophy. II good: bullous keratopathy, Fuchs' and macular dystrophy, interstitial keratitis. III fair: vascularised cornea, active herpes or bacterial keratitis, failed grade II graft. IV guarded: active fungal keratitis, congenital glaucoma, paediatric graft, staphyloma, severe dry eye. V poor: severe chemical and thermal burns.

Q: What is the prognosis of a regraft?
A: Each regraft does worse: the host is sensitised and the eye has more vessels and glaucoma. Failure from rejection rose from 8% after a first graft to 40% after two or more in one series.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: What did the Collaborative Corneal Transplantation Studies (CCTS) show?
A: In high-risk eyes (deep vessels in 2 or more quadrants or previous rejection), human leucocyte antigen (HLA) matching did not reduce rejection; ABO matching might; intensive steroid, compliance and close follow-up mattered most.

Q: What are the rejection rates after the different keratoplasties?
A: DMEK 1–3%, DSAEK 8–14%, DALK 2–10% (no endothelial rejection), low-risk PK 5–17%, high-risk PK up to 68%.

Q: What is a keratoprosthesis and when is it used?
A: An artificial cornea when any graft will fail: multiple failures, heavy vascularisation, severe limbal stem cell deficiency. Boston type I (polymethyl methacrylate optic in a donor corneal carrier) for a wet eye with a good blink; Boston type II or osteo-odonto-keratoprosthesis for dry, keratinised eyes (Stevens–Johnson syndrome, pemphigoid). Lifelong bandage lens, antibiotics and IOP checks; risks are glaucoma, retroprosthetic membrane, melt and endophthalmitis.

Q: What are the principles of therapeutic keratoplasty?
A: Excise all infected tissue with a 1 mm clear margin; oversize the donor by 0.75–1 mm; interrupted sutures; peripheral iridectomy; anterior chamber wash; send the button for culture and histopathology; continue antimicrobials; no topical steroid for about 2 weeks after a fungal graft. The aim is a quiet globe; an optical regraft follows later.

Q: What newer techniques are used for endothelial failure?
A: DMEK; Rho-kinase inhibitor drops (ripasudil 0.4%, netarsudil 0.02%); injected cultured endothelial cells; femtosecond laser-shaped wounds.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Calling every opaque graft "rejection": rejection needs a previously clear graft and inflammation; a quiet opaque graft is **failure**.
- Not asking how good the vision was after surgery and when it fell: this one question separates primary failure, late failure and rejection.
- Skipping IOP, or using Goldmann on an irregular graft; say "Tono-Pen" and that glaucoma is a leading cause of failure.
- Missing loose sutures and the vessels along them: both a sign and a cause of rejection.
- Using "KP" loosely: on the list it means keratoplasty; say keratic precipitates in full.
- For S.No 17, not explaining why one eye has both PK and DSEK, and not checking the order in the records.
- Offering a regraft without checking projection, B-scan and IOP.
:::

:::recall
- Rejection is immune and often reversible; failure is permanent; rejection is one cause of failure.
- Clear for 2 weeks first; Khodadoust line = endothelial rejection; hourly prednisolone acetate 1%, best within 8 days.
- Donor at least 2,000 cells/mm²; MK medium 2–4 days, Optisol up to 14 days; oversize 0.25–0.5 mm, same size in keratoconus.
- Rejection: DMEK 1–3%, DSAEK 8–14%, high-risk PK up to 68%.
- After repeated failures: keratoprosthesis.
- High-risk graft, mnemonic **RIVAL**: Regraft, Inflammation (and herpes), Vascularisation, Age (young), Large or eccentric graft.
:::
