## At a glance
- **What it is:** a bilateral, asymmetric, progressive, non-inflammatory ectasia: the cornea thins (usually inferior to centre), bulges into a cone and develops irregular myopic astigmatism. Onset at puberty; progression slows after 30 and usually stops by 40.
- **Why it is kept:** eponymous signs, a management ladder from spectacles to cross-linking and keratoplasty, and the numbers examiners love (47 dioptres, 400 µm, 3 mW/cm²).
- **The listed patient is unusual:** Lisch nodules are iris hamartomas of neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1). Confirm what the iris shows (true Lisch nodules, iris naevi or freckles, or Brushfield spots of Down syndrome) and examine the skin before accepting the label.
- **Diagnosis line for S.No 44:** "Both eyes (BE) keratoconus, Amsler–Krumeich stage … right eye (RE) and … left eye (LE), with bilateral iris nodules suggestive of Lisch nodules; to rule out NF1".
- If the spots are Brushfield spots: "BE keratoconus with Brushfield spots; suspect Down syndrome". If the iris is normal, write "BE keratoconus" and say the label needs checking.

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
Mr X, a 19-year-old student, has gradually blurred, distorted vision in both eyes for four years, with frequent changes of glasses, night glare and constant rubbing of itchy eyes since childhood; there has been no sudden painful whitening of either eye. He has several light-brown skin patches, and his mother has similar ones. Best-corrected spectacle vision is 6/12 in the right eye and 6/36 in the left, improving to 6/6 and 6/9 with rigid lenses; retinoscopy shows scissoring in both eyes. Both corneas show inferocentral thinning and protrusion with a Fleischer ring and Vogt striae, the left more advanced with a positive Munson sign; both irides carry several small, smooth, dome-shaped light-brown nodules. Tomography shows mean central keratometry of 49 and 54 dioptres (D) and thinnest pachymetry of 455 and 390 µm, with inferior steepening and abnormal posterior elevation in both eyes.

**Diagnosis: Both eyes keratoconus, Amsler–Krumeich stage 2 in the right eye and stage 3 in the left, with bilateral Lisch nodules and café-au-lait macules suggestive of neurofibromatosis type 1, and allergic eye disease with eye rubbing.**

If the spots are Brushfield spots: **Both eyes keratoconus with Brushfield spots; Down syndrome to be confirmed.**
:::

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Define keratoconus.
A: A bilateral, usually asymmetric, progressive, non-inflammatory ectasia with central or paracentral stromal thinning, apical protrusion and irregular astigmatism (the classic triad). The 2015 global consensus says truly unilateral keratoconus does not exist.

Q: Who gets it?
A: Teenagers, usually noticed around 15; more males (Baidya); about 400 per 100,000, more in South Asians, who present younger, often with vernal disease; 6–18% have an affected relative.

Q: What is the pathogenesis?
A: A genetically susceptible cornea plus mechanical stress, chiefly rubbing: raised matrix metalloproteinases, reduced inhibitors, keratocyte apoptosis driven by interleukin-1, oxidative stress and loss of collagen lamellae.

Q: What is the histological triad?
A: Stromal thinning, breaks in Bowman layer, and iron in the basal epithelium (Fleischer ring); Descemet membrane and endothelium are normal unless hydrops occurs.

Q: Name the associations.
A: Atopy and VKC with rubbing; Down syndrome; Marfan, Ehlers–Danlos, osteogenesis imperfecta, mitral valve prolapse; LCA (oculodigital poking) and retinitis pigmentosa; floppy eyelid syndrome; Crouzon, Turner; NF1 (rare).

Q: What are Lisch nodules?
A: Melanocytic iris hamartomas of NF1: multiple, bilateral, smooth, dome-shaped, tan to brown nodules on the iris surface; appear in childhood, present in nearly all adults with NF1; harmless; two or more is a diagnostic criterion.

Q: How do you handle "keratoconus with Lisch nodules"?
A: Confirm at the slit lamp: Lisch nodules are raised, multiple, bilateral; naevi usually single, flat, darker; Brushfield spots grey-white in a mid-peripheral ring (Down syndrome, a strong keratoconus association); mammillations tiny uniform villi. If true Lisch nodules, assess NF1, then treat the keratoconus on its merits; the link is weak.

Q: What are the diagnostic criteria for NF1?
A: Two or more of: six or more café-au-lait macules (over 5 mm before puberty, over 15 mm after); axillary or inguinal freckling; two neurofibromas or one plexiform; optic pathway glioma; two or more Lisch nodules or choroidal abnormalities; sphenoid wing dysplasia or tibial bowing; a pathogenic NF1 variant (17q11.2, autosomal dominant). One suffices with an affected parent.

### Examination and signs
Q: What is the earliest sign?
A: Scissoring of the retinoscopy reflex; at the slit lamp the Fleischer ring is the earliest (Baidya). Subclinical disease shows only on topography or tomography.

Q: Describe Munson and Rizzuti signs.
A: Munson: V-shaped bulge of the lower lid on downgaze, in moderate to advanced disease. Rizzuti: a penlight from the temporal side is focused by the cone into a sharp beam just inside the nasal limbus.

Q: What is the Fleischer ring?
A: Yellow-brown iron (haemosiderin) in the basal epithelium around the base of the cone; partial or complete; best seen with the cobalt-blue filter; outlines the cone and guides graft size.

Q: What are Vogt striae?
A: Fine vertical stress lines in the posterior stroma and Descemet membrane at the apex, parallel to the cone axis, which vanish briefly on gentle pressure.

Q: Explain the oil-droplet reflex and scissoring.
A: Oil droplet (Charleux): through a dilated pupil the cone shows a dark ring round a bright centre, from total internal reflection. Scissoring: the cone and its periphery differ in power, so near neutralisation two reflex bands move in opposite directions.

Q: What types of cone are there?
A: Nipple: 5 mm or less, central or inferonasal, easiest to fit. Oval: 5–6 mm, inferotemporal, more hydrops. Globus: over 75% of the cornea, hardest to fit.

Q: How do you tell keratoconus from pellucid degeneration and keratoglobus?
A: Keratoconus: puberty, thinning maximal at the apex, Fleischer ring, Vogt striae. Pellucid: 20–40 years, inferior 1–2 mm band of thinning with protrusion above it, against-the-rule astigmatism, "crab-claw" map. Keratoglobus: from birth, limbus-to-limbus thinning, globular cornea, easy perforation.

### Investigations
Q: What are the Placido topography criteria?
A: Rabinowitz: central keratometry (K) over 47.2 D; inferior–superior asymmetry (I–S) over 1.4 D (1.2–1.4 suspect); skew of the steepest radial axes (SRAX) over 21° (asymmetric bow-tie); more than 1 D difference in central K between eyes.

Q: What is KISA%?
A: K × I–S × AST × SRAX × 100 ÷ 300, where AST is the regular corneal astigmatism. Over 100% is keratoconus; 60–100% suspect.

Q: Why use Pentacam, and what does it show?
A: The rotating Scheimpflug camera maps the posterior surface and thickness limbus to limbus, correcting for eye movement. Keratoconus: abnormal posterior elevation (often first), thinnest point under about 480 µm and displaced inferiorly, fast peripheral thickness increase, high Kmax.

Q: What is the Belin–Ambrosio enhanced ectasia display?
A: Elevation against a best-fit sphere that excludes the 3.5–4 mm zone around the thinnest point, exaggerating the cone, plus thickness-progression indices; deviation values are white, yellow (suspect) or red (abnormal), and the final D summarises ectasia risk. Used to screen refractive-surgery candidates.

Q: What is forme fruste keratoconus?
A: Subclinical disease: no clinical signs but abnormal topography or tomography, typically the better eye of a keratoconic patient. It contraindicates LASIK.

Q: Give the Amsler–Krumeich classification.
A: Stage 1: eccentric steepening, myopia or astigmatism under 5 D, mean K 48 D or less. Stage 2: 5–8 D, K 53 D or less, no scar, thinnest 400 µm or more. Stage 3: 8–10 D, K over 53 D, no scar, 300–400 µm. Stage 4: refraction impossible, K over 55 D, central scar, about 200 µm.

Q: How do you document progression?
A: Serial tomography on one machine: Kmax up 1 D or more in 12 months (or 0.5 D in 6), thinning, or rising astigmatism or myopia; also falling best-corrected vision or new contact lens intolerance.

### Management
Q: Outline the management ladder.
A: Stop rubbing and treat allergy; spectacles; contact lenses (soft toric, rigid gas-permeable, hybrid, piggyback, scleral); corneal collagen cross-linking (CXL) once progression is shown; intracorneal ring segments for lens intolerance with a clear centre; keratoplasty: deep anterior lamellar (DALK) preferred, penetrating (PK) for Descemet scarring.

Q: Which contact lens and which fit?
A: Rigid gas-permeable first, fitted by three-point touch (light apical bearing with mid-peripheral touch) for vision, comfort and less scarring; Rose K designs for steep cones; scleral lenses for advanced or intolerant eyes.

Q: How does CXL work?
A: Ultraviolet A (UVA) light excites riboflavin to release reactive oxygen species that bond collagen fibrils in the anterior 300 µm, stiffening the cornea; anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) shows a demarcation line at about that depth.

Q: Describe the Dresden protocol.
A: Remove 7–9 mm of central epithelium; riboflavin 0.1% in 20% dextran every 2–5 minutes for 30 minutes until seen in the anterior chamber; UVA 370 nm at 3 mW/cm² for 30 minutes (5.4 J/cm²), riboflavin continued; bandage contact lens and antibiotic.

Q: Why 400 µm, and what if the cornea is thinner?
A: Riboflavin absorbs most UVA anteriorly, so at 400 µm the endothelium is safe. For 320–400 µm, hypo-osmolar riboflavin (in saline, no dextran) swells the stroma above 400 µm before irradiation.

Q: What are accelerated and epithelium-on CXL?
A: Accelerated: same 5.4 J/cm² in less time (9 mW/cm² for 10 minutes, 18 for 5, 30 for 3; Bunsen–Roscoe law), slightly weaker; pulsed light restores oxygen. Epithelium-on: less pain and infection, but less riboflavin penetration and effect.

Q: Give contraindications and complications of CXL.
A: Contraindications: thinnest under 400 µm (standard protocol), past herpetic keratitis, active infection, severe dry eye, central scar, pregnancy; Kmax over 58 D fails more. Complications: pain, delayed healing, infection, herpes reactivation, sterile infiltrates, haze, endothelial damage, failure.

Q: How do intracorneal ring segments work, and in whom?
A: Polymethyl methacrylate arcs (Intacs, hexagonal; Ferrara, triangular) at about 75% depth shorten the arc and flatten the centre 2–3 D. Need a clear centre, at least 450 µm at the track, K 46–60 D, contact lens failure. Risks: extrusion, migration, infection, melt, vascularisation.

Q: DALK or PK?
A: DALK (big bubble: air at 60–80% depth bares Descemet membrane) keeps the host endothelium: no endothelial rejection, shorter steroids, stronger globe. PK for post-hydrops Descemet scars or perforated DALK; success 90–95%, as the cornea is avascular, uninflamed, with healthy endothelium.

Q: What is Urrets-Zavalia syndrome?
A: A fixed dilated pupil with iris atrophy after keratoplasty for keratoconus, attributed to iris ischaemia from raised IOP, air or gas in the anterior chamber, or mydriatics.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: What is acute hydrops?
A: A break in Descemet membrane lets aqueous into the stroma: sudden painful blur, a white swollen cone, intrastromal clefts on AS-OCT. Commoner with large cones, rubbing, vernal disease and Down syndrome.

Q: How do you treat acute hydrops?
A: 5% sodium chloride drops and ointment, cycloplegic, topical steroid, antibiotic if epithelium breaks, IOP-lowering drops, shield, no rubbing; clears in 2–4 months. Faster: intracameral air or sulphur hexafluoride 20% or perfluoropropane 14%, with or without compression sutures; graft for a residual scar after 2–3 months.

Q: Why is keratoconus a contraindication to LASIK, and what else excludes it?
A: Flap and stromal ablation weaken an already weak cornea and cause ectasia; photorefractive keratectomy (PRK) is also avoided. Also excluded: forme fruste or abnormal Belin–Ambrosio display, thin cornea, residual bed under 250–300 µm or over 40% tissue altered, unstable refraction, rubbing.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: Name newer options for keratoconus.
A: Topography-guided photorefractive keratectomy with CXL (Athens protocol); corneal allogenic intrastromal ring segments (donor tissue); Bowman layer transplantation for very steep cones; toric phakic lenses once stable; epithelial thickness maps and Corvis biomechanics for early diagnosis.

Q: What is posterior keratoconus?
A: An unrelated congenital, usually unilateral, non-progressive excavation of the posterior corneal surface with stromal haze: anterior segment dysgenesis.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Accepting "Lisch nodules" without looking: say what you actually see and how you separated Lisch nodules from naevi and Brushfield spots.
- Missing Brushfield spots: Down syndrome is a strong association and its patients get hydrops.
- Saying Fleischer ring is in Descemet membrane: it is iron in the basal epithelium (the Kayser–Fleischer ring is at Descemet).
- Recommending cross-linking without documented progression or without checking the thinnest pachymetry against 400 µm.
- Calling acute hydrops an emergency for keratoplasty: treat medically or with air or gas first.
- Forgetting to examine the tarsal conjunctiva and to advise against eye rubbing.
- Offering LASIK, PRK or ring segments to a scarred stage 4 cornea.
:::
