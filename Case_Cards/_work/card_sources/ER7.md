## At a glance
- **High myopia:** a spherical equivalent of −6.00 dioptres (D) or more, or an axial length over 26 mm (normal adult 22.5–24.5 mm; each extra 1 mm adds about 3 D).
- **Pathological myopia:** excessive axial elongation with degenerative change in the posterior segment — myopic maculopathy of META-PM (Meta-Analysis for Pathologic Myopia) category 2 or more, a "plus" lesion, or a posterior staphyloma. It is progressive, often −6 to −25 D, with a strong genetic and Asian predilection.
- **Why examiners keep it:** a fundus full of named signs (tessellation, crescent, lacquer cracks, Foster–Fuchs spot, staphyloma), a modern classification, and questions that range into choroidal neovascularisation (CNV), retinal detachment (RD), glaucoma, refractive surgery and cataract surgery.
- **S.No 41:** "Both eyes pathological myopia (right eye −__ D, left eye −__ D; axial lengths __ and __ mm) with myopic maculopathy, META-PM category __ (+ lacquer cracks or Fuchs spot if present), posterior staphyloma, tilted discs with temporal crescents and peripapillary atrophy; no active CNV; retina attached."
- **Always state:** best-corrected acuity, the category in each eye, any plus lesion, activity of CNV, the periphery and the intraocular pressure (IOP).

## What to look for and present
@widths 32 68
| Look for | What to say or record |
|---|---|
| Myopia history | Age at first glasses, rate of progression, current power, contact lenses, refractive surgery, family history (genetic tendency). |
| Recent symptoms | Sudden central blur or distortion = CNV until proved otherwise; gradual distortion = traction maculopathy; flashes, floaters, curtain = RD; glare = cataract. |
| Syndromic clues | Tall with long limbs (Marfan), joint laxity and cleft palate (Stickler), hearing loss; prematurity. |
| Vision and refraction | Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) with the actual prescription, near vision, retinoscopy (against movement; dull, slow reflex in very high myopia), keratometry and axial length. |
| Anterior segment and IOP | Deep anterior chamber, large pupil; nuclear or posterior subcapsular cataract; pigment on endothelium (pigment dispersion); IOP and its measurement errors. |
| Disc | Large, tilted, oval disc; temporal crescent (myopic conus) with peripapillary atrophy; supertraction crescent on the nasal side; shallow cup that is hard to judge. |
| Posterior pole | Tessellated (tigroid) fundus; diffuse or patchy atrophy; lacquer cracks; Foster–Fuchs spot; haemorrhage or a grey CNV membrane; macular atrophy. |
| Staphyloma | Edge seen as a curved line where vessels bend; whole posterior pole bowed backwards; type by extent (Curtin). |
| Vitreous and periphery | Syneresis, posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) with Weiss ring; lattice, snail-track, white without pressure, atrophic holes, horseshoe tears — examine with indentation. |

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
Mr X, 48, has worn glasses for myopia since the age of 8, with steady progression into his twenties and a slow fall in vision over the past few years, but no recent distortion or flashes. Best-corrected vision is 6/18 in the right eye with minus 14 dioptres and 6/24 in the left with minus 16 dioptres; axial lengths are 29.4 and 30.2 millimetres. The anterior chambers are deep and there is early nuclear sclerosis in both eyes, with normal pressures. Both discs are tilted with large temporal crescents and peripapillary atrophy. Both fundi are tessellated with a posterior staphyloma, yellow lacquer cracks and well-defined patches of chorioretinal atrophy at the posterior pole, and a pigmented Foster–Fuchs spot at the left fovea. There is no haemorrhage or fluid, the retina is attached, and the periphery shows lattice degeneration without holes.

**Diagnosis (S.No 41):** both eyes pathological myopia with myopic maculopathy — category 3 (patchy chorioretinal atrophy) of the international meta-analysis grading of pathologic myopia, with lacquer cracks, a left Foster–Fuchs spot (old scarred choroidal neovascular membrane), posterior staphyloma, tilted discs with temporal crescents, and lattice degeneration; retina attached; early nuclear cataract both eyes.
:::

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Define myopia and grade it.
A: Parallel rays focus in front of the retina with accommodation at rest. Low up to −3 D, moderate −3 to −6 D, high −6 D or more (or axial length over 26 mm).

Q: How does pathological myopia differ from simple (school) myopia?
A: Simple myopia starts in school years, stabilises by 18–20 years, rarely exceeds −6 D and is a variant of normal. Pathological myopia keeps progressing into adult life, reaches −6 to −25 D or more, and causes degenerative change of the sclera, choroid, Bruch's membrane and retina.

Q: What is the pathogenesis of the fundus changes?
A: Excess growth is posterior: the sclera thins and stretches, dragging the choroid, Bruch's membrane and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). The choroid thins (often under 100 µm under the fovea), the choriocapillaris is lost, Bruch's membrane cracks and the posterior wall bulges — giving atrophy, lacquer cracks, CNV and staphyloma.

Q: Give the META-PM classification.
A: Category 0 — no myopic macular lesion; 1 — tessellated fundus only; 2 — diffuse chorioretinal atrophy; 3 — patchy chorioretinal atrophy; 4 — macular atrophy. "Plus" lesions: lacquer cracks, myopic CNV, Foster–Fuchs spot. Pathological myopia = category 2 or more, any plus lesion, or a posterior staphyloma.

Q: Name the secondary causes and syndromes of high myopia.
A: Stickler (COL2A1), Marfan, Ehlers–Danlos, homocystinuria, Knobloch syndrome, congenital stationary night blindness, retinopathy of prematurity, microspherophakia (lenticular myopia), and buckling surgery or keratoconus (secondary).

### Examination and signs
Q: List the fundus signs of pathological myopia.
A: Disc — large, tilted, temporal crescent, peripapillary atrophy, supertraction crescent nasally; posterior pole — tessellation, diffuse and patchy atrophy, lacquer cracks, Foster–Fuchs spot, CNV, staphyloma, foveoschisis, macular hole; periphery — lattice, snail-track, white without pressure, atrophic holes; vitreous — syneresis and early PVD.

Q: Why is the fundus tessellated?
A: Thinning and depigmentation of the RPE and choroid let the large orange choroidal vessels and the dark spaces between them show through, giving a tiger-stripe (tigroid) pattern.

Q: What is a myopic crescent?
A: A white or pigmented crescent, usually temporal, where the choroid and RPE stop short of the disc margin as the globe stretches, exposing sclera (peripapillary atrophy). It is acquired, unlike a congenital inferior crescent, which stays unchanged. On the nasal side the retina may be pulled over the disc (supertraction crescent).

Q: What are lacquer cracks?
A: Yellow-white, irregular, branching lines at the posterior pole — mechanical breaks in the Bruch's membrane–RPE–choriocapillaris complex. A new crack may bleed (a "simple" haemorrhage without CNV that clears). They predispose to patchy atrophy and CNV. On indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) they are late hypofluorescent lines; on fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA), window defects.

Q: What is the Foster–Fuchs spot?
A: A dark, raised pigmented spot at the macula — RPE hyperplasia over a regressed myopic CNV; seen in about 5–10% of pathological myopes. Vision is poor because the scar is foveal.

Q: Describe and classify posterior staphyloma.
A: An outpouching of the posterior wall whose curvature is sharper than the surrounding wall; its edge is seen where vessels bend. Curtin described 10 types: I — posterior pole (commonest), II — macular, III — peripapillary, IV — nasal, V — inferior; VI–X compound. Three-dimensional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) groups them as wide macular, narrow macular, peripapillary, nasal and inferior.

Q: What is myopic traction maculopathy?
A: Traction from vitreous, internal limiting membrane (ILM), vessels and a stiff staphyloma splits the macula: foveoschisis (inner or outer), foveal detachment, lamellar hole, full-thickness macular hole and macular hole RD. Seen in about 9–34% of highly myopic eyes with staphyloma; OCT is essential.

Q: What is a dome-shaped macula?
A: An inward bulge of the macula within a staphyloma, from relatively thick sclera under the fovea; it can cause serous foveal detachment and CNV.

### Investigations
Q: Which investigations would you order?
A: Cycloplegic refraction in the young; keratometry; axial length by optical biometry; OCT with enhanced depth or swept source (schisis, CNV, choroidal thickness, dome-shaped macula); OCT angiography; FFA or ICGA when CNV is suspected; autofluorescence for atrophy; B-scan or MRI for staphyloma; visual fields; indented peripheral examination.

Q: How do FFA and OCT show myopic CNV?
A: FFA: early well-defined hyperfluorescence with mild late leakage (a classic, type 2 membrane). OCT: a hyperreflective lesion above the RPE with little subretinal fluid; OCT angiography shows the vascular net without dye. A lacquer-crack bleed shows no leakage.

### Management
Q: Outline management of S.No 41.
A: Best spectacle or contact lens correction; Amsler self-monitoring with urgent review for new distortion; OCT every 6–12 months; treat CNV with anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) injections; laser to symptomatic tears; manage cataract and glaucoma; low-vision aids; genetic and lifestyle counselling for children.

Q: How do you treat myopic CNV?
A: Intravitreal anti-VEGF — ranibizumab 0.5 mg, aflibercept 2 mg or bevacizumab 1.25 mg: one injection, then monthly review and re-injection only for activity (1 + as needed). Few injections are needed (median 2–4 in the first year). Photodynamic therapy with verteporfin is now second-line, and thermal laser is obsolete.

Q: What did RADIANCE show?
A: Ranibizumab 0.5 mg, re-treated by vision stability or by disease activity, gained about 10.5 letters at 3 months versus about 2 with verteporfin photodynamic therapy, and about 14 letters by 12 months with a median of only 2–4 injections. MYRROR showed a similar gain (about 12 letters at 24 weeks) with aflibercept 2 mg.

Q: How do you manage myopic traction maculopathy?
A: Observe stable schisis with good vision. Operate for falling vision, foveal detachment or a lamellar hole: pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) with fovea-sparing ILM peel (to avoid a postoperative hole) ± gas; a macular buckle for deep staphyloma; macular hole RD needs PPV, inverted ILM flap and gas or silicone oil.

Q: What are the refractive options in high myopia?
A: Spectacles (slight undercorrection; minification and aberrations); contact lenses (larger image, wider field); laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis (LASIK) or small-incision lenticule extraction (SMILE) only within corneal-thickness limits (residual stromal bed at least 250–300 µm, usually up to about −8 to −10 D); phakic intraocular lens (IOL) for higher powers; refractive lens exchange (RLE) mainly in presbyopes.

Q: What is the implantable collamer lens (ICL), and what are its problems?
A: A posterior chamber phakic IOL sitting in the sulcus, correcting up to about −18 D. Needs adequate anterior chamber depth and endothelial count; vault 250–750 µm. Problems: anterior subcapsular cataract (low vault), pupillary block or angle closure (high vault — older models need iridotomy; newer ones have a central port), endothelial loss, pigment dispersion.

Q: Why is RLE risky in a young high myope?
A: It removes accommodation and raises the RD risk (about 2–8% over years), most in young men with long eyes and no PVD. Prefer a phakic IOL until presbyopia.

Q: What are the problems in cataract surgery in high myopia?
A: Biometry — staphyloma makes A-scan unreliable, so use optical biometry and modern formulas (Barrett Universal II, Kane, Haigis, or SRK/T with Wang–Koch axial adjustment) to avoid a hyperopic surprise; low or minus IOL powers; very deep, fluctuating chamber with lens–iris diaphragm retropulsion; weak zonules; needle perforation risk with peribulbar block, so use topical or sub-Tenon; higher RD risk afterwards, especially with capsule rupture or laser capsulotomy.

Q: How do you rehabilitate low vision from myopic maculopathy?
A: Refraction first; near work without glasses or with high adds; hand, stand and electronic magnifiers; telescopes for distance; good lighting, contrast and large print; eccentric viewing training; tints for glare; phone accessibility tools; disability certification and counselling.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: List the complications of pathological myopia.
A: Macular — CNV, atrophy, traction maculopathy, macular hole; RD; early nuclear and posterior subcapsular cataract; glaucoma; vitreous degeneration and haemorrhage; amblyopia (anisometropic); strabismus (myopic strabismus fixus).

Q: What is the RD risk in myopes and why?
A: About 40% of RDs occur in myopes. Reasons: a stretched, thin retina; more lattice (in 8% of people but 40% of RDs) and atrophic holes; earlier PVD; macular holes; vitreous loss in cataract surgery; diffuse chorioretinal atrophy.

Q: Why is glaucoma hard in high myopes?
A: Myopia raises the risk of primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) and pigment dispersion, but tilted oval discs, shallow cups and peripapillary atrophy hide cupping; OCT normative databases exclude long eyes (false "red"); staphyloma and atrophy cause field defects; Schiötz underestimates IOP because of low scleral rigidity, and a cornea thinned by refractive surgery reads low on applanation.

Q: What is the prognosis?
A: Slowly progressive; atrophy (category 3–4) and CNV are the main causes of permanent central loss. Anti-VEGF restores vision in CNV, but chorioretinal atrophy around the scar often erodes it after 5–10 years.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: What is the ATN classification?
A: A 2019 extension of META-PM: Atrophy A0–A4 (as categories 0–4); Traction T0–T5 (none, inner or outer schisis, both, foveal detachment, full-thickness hole, macular hole RD); Neovascular N0, N1 (lacquer cracks), N2a (active CNV), N2s (scar or Fuchs spot).

Q: What is peripapillary intrachoroidal cavitation?
A: A yellow-orange crescent just below the disc in highly myopic eyes — a cavity within the choroid on OCT; it is associated with field defects that mimic glaucoma.

Q: What is myopic strabismus fixus (heavy eye syndrome)?
A: Progressive esotropia and hypotropia in very long eyes: the globe herniates up and out between the superior and lateral recti, displacing the lateral rectus downwards. Treat by the Yokoyama procedure — joining the superior and lateral rectus bellies — with medial rectus recession if needed.

Q: How can myopic progression be slowed in children?
A: Low-dose atropine (0.01–0.05% at night), at least 2 hours outdoors a day, less near work, myopia-control spectacle lenses, dual-focus contact lenses and orthokeratology.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Calling every tessellated fundus pathological — tessellation alone is category 1; pathological myopia needs category 2 or more, a plus lesion or staphyloma.
- Missing active CNV: a new grey lesion or haemorrhage with recent distortion needs OCT that day; a lacquer-crack bleed is the main mimic.
- Describing the temporal crescent as "glaucomatous" or missing the tilted disc when judging cupping.
- Forgetting to examine the periphery with indentation and to ask about flashes and floaters.
- Offering clear lens exchange to a young high myope without mentioning the RD risk.
- Forgetting staphyloma in biometry and a sharp-needle block in a long eye.
- Trusting a Schiötz reading in a high myope.
:::
