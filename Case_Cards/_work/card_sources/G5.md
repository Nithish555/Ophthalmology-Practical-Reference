## At a glance
- **Neovascular glaucoma (NVG)**: a severe secondary glaucoma. A fibrovascular membrane covers the iris and angle after long-standing retinal ischaemia — **proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR)**, **ischaemic central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO)**, **ocular ischaemic syndrome**.
- Kept for its signs: neovascularisation of the iris (NVI) and of the angle (NVA), ectropion uveae, very high intraocular pressure (IOP). Last year's case had applanation tonometry as its task.
- Examiners test: iris and angle **before dilatation**; the vessels in slide order; **stage**; **cause**; anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) → panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) → surgery; drugs to avoid.

## Case format — history
**Chief complaint:** "Mr X, a 62-year-old man, came with complaints of pain, redness and defective vision in the right eye for the past 10 days, and sudden loss of vision in the same eye 3 months ago."

@widths 42 58
| What to ask | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **History of present illness:** pain, redness, haloes, headache, vomiting; bleeding into the eye | Acute angle-closure stage; **hyphaema** from fragile vessels |
| Earlier loss of vision — sudden or gradual? | Sudden: ischaemic CRVO (**"100-day glaucoma"**), central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO). Gradual: diabetic retinopathy, chronic uveitis, long-standing retinal detachment, radiation retinopathy |
| Retinal laser, intravitreal injections; known glaucoma, drops, old records | Prior treatment changes the plan; pre-existing primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) raises IOP more |
| Glasses for hypermetropia; similar attacks | Primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG); chronic angle closure can itself cause NVG (**important in India**) |
| **Negative history:** no trigger (dim light, reading, dilating drops, stress) | Against **primary acute angle closure** |
| No recurrent red eye treated with steroid drops | Rules out **uveitic glaucoma** |
| No injury | Rules out **old trauma** (angle recession) |
| **Past, drug and surgical:** diabetes | **Commonest cause** of NVI (a third of rubeotic eyes) |
| Hypertension, hypercoagulable states | Cause of the ischaemia |
| Carotid or heart disease | **Ocular ischaemic syndrome**; heart disease bars intravenous mannitol |
| Asthma; sulpha allergy | No beta-blocker; no carbonic anhydrase inhibitor (CAI) |
| Cataract surgery (capsule rupture, aphakia), vitrectomy | Lens, capsule and vitreous are **barriers** to angiogenic factors |

- **Diabetes, hypertension**: complications. **Chest**: asthma rules out beta-blockers.

### Special tests
**1. New vessels on the iris — before dilatation** (department slide)
1. No drops; slit lamp at **high magnification**: pupillary margin first, then the iris surface and any iridotomy margin.
2. Describe: **appearance → character → course → arrangement → extent** (clock hours).

Record: "RE: **thin, friable, arborising, irregularly arranged** new vessels at the pupillary margin, extending over the iris from **11 o'clock to 8 o'clock** — suggestive of neovascularisation of the iris."

**2. Applanation tonometry** (card G8)
1. GAT in both eyes before dilatation; note the time.
2. Repeat once the corneal oedema clears.

Record: "Tn by GAT: RE 48, LE 14 mm Hg at 10 am."

**3. Gonioscopy** (card G9)
1. Goldmann **2-mirror** lens, both eyes, before dilatation; defer while the cornea is oedematous.
2. NVA: vessels run from the iris root **across the ciliary body band and scleral spur**, arborising over the trabecular meshwork. Present without NVI in about **12%**.
3. PAS extent in degrees (PRP can still reverse the IOP rise if synechial closure is **not more than 270°**); **4-mirror** indentation separates appositional from synechial closure.

## Differential diagnosis
@widths 24 32 44
| Condition | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Primary acute angle closure | Painful red eye, haloes, high IOP | No NVI, NVA, fibrovascular membrane or ectropion uveae |
| Uveitic glaucoma | Flare, cells, raised IOP; dilated iris vessels | Keratic precipitates; dilated vessels are not rubeosis |
| Fuchs' heterochromic iridocyclitis | Fine angle vessels, spontaneous hyphaema | White, quiet eye; stellate keratic precipitates; heterochromia |
| Iridocorneal endothelial (ICE) syndrome | PAS, distorted iris, raised IOP | Corneal decompensation, corectopia, pseudopolycoria |
| Old trauma (angle recession) | PAS, distorted iris, hyphaema | Injury; recessed angle; no NVI |
| Normal iris vessels (light or atrophic iris) | Visible vessels | Stromal, radial, uniform, unbranched; no leak |

## What you must know
### Definition
**NVG**: severe secondary glaucoma; fibrovascular tissue proliferates over the angle and iris, giving very high IOP, after long-standing, severe retinal ischaemia. **Rubeosis iridis**: new vessels on the iris surface, whatever the angle or IOP. Also called haemorrhagic, thrombotic, congestive or **100th-day** glaucoma.

**Causes**: the three commonest are **diabetic retinopathy**, **ischaemic CRVO** and **ocular ischaemic syndrome**; in India also **chronic angle-closure glaucoma**. Others: RUBEOTIC (Quick recall); also CRAO, sickle cell, Coats', cataract surgery, radiation.

### Classification
**Clinicopathological stages:**

@widths 22 34 16 28
| Stage | NVI and gonioscopy | IOP | Other signs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Prerubeosis** | No NVI or NVA; open angle | Normal | Iris angiography shows early leakage |
| **Pre-glaucoma (rubeosis iridis)** | NVI at the pupil margin; open angle, early NVA | Normal | Usually none |
| **Open-angle glaucoma** | Florid NVI; open angle, florid NVA under a fibrovascular membrane | Raised | Cells, flare, hyphaema |
| **Angle-closure glaucoma** | Vessels less apparent; PAS up to total closure | Persistently high; can reach **60 mm Hg** | Ectropion uveae, glistening iris, corneal oedema, disc damage |

Some add a **burnt-out stage** (total synechial closure, few vessels).

**Wand's classification**: stage 1 vessels at the pupillary margin · 2 up to the collarette · 3 up to the angle · 4 crossing the scleral spur.

### Pathogenesis
- **Retinal hypoxia** → angiogenic factors: **VEGF**, fibroblast growth factor, tumour necrosis factor-α, angiogenin.
- Lens, posterior capsule and vitreous are barriers (and supply vaso-inhibitory factors); capsule breach and vitrectomy remove them.
- Vessels bud from capillaries of the **minor arterial circle** at the pupillary margin; thin-walled and fenestrated, they leak and bleed.
- An invisible **fibrovascular membrane** covers the trabecular meshwork → **open-angle (pretrabecular) glaucoma**; its myofibroblasts contract → PAS, flat iris, **ectropion uveae** → **synechial angle closure**.

### Investigations
@widths 28 30 42
| Test | Why | Expected finding |
|---|---|---|
| Iris fluorescein angiography, fluorophotometry | Earliest sign, before vessels show | Leakage at the pupillary margin |
| Fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) | Extent of ischaemia; plan PRP | Extensive capillary closure; leaking iris vessels |
| Fields; optical coherence tomography (OCT), nerve fibre layer | Known glaucoma | Progression |
| Systemic work-up | Cause; drug safety | Diabetes, hypertension, hypercoagulable state, heart or carotid disease |

### Management
**Goals:** control IOP, stop new vessels, treat the cause, relieve symptoms.

**Step 1 — Medical.** IOP below 40 mm Hg: topical aqueous suppressants. Above **40 mm Hg**: add intravenous mannitol.

@widths 32 22 20 26
| Drug | Strength / dose | Frequency | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Timolol** (beta-blocker) | 0.5% | Twice daily | Not in asthma |
| **Brimonidine** (alpha-2 agonist) | 0.2% | Three times | — |
| **Dorzolamide** (topical CAI) | 2% | Three times | Not in sulpha allergy |
| **Acetazolamide** tablet | 250 mg | Twice daily | Not in sulpha allergy |
| **Mannitol 20%** intravenous | 1–2 g/kg over 20–30 min | Initial control | Not in heart disease, low blood pressure |
| **Prednisolone acetate** | 1% | 6–8 times a day | Inflammation |
| **Atropine** (or homatropine 2%) | 1% | Three times | Pain; cycloplegia is treatment |

**Avoid:** miotics (more inflammation and pain), **prostaglandin analogues** and **adrenaline**.

**Step 2 — Treat the cause** once the cornea clears (gonioscopy and fundus are a must).
- **Bevacizumab 1.25 mg in 0.05 ml**, intravitreal through the pars plana (or intracameral, same dose). NVI regresses within **24–48 hours**, but the effect lasts only weeks, so it is an adjunct to PRP. Give it **2–3 days before** surgery: less bleeding, inflammation and failure.
- **PRP — the treatment of choice**: ablating ischaemic retina cuts VEGF; NVI regresses from 2 weeks, fully by **4–6 weeks**. It can still reverse the IOP rise while synechial closure is **not more than 270°**. Green 532 nm laser, **500 µm**, **0.1 s**; mild white burns one burn-width apart, out to the equator; **1200–1600 burns in 3–4 sittings**.
- **Hazy media**: trans-scleral panretinal cryotherapy, or bevacizumab alone.

**Step 3 — Surgery** when IOP stays high after retinal ablation (extensive PAS).
1. **Visual potential** (rare): after anti-VEGF, **trabeculectomy with mitomycin C (MMC) 0.2–0.4 mg/ml for 2 min** (or 5-fluorouracil 50 mg/ml for 5 min). Poor alone; with pre-operative bevacizumab and/or PRP, success may reach **95%**.
2. **Glaucoma drainage device** (**Ahmed valve**, Molteno, Baerveldt): often **primary** when filtering surgery is likely to fail.
3. **Poor visual potential with pain**: **cyclodestruction** — diode or neodymium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet (Nd:YAG) laser cyclophotocoagulation, cyclocryotherapy.
4. **Blind, painful eye**: **retrobulbar alcohol injection**, or **enucleation** after counselling.

**Follow-up:** every visit — undilated pupil margin and angle at high magnification, IOP, disc; both eyes.

### Complications
Hyphaema, vitreous haemorrhage, optic atrophy. Late: **painful bullous keratopathy**, **complete synechial angle closure**, **intractable glaucoma**.

### Prognosis and counselling
- Potentially blinding. Anti-VEGF with PRP controls IOP only in the open-angle stage; after extensive synechial closure, surgery is often needed and useful vision is rare.
- Control diabetes and hypertension.

### Recent advances
- Intracameral bevacizumab alone can stabilise NVI and control IOP in the early open-angle stage.
- Newer: alpha-interferon, troxerutin, gene transfer of pigment epithelium-derived factor (PEDF).

## Viva questions
### Why did you ask / examine…?
Q: Why did you ask about sudden loss of vision months earlier?
A: Ischaemic CRVO: NVG usually follows in 3–5 months ("100-day glaucoma"); range 2 months to 2 years.

Q: Why did you ask about diabetes?
A: Diabetic retinopathy is the commonest cause of NVI: a third of rubeotic eyes have it.

Q: Why did you ask about carotid and heart disease?
A: Ocular ischaemic syndrome is one of the three commonest causes; heart disease bars intravenous mannitol.

Q: Why did you ask about cataract surgery or vitrectomy?
A: Lens, capsule and vitreous block angiogenic factors; removing them raises the risk.

Q: Why did you look at the iris before dilating?
A: Early vessels are fine and at the pupillary margin; dilatation hides them.

Q: Why gonioscopy when the iris is clean?
A: NVA can be present without NVI in about 12%.

### Diagnosis
Q: What is the first sign of NVG?
A: Clinically, vessel tufts at the pupillary margin; on fluorescein angiography, leakage there.

Q: How do you tell new vessels from normal iris vessels?
A: New: superficial, irregular, varying size, branching, leaky, crossing the scleral spur. Normal: stromal, radial, uniform.

Q: What is Wand's classification?
A: Vessels at the pupillary margin (1), to the collarette (2), to the angle (3), crossing the scleral spur (4).

Q: Why does ectropion uveae occur?
A: The contracting fibrovascular membrane pulls the posterior pigment layer round the pupil margin.

Q: What closes the angle?
A: Myofibroblasts in the fibrovascular membrane contract: PAS and a flat iris.

### Investigations
Q: Why do new vessels leak fluorescein?
A: Gap junctions between endothelial cells and fenestrations in the basement membrane.

Q: Where else does fluorescein leak from the pupillary margin?
A: Pseudoexfoliation (pupillary ruff defects) and Fuchs' heterochromic iridocyclitis.

### Management and surgery
Q: What is the treatment of choice, and how does it work?
A: PRP. Destroying retinal pigment epithelium and photoreceptors cuts oxygen demand, so fewer angiogenic factors are released.

Q: What is the role of bevacizumab?
A: 1.25 mg in 0.05 ml intravitreally; NVI regresses within 48 hours, so PRP and surgery bleed less. The effect lasts weeks.

Q: Which drugs are contraindicated?
A: Miotics (more inflammation and pain), adrenaline, and prostaglandin analogues (more inflammation; the membrane blocks uveoscleral outflow).

Q: Why atropine and a steroid?
A: They reduce inflammation and pain; cycloplegia is treatment even in angle closure.

Q: Which operation would you choose?
A: Visual potential: trabeculectomy with MMC after anti-VEGF, or a drainage device. Poor potential: cyclophotocoagulation. Blind, painful: retrobulbar alcohol or enucleation.

Q: When is PRP replaced by cryotherapy?
A: When hazy media prevent PRP: trans-scleral panretinal cryotherapy.

Q: When will you do prophylactic PRP?
A: PDR; diabetic retinopathy with pupillary-margin leakage before lensectomy or vitrectomy; ischaemic CRVO with extensive ischaemia when follow-up is unreliable.

### Complications
Q: What are the late complications?
A: Painful bullous keratopathy, complete synechial angle closure, intractable glaucoma.

### Recent advances
Q: What newer treatments exist?
A: Anti-VEGF antibodies (bevacizumab), alpha-interferon, troxerutin, PEDF gene transfer.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Dilating, or any drop, before seeing the pupil margin and angle: early NVI is missed.
- No gonioscopy because the iris is clean: NVA occurs alone in 12%.
- Treating it as primary angle closure: miotics, prostaglandins and adrenaline are contraindicated.
- Lowering pressure without naming the cause: PRP is the treatment of choice; anti-VEGF lasts weeks.
- No stage or PAS extent: they decide whether PRP can still reverse the IOP rise (closure not more than 270°).
- Calling radial stromal vessels in a light iris "rubeosis".
:::

## Short-case version
:::short
**Spot diagnosis:** rubeosis iridis — new vessels at the pupillary margin and on the iris surface.

**Focused examination:**
1. Before any drop — **PART B**: Pupillary reflex, Angle, Rubeosis, Tension, Best-corrected visual acuity.
2. Slit lamp, **high magnification**, undilated pupil: pupillary margin, iris surface; describe in slide order.
3. **Gonioscopy**: NVA crossing the scleral spur; PAS extent.
4. **IOP** by GAT: normal IOP means the pre-glaucoma stage.
5. **Fundus** after dilatation for the cause: CRVO, PDR, CRAO.

**Three signs to show:**
1. New vessel **tufts at the pupillary margin**.
2. **Irregular, branching, non-radial** surface vessels.
3. **NVA crossing the scleral spur** (ectropion uveae when advanced).

"Right eye rubeosis iridis, pre-glaucoma stage, secondary to proliferative diabetic retinopathy."
:::

## Say-it script
:::say
My patient, a sixty-two-year-old man with diabetes and hypertension, came with pain, redness and defective vision in the right eye for ten days, with haloes and vomiting, three months after sudden loss of vision in that eye. There is no history of injury, eye surgery or steroid drops.

In the right eye, vision is hand movements, with circumciliary congestion, mild corneal oedema and flare. Before dilatation, I saw thin, friable, arborising, irregularly arranged new vessels at the pupillary margin from eleven to eight o'clock, with ectropion uveae and a relative afferent pupillary defect. Intraocular pressure by Goldmann applanation is forty-eight millimetres of mercury in the right eye and fourteen in the left at ten in the morning. Gonioscopy shows peripheral anterior synechiae superiorly and inferiorly, and new vessels crossing the scleral spur elsewhere. The fundus shows an old central retinal vein occlusion.

My diagnosis is right eye neovascular glaucoma, angle-closure stage, secondary to ischaemic central retinal vein occlusion, with uncontrolled intraocular pressure; left eye normal.

I would lower the pressure with timolol, brimonidine, dorzolamide, oral acetazolamide and intravenous mannitol, and give prednisolone acetate and atropine, avoiding miotics and prostaglandin analogues. Once the cornea clears, I would do fundus fluorescein angiography, give intravitreal bevacizumab, then panretinal photocoagulation. If the pressure stays high: a glaucoma drainage device or trabeculectomy with mitomycin C, or cyclophotocoagulation if the visual potential is poor.
:::

## Quick recall
:::recall
- Commonest causes: **diabetic retinopathy**, **ischaemic CRVO**, **ocular ischaemic syndrome**.
- After CRVO: **"100-day glaucoma"** (usually 3–5 months).
- Vessels start at the **pupillary margin**; NVA without NVI in **12%**.
- **PRP**: treatment of choice; reverses the IOP rise only while synechial closure is **not more than 270°**; **1200–1600 burns**.
- **Bevacizumab 1.25 mg/0.05 ml**: NVI regresses in **24–48 hours**; **2–3 days** before surgery.
- IOP above **40 mm Hg**: mannitol 20%, 1–2 g/kg intravenously.
- **Avoid** miotics, prostaglandin analogues, adrenaline.

**Mnemonic:** **RUBEOTIC** (causes) — **R**etinopathy and **R**etinal vein occlusion (PDR, CRVO), **R**etinal detachment · **U**veitis · **B**ranch retinal vein occlusion · **E**ales' disease · **O**cular ischaemic syndrome · **T**rauma · **I**ntraocular tumours (choroidal melanoma) · **C**arotid–cavernous fistula.

**Mnemonic:** **ABCD** (medical treatment) — **A**tropine · **B**eta-blockers · **C**idamex (oral acetazolamide) · **D**examethasone (topical steroid).
:::
