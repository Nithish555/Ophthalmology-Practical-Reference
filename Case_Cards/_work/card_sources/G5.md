## At a glance
- **Neovascular glaucoma (NVG)** is a severe secondary glaucoma: a fibrovascular membrane covers the iris and angle after long-standing retinal ischaemia. The commonest causes are **proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR)**, **ischaemic central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO)** and **ocular ischaemic syndrome**.
- Its signs: neovascularisation of the iris (NVI, **rubeosis iridis**) and angle (NVA), **ectropion uveae** and very high intraocular pressure (IOP). Last year's case had applanation tonometry as its task.
- Examiners test: iris and angle **before any drop**; the **stage**; the **cause**; the ladder; the drugs to avoid.

**Keywords the examiner listens for:** rubeosis iridis · neovascularisation of the angle · ectropion uveae · fibrovascular membrane · peripheral anterior synechiae · ischaemic central retinal vein occlusion · proliferative diabetic retinopathy · ocular ischaemic syndrome · 100-day glaucoma · Wand's classification · panretinal photocoagulation · bevacizumab · glaucoma drainage device · cyclophotocoagulation

## History — positive and negative, with reasons
**Chief complaint:** "Mr X, 62 years, came with pain, redness and defective vision in the right eye for 10 days."

**History of present illness (house wording):** "Patient was apparently normal 3 months ago. Then he had a sudden, painless loss of vision in the right eye. For 10 days the eye has been painful and red, with coloured haloes, headache and vomiting."

### Step 1 — Visual acuity
**Do:** Snellen vision in each eye, with pinhole. Below 1/60: counting fingers, hand movements, or light perception with projection.

**Record:** "VA RE: HM, PL+, PR accurate. LE: 6/9, with pinhole 6/6."

Q: Why is the vision so poor?
A: **Corneal oedema** from the acute pressure rise, and **glaucomatous optic atrophy**, which can leave no perception of light. Macular ischaemia or vitreous haemorrhage adds to it.

Q: Can an NVG eye keep useful vision?
A: Yes. Vision depends on the stage. A well-treated ischaemic retinal event can leave working visual acuity.

### Step 2 — Pupils, before any drop
**Do:** Before any drop: direct and consensual reactions, then the swinging flashlight test. Then the pupil margin on the slit lamp.

**Record:** "RE pupil mildly dilated, oval, fixed; RAPD +; ectropion uveae +. LE round, reacting."

Q: What is ectropion uveae, and why does it occur?
A: **Ectropion uveae** is out-rolling of the posterior pigment epithelium at the pupil margin. The contracting **fibrovascular membrane** pulls the posterior iris layer round onto the front.

Q: What does a relative afferent pupillary defect mean here?
A: The **RAPD** shows the extent of optic-nerve damage. A marked RAPD (over 0.7 log unit) also marks an ischaemic CRVO.

### Step 3 — Slit lamp, before dilating
**Do:** Dim light, high magnification, no drops. Cornea, anterior chamber by Van Herick, then the pupil margin, iris surface and any iridotomy margin.

**Record:** "RE: corneal epithelial oedema; VH grade 3, flare +; thin, friable, arborising, irregular new vessels from 11 to 8 o'clock."

Q: Why must the iris be seen before dilatation?
A: Early **new vessels** are very fine and sit at the pupil margin. After dilatation they may not be found.

Q: How do you tell new vessels from normal iris vessels?
A: **New vessels** are superficial, irregular, varying in size, branching and leaky. Normal vessels are stromal, radial and uniform. Only new vessels cross the scleral spur.

Q: What do flare and hyphaema mean here?
A: **Flare and cells** go with the florid open-angle stage or a recent attack. **Hyphaema** comes from the fragile new vessels.

### Step 4 — Tonometry
**Do:** Goldmann applanation in both eyes before dilatation, noting the time. With corneal oedema, take the true IOP as higher; repeat once it clears.

**Record:** "Tn by GAT: RE 48 mm Hg, LE 14 mm Hg at 10 am."

Q: What pressure do you expect at each stage?
A: **Normal** in prerubeosis and pre-glaucoma, unless POAG or angle closure co-exists. **Raised** in the open-angle stage. **Persistently high**, and it can reach 60 mm Hg, in the angle-closure stage.

Q: Why does the exact reading matter?
A: It sets the first treatment: topical aqueous suppressants below 40 mm Hg; intravenous mannitol added above **40 mm Hg**.

Q: The cornea is oedematous. Which tonometer helps?
A: The **Tonopen** is useful on oedematous and scarred corneas, and it is portable. With Goldmann, I take the true pressure as higher than the reading. I repeat applanation once the oedema clears.

### Step 5 — Gonioscopy
**Do:** Goldmann 2-mirror lens, both eyes, before dilatation; defer it while the cornea is oedematous. Note new vessels and PAS in degrees. Indent with a 4-mirror lens: appositional versus synechial closure.

**Record:** the cross diagram for each eye.

:::gonio
RE | S=Closed | T=SS | I=Closed | N=SS
LE | S=SS | T=SS | I=SS | N=SS
caption: Deepest structure seen (SS = scleral spur). RE: closed by PAS above and below; new vessels cross the spur elsewhere. LE: open in all quadrants
:::

Q: How do you identify new vessels in the angle?
A: **Neovascularisation of the angle** crosses the ciliary body band and scleral spur, then arborises over the meshwork. In about **12%**, it occurs without iris new vessels.

Q: What does gonioscopy show at each stage?
A: Pre-glaucoma: open angle, early NVA. Open-angle stage: florid NVA under the membrane. Angle-closure stage: **peripheral anterior synechiae**, sometimes complete closure.

Q: Why measure the extent of PAS?
A: PRP still reverses the IOP rise only while synechial closure is **not more than 270°**. PAS also mark where a drainage tube must not go.

Q: The cornea is too hazy for gonioscopy. What will you do?
A: **Anhydrous glycerine**, one or two drops, clears the oedema enough for gonioscopy if it cannot wait. **Ultrasound biomicroscopy** images the angle even through corneal oedema or opacity.

### Step 6 — Dilated fundus for the cause
**Do:** Dilate with a cycloplegic, which also treats NVG. Expect a festooned pupil from multifocal posterior synechiae. Then +90 dioptre (D) slit-lamp biomicroscopy and indirect ophthalmoscopy.

**Record:** "RE: media hazy; disc pale, CDR 0.8, optociliary shunts; scattered haemorrhages, suggestive of old ischaemic CRVO."

Q: Why dilate with a cycloplegic when the pressure is high?
A: In NVG, **cycloplegia is a treatment**, even in the angle-closure stage with corneal oedema. It is both therapeutic and diagnostic.

Q: What will you look for in the fundus?
A: The disc: hyperaemia and oedema if acute; pallor and cupping if chronic. Then the cause: **ischaemic central retinal vein occlusion**, **PDR**, CRAO, or the mid-peripheral haemorrhages of ocular ischaemia.

Q: You recorded optociliary shunts. What are they?
A: **Optociliary shunt vessels** are abnormal vessels on the disc. They carry blood from the retinal to the choroidal circulation, a sign of good compensatory circulation after an old CRVO.

### Step 7 — The fellow eye
**Do:** Repeat the undilated pupil margin, tonometry and gonioscopy in the other eye, then its fundus.

**Record:** "LE: no NVI; scleral spur seen in all quadrants; Tn 14 mm Hg; mild non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy."

Q: Why examine the other eye with the same care?
A: Diabetes and hypertension affect both eyes. CRVO is also associated with open-angle glaucoma, so I check its pressure and angle. The **fellow eye** has a CRVO risk of 1% a year, 7% within 5 years.

Q: What would make you treat the fellow eye now?
A: **Proliferative diabetic retinopathy**: it is an indication for prophylactic PRP before rubeosis appears.

## How I will manage
### Aim
Control the intraocular pressure, stop further new vessels, treat the cause and relieve pain.

### The ladder
1. **Lower the pressure now.** IOP below 40 mm Hg: timolol 0.5% twice daily, brimonidine 0.2% and dorzolamide 2% three times daily. Above **40 mm Hg**, I add intravenous mannitol 20%, 1–2 g/kg over 20–30 minutes, if heart and blood pressure allow. Oral acetazolamide 250 mg two to four times a day if there is no sulpha allergy or vomiting.
2. **Settle the eye.** Atropine 1% (or homatropine 2%) three times daily and prednisolone acetate 1% 6–8 times a day ease inflammation and pain. I avoid miotics, prostaglandin analogues and adrenaline.
3. **Find the cause** — trigger: the cornea clears. Gonioscopy and a dilated fundus are then a must.
4. **Anti-VEGF.** Intravitreal **bevacizumab 1.25 mg in 0.05 ml** through the pars plana. Iris new vessels regress in 24–48 hours, but the effect lasts only weeks. So it is an adjunct to PRP, and I give it **2–3 days before** surgery.
5. **PRP — the treatment of choice.** Trigger: ischaemic retina and synechial closure not more than 270°. Settings: 532 nm, **500 µm** and **0.1 s**. Burns are mild white, one burn-width apart, out to the equator. I give **1200–1600 burns in 3–4 sittings**, 3–4 weeks apart.
6. **Hazy media.** Indirect laser, trans-scleral panretinal cryotherapy, or bevacizumab alone. With vitreous haemorrhage: anti-VEGF, then vitrectomy with PRP and the glaucoma operation.
7. **Surgery** — trigger: IOP stays high after retinal ablation, from extensive PAS.
   - Useful vision (rare): anti-VEGF, then trabeculectomy with mitomycin C (MMC) 0.2–0.5 mg/ml for 1–5 minutes. The alternative is 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) 50 mg/ml for 5 minutes, or 5 mg subconjunctival injections after surgery. A **glaucoma drainage device** (Ahmed valve) is often the primary operation when filtering surgery is likely to fail.
   - Poor vision with pain: **cyclophotocoagulation** (diode, or neodymium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet (Nd:YAG) laser) or cyclocryotherapy.
   - Blind, painful eye: retrobulbar alcohol injection, or enucleation after counselling.
8. **Treat the systemic cause** with the physician. Severe symptomatic carotid stenosis goes for endarterectomy.

### Follow-up and counselling
- At every visit: the undilated pupil margin and angle, the IOP and the disc, in both eyes.
- After PRP, new vessels should regress by 4–6 weeks; I add laser if they persist.
- NVG is potentially blinding; useful vision is rarely regained once the angle closes.
- Control sugar and blood pressure; return at once if pain or blurring recurs.

## What you must know
### Definition
"**Neovascular glaucoma** is a severe form of secondary glaucoma characterised by the proliferation of fibrovascular tissue in the anterior chamber angle, giving rise to highly elevated IOP. NVG develops as a result of aggressive iris neovascularisation and angle neovascularisation due to long-standing severe retinal ischaemia."

In plain words: a starved retina makes growth factors, and new vessels close the drain.

- **Rubeosis iridis**: new vessels on the iris surface, regardless of the angle or the presence of glaucoma.
- **Neovascularisation of the angle**: new vessels from the iris root, across the scleral spur, onto the meshwork.
- **Ectropion uveae**: out-rolling of the posterior pigment epithelium of the iris at the pupillary margin.
- **Ocular ischaemic syndrome**: the ophthalmic features that result from chronic hypoperfusion of the entire arterial supply to the eye (carotid or ophthalmic artery occlusion).
- **100-day glaucoma**: NVG about 3 months after a CRVO. Other names: haemorrhagic, thrombotic, rubeotic, congestive glaucoma.

**Causes — most common first.** The top three are diabetic retinopathy, ischaemic CRVO and ocular ischaemic syndrome; in India, chronic angle closure too.
- **Retinal vascular**: diabetic retinopathy, ischaemic CRVO, CRAO, branch occlusions, sickle cell, Eales', Coats', retinopathy of prematurity.
- **Extraocular vascular**: carotid obstruction, giant cell arteritis, Takayasu disease, carotid–cavernous fistula, heart failure.
- **Inflammatory**: chronic uveitis, chronic retinal detachment, endophthalmitis, Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada disease.
- **Tumours**: malignant melanoma, retinoblastoma, metastases.
- **After surgery**: cataract extraction in diabetics, vitrectomy, detachment surgery, Nd:YAG capsulotomy, radiation.

### Classification
**Clinicopathological stages:**

@widths 22 34 16 28
| Stage | NVI and gonioscopy | IOP | Other signs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Prerubeosis** | No NVI or NVA; open angle | Normal | Iris angiography shows early leakage |
| **Pre-glaucoma (rubeosis iridis)** | NVI at the pupil margin; open angle, early NVA | Normal | Usually none |

Some add a **burnt-out stage**: total synechial closure, few visible vessels.

**Wand's classification**: stage 1 pupillary margin · 2 collarette · 3 angle · 4 crossing the scleral spur.

### Pathogenesis
- **Retinal hypoxia** releases **VEGF**, fibroblast growth factor, tumour necrosis factor-α and angiogenin.
- Lens, capsule and vitreous are barriers and give vaso-inhibitory factors; surgery removes them.
- Vessels bud from capillaries of the **minor arterial circle** at the pupil margin. Thin-walled and fenestrated, they leak and bleed.
- An invisible **fibrovascular membrane** covers the meshwork: open-angle (pretrabecular) glaucoma.
- Its myofibroblasts contract: PAS, flat iris, ectropion uveae, then **synechial angle closure**.

### Complications
- **Disease**: hyphaema, vitreous haemorrhage, optic atrophy. Late: **painful bullous keratopathy**, complete synechial closure, intractable glaucoma.
- **Treatment**: PRP (field loss, macular oedema); surgery (hyphaema, hypotony, failure); cyclodestruction (phthisis, sympathetic ophthalmia).

### Prognosis
- **Good**: early stage; synechial closure not more than 270°; prompt PRP with anti-VEGF; well-treated ischaemia, which can leave working vision.
- **Poor**: delayed diagnosis; extensive PAS; optic atrophy. Useful vision is then rare, and trabeculectomy alone does poorly.

### Recent advances
- *(extra)* Intracameral bevacizumab alone can stabilise NVI and control IOP in the early open-angle stage.

### Why did you ask…?
Q: Why did you ask about a sudden loss of vision three months ago?
A: **Ischaemic central retinal vein occlusion**: NVI appears in about half, usually at 2–4 months. NVG follows at 3–5 months, the **100-day glaucoma**.

Q: Why did you ask how long he has had diabetes?
A: **Diabetic retinopathy** is the commonest cause. Longer duration, hypertension and high cholesterol raise the NVG risk in a diabetic eye.

Q: Why did you ask about cataract surgery or vitrectomy?
A: Lens, capsule and vitreous are **mechanical barriers** to angiogenic factors. Capsule rupture, aphakia and vitrectomy remove them.

Q: Why did you ask about blackouts of vision?
A: **Amaurosis fugax** suggests carotid disease and **ocular ischaemic syndrome**, one of the three commonest causes.

### Diagnosis and differentials
Q: What is the first sign of NVG?
A: Clinically, **new-vessel tufts at the pupillary margin**. Fluorescein angiography shows leakage there even earlier.

Q: What are the theories of new-vessel formation in NVG?
A: **Retinal hypoxia** releases angiogenic factors. Tumours release tumour angiogenesis factor. Hypoxic iris vessels dilate chronically. Vitrectomy and lensectomy remove vaso-inhibitory factors.

Q: Where do the new vessels arise?
A: At the **pupillary margin**, from capillaries of the **minor arterial circle**, as endothelial buds that become tufts.

Q: What is Wand's classification?
A: **Wand's classification** stages vessels by reach: 1 pupillary margin, 2 collarette, 3 angle, 4 crossing the scleral spur.

Q: Which types of glaucoma occur in NVG, and what closes the angle?
A: First **open-angle (pretrabecular)**, as the **fibrovascular membrane** covers the meshwork. Later, its **myofibroblasts** contract and pull the iris into **peripheral anterior synechiae**. This is **secondary angle closure without pupillary block**.

Q: Will a laser iridotomy open this angle?
A: No. There is **no pupillary block**: the contracting membrane pulls the iris forward. Neovascular glaucoma is a contraindication to laser iridotomy and to laser trabeculoplasty.

Q: What symptoms suggest ocular ischaemic syndrome?
A: **Ocular angina**, a dull brow ache (40%), with visual loss over weeks to months. Also slow recovery after bright light, amaurosis fugax (10%) and transient neurological deficits.

Q: Why are iris new vessels commoner than disc new vessels after CRVO?
A: The retina is too ischaemic to grow vessels. The **anterior-segment capillaries** are still viable, so they respond.

Q: How often does ischaemic CRVO lead to NVG?
A: Within 6 months: NVI 49%, NVA 37%, **NVG 29%**. The NVG risk reaches 45% at 3 years.

Q: How do you tell ischaemic from non-ischaemic CRVO?
A: **Ischaemic CRVO** has vision of counting fingers or worse and a marked RAPD, over 0.7 log unit. Haemorrhages and disc and macular oedema are more marked, and cotton-wool spots more numerous. Fluorescein angiography shows non-perfusion over **10 disc areas**, and the electroretinogram is always depressed.

Q: How do you tell ocular ischaemic syndrome from CRVO and diabetic retinopathy on the fundus?
A: In **ocular ischaemic syndrome**, the veins are dilated but not tortuous, with dot and blot haemorrhages in the mid-periphery. The disc is normal; angiography shows delayed choroidal filling. CRVO has tortuous veins, flame haemorrhages throughout and a swollen disc. Non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy is always bilateral, with hard exudates and posterior-pole haemorrhages.

### Investigations
Q: Why do new vessels leak fluorescein?
A: They have **gap junctions** between endothelial cells and **fenestrations**. Normal vessels have three coats and an intact blood–aqueous barrier.

Q: How will you confirm ocular ischaemic syndrome?
A: **Carotid colour Doppler**; carotid angiography is the gold standard. FFA shows an arm-to-retina time over 20 seconds.

### Management — drugs
Q: Which drugs are contraindicated, and why?
A: **Miotics** increase inflammation and pain. **Prostaglandin analogues**: the membrane blocks uveoscleral outflow. **Adrenaline** is also contraindicated.

Q: Why atropine and a steroid in a glaucoma?
A: They reduce **inflammation and pain**. Cycloplegia treats NVG even in the angle-closure stage.

Q: Why not oral glycerol?
A: Glycerol is not given to a diabetic, and diabetes is the commonest cause of NVG. I use **intravenous mannitol**.

Q: What do you tell the patient after intravenous mannitol?
A: **Not to get up at once**: it can cause hypotension and, rarely, coning of the brain.

Q: What is the role of anti-VEGF?
A: **Bevacizumab** 1.25 mg in 0.05 ml, intravitreal or intracameral, regresses NVI in 24–48 hours. It cuts bleeding at PRP and surgery, but lasts only weeks. It cannot replace PRP: when it stops, new vessels return more strongly.

Q: Which anti-VEGF agents and doses, and how do they differ?
A: **Bevacizumab** 1.25 mg/0.05 ml is a complete antibody, off-label and much cheaper; it is the most reported in NVG. **Ranibizumab** 0.5 mg/0.05 ml is a humanised antibody fragment against all isoforms of VEGF-A. **Aflibercept** 2 mg/0.05 ml is a fusion protein that also binds VEGF-B and placental growth factor.

### Management — laser and surgery
Q: What is the treatment of choice, and how does it work?
A: **Panretinal photocoagulation**. Destroying retinal pigment epithelium and photoreceptors cuts oxygen demand, so less VEGF is released.

Q: Why 3–4 sittings, not one?
A: One sitting can worsen **macular oedema** and cause pain, exudative or choroidal detachment and **angle-closure glaucoma**.

Q: When will you do prophylactic PRP?
A: In **PDR**, and in diabetics with peripupillary leakage before lensectomy or vitrectomy. In ischaemic CRVO, the **Central Vein Occlusion Study** advised prompt PRP once NVI or NVA appears, not prophylactic PRP. I give it early only when follow-up is unreliable or ischaemia is extensive.

Q: Which operation would you choose?
A: Useful vision: trabeculectomy with MMC after anti-VEGF, or a **glaucoma drainage device**. Poor vision with pain: **cyclophotocoagulation**. Blind: retrobulbar alcohol or enucleation.

Q: Why does trabeculectomy do poorly in NVG, and how do you help it?
A: **NVG is a poor-prognosis eye** for filtering surgery and a high-risk indication for an antimetabolite. Alone, trabeculectomy does poorly. Pre-operative anti-VEGF cuts failure, bleeding and inflammation; with MMC and PRP, success may reach 95%.

Q: When is cyclodestruction indicated?
A: **Pain with poor visual potential**, with rubeosis and glaucoma that cannot be controlled.

Q: How do you give a retrobulbar alcohol injection?
A: **Retrobulbar alcohol** relieves pain for 3–6 months. I first inject 2–3 ml of lignocaine behind the globe. Keeping the needle in place, I change to a 1 ml syringe of 95–100% alcohol. Expect transient ptosis, lid swelling and restricted eye movements.

### Complications
Q: What are the late complications of NVG?
A: **Painful bullous keratopathy**, **complete synechial angle closure** and **intractable glaucoma**.

Q: What are the complications of cyclophotocoagulation?
A: Uveitis, **hypotony**, phthisis, malignant glaucoma and **sympathetic ophthalmia**.

### Recent advances
Q: What newer treatments exist? *(extra)*
A: **Anti-VEGF antibodies** such as bevacizumab, alpha-interferon, troxerutin and pigment epithelium-derived factor gene transfer.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Any drop before seeing the pupil margin and angle: early NVI is missed.
- No gonioscopy because the iris is clean: NVA occurs alone in 12%.
- Treating it as primary angle closure: miotics and prostaglandins are contraindicated.
- Lowering pressure without naming the cause: PRP is the treatment of choice.
- No stage or PAS extent: they decide whether PRP can still work (closure not more than 270°).
- Forgetting the other eye: it may need prophylactic PRP.
- Calling radial stromal vessels in a light iris "rubeosis".
- Offering laser iridotomy for the closed angle: there is no pupillary block.
:::

## Short-case version
### Rubeosis iridis
**The instruction you may get:** "Examine this patient's right iris."

**Spot diagnosis:** rubeosis iridis — fine new vessels at the pupillary margin and on the iris surface.

**Focused examination:**
1. No drops. Torch: pupil reactions and RAPD.
2. Slit lamp, high magnification, undilated: the **pupillary margin first**, for tufts and ectropion uveae.
3. Then the **iris surface** to the root, and any iridotomy margin; count the clock hours.
4. **Gonioscopy**: new vessels crossing the scleral spur; PAS.
5. **IOP** by Goldmann applanation: normal means the pre-glaucoma stage.
6. Ask to dilate and examine the fundus for the cause.

:::say
On slit-lamp examination of the right eye, before dilatation, the iris shows new vessels. They are thin in appearance and friable in character. Their course is arborising. They are irregularly arranged, not radial like normal vessels. They start at the pupillary margin and extend from 11 o'clock to 8 o'clock. There is no ectropion uveae. On gonioscopy, new vessels cross the scleral spur, without peripheral anterior synechiae. The pressure is 16 millimetres of mercury by Goldmann applanation. These features are suggestive of neovascularisation of the iris. My diagnosis is right eye rubeosis iridis, pre-glaucoma stage, probably due to proliferative diabetic retinopathy.
:::

**Three signs to show:**
1. New-vessel **tufts at the pupillary margin**.
2. **Irregular, branching, non-radial** surface vessels, with their extent in clock hours.
3. **New vessels crossing the scleral spur** on gonioscopy.

Q: Define rubeosis iridis.
A: **Rubeosis iridis** means new vessels on the iris surface, regardless of the state of the angle or the presence of glaucoma.

Q: Is rubeosis iridis the same as NVG?
A: No. **Rubeosis iridis** with normal pressure is the pre-glaucoma stage. It becomes NVG when the pressure rises.

Q: What does "PART B" remind you to do?
A: Before dilating, check the **Pupillary reflex, Angle, Rubeosis, Tension** and **Best-corrected visual acuity**.

Q: What else can cause iris new vessels?
A: **Chronic uveitis**, chronic retinal detachment and intraocular tumours such as melanoma and retinoblastoma.

Q: How will you manage this patient?
A: I will find the cause on fundus examination and FFA, then give **PRP**, with anti-VEGF as an adjunct.

## Say-it script
### Opening (about 1 minute)
:::say
My patient, Mr X, is a sixty-two-year-old man. He came with pain, redness and defective vision in the right eye for ten days.

He was apparently normal three months ago, when he had a sudden, painless loss of vision in the right eye. Ten days ago the eye became painful and red, with coloured haloes, headache and vomiting. There was no precipitating factor such as dim light or dilating drops.

There is no history of injury or of recurrent red eye treated with steroid drops. He has had no eye surgery, retinal laser or injections. He has had no brief blackouts of vision and no stroke. There have been no similar attacks in either eye.

He is a known diabetic for twelve years, poorly controlled on tablets, and hypertensive for eight years, on treatment. He has no heart disease, no kidney disease, no asthma and no sulpha allergy. He was started on timolol eye drops twice daily five days ago.
:::

### Closing (2 minutes)
:::say
To summarise, a sixty-two-year-old diabetic and hypertensive man has a painful, red right eye with poor vision for ten days. This followed a sudden loss of vision in that eye three months ago.

The right eye has hand-movements vision, circumciliary congestion, mild corneal oedema and flare. Before dilatation, I saw thin, friable, arborising, irregular new vessels from eleven to eight o'clock. There is ectropion uveae and a relative afferent pupillary defect. By Goldmann applanation at ten in the morning, the pressure is forty-eight millimetres of mercury in the right eye. It is fourteen in the left. Gonioscopy shows peripheral anterior synechiae above and below, with new vessels elsewhere. The fundus shows an old ischaemic central retinal vein occlusion.

My diagnosis is right eye neovascular glaucoma, angle-closure stage, secondary to ischaemic central retinal vein occlusion, with uncontrolled pressure. The left eye has mild non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy.

I will lower the pressure with timolol, brimonidine, dorzolamide, oral acetazolamide and intravenous mannitol. I will add prednisolone acetate and atropine, and avoid miotics. Once the cornea clears, I will do fluorescein angiography and carotid Doppler. I will give intravitreal bevacizumab, then panretinal photocoagulation. If the pressure stays high, I will advise a glaucoma drainage device or trabeculectomy with mitomycin C. If vision is poor and the eye painful, I will offer cyclophotocoagulation.
:::

## Quick recall
:::recall
- Top three causes: **diabetic retinopathy**, **ischaemic CRVO**, **ocular ischaemic syndrome**.
- Ischaemic CRVO: NVI at 2–4 months; NVG at 3–5 months, the **"100-day glaucoma"**.
- Vessels start at the **pupillary margin**; NVA without NVI in **12%**.
- Stages: prerubeosis → pre-glaucoma → open-angle → angle-closure.
- Above **40 mm Hg**: mannitol 20%, 1–2 g/kg intravenously over 20–30 minutes.
- **Avoid** miotics, prostaglandin analogues, adrenaline.
- **Bevacizumab 1.25 mg/0.05 ml**: regression in **24–48 hours**; **2–3 days** before surgery.
- **PRP**: 500 µm, 0.1 s, **1200–1600 burns in 3–4 sittings**; closure **not more than 270°**.

**Mnemonic:** **RUBEOTIC** (causes) — **R**etinopathy, **R**etinal vein occlusion, **R**etinal detachment · **U**veitis · **B**ranch retinal vein occlusion · **E**ales' disease · **O**cular ischaemic syndrome · **T**rauma · **I**ntraocular tumours · **C**arotid–cavernous fistula.

**Mnemonic:** **ABCD** (medical treatment) — **A**tropine · **B**eta-blockers · **C**idamex (oral acetazolamide) · **D**examethasone (topical steroid).

**Mnemonic:** **PART B** (before dilating) — **P**upillary reflex · **A**ngle · **R**ubeosis · **T**ension · **B**est-corrected visual acuity.
:::
