## At a glance
- **Bacterial keratitis**: an epithelial defect with stromal suppuration caused by bacteria. It is an ocular emergency: Pseudomonas and pneumococcus can perforate the cornea within 48–72 hours.
- **Hypopyon**: leucocytes and fibrin settled in the anterior chamber (AC), produced by toxin-driven iritis. In a bacterial ulcer it is **sterile and mobile** until the cornea perforates.
- Why kept: a classic case; examiners test the ulcer description, bacterial versus fungal, scraping and media, fortified drops, steroids and the management of perforation.
- Diagnosis line, S.No 53: "Left eye central (or paracentral) bacterial corneal ulcer, __ × __ mm, dense stromal infiltrate to __ depth, surrounding oedema, __ mm mobile hypopyon, no perforation, Seidel negative, lacrimal sac __; probably pneumococcal (or Pseudomonas); severe (or moderate)."
- Always give fungal keratitis as the main differential and say that the scraping report decides.

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
"Mr X, a 50-year-old farmer, has had pain, redness, watering and blurred vision in the left eye for 4 days after a fingernail injury, treated with drops from a pharmacy and no steroid. The left eye has lid oedema, ciliary congestion and a 4 × 3.5 mm central ulcer with a dense cream-white stromal infiltrate to about half the stromal depth, fluffy margins, surrounding stromal oedema and an epithelial defect of the same size. There is a 2 mm hypopyon with a horizontal level that shifts when the head is tilted; there is no thinning, satellite lesion or perforation, and the Seidel test is negative. ROPLAS is negative and corneal sensation is present. The right eye is normal."

**Diagnosis: Left eye central bacterial corneal ulcer (moderate to severe) with mobile hypopyon, no perforation. Plan: scraping for Gram stain, potassium hydroxide (KOH) mount and culture, then fortified cefazolin 5% and tobramycin 1.3% with atropine.**
:::

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Define corneal ulcer and hypopyon.
A: Corneal ulcer: a break in the epithelium with loss of stromal tissue and inflammatory infiltration. Hypopyon: polymorphonuclear leucocytes and fibrin collected in the lower AC, produced by iritis from bacterial toxins.

Q: Why is the hypopyon of a bacterial ulcer sterile?
A: Bacteria cannot cross an intact Descemet's membrane, so the hypopyon is an inflammatory response to toxins and becomes infected only after perforation. Fungal hyphae can penetrate Descemet's membrane, so a fungal hypopyon may contain organisms.

Q: Which bacteria cause keratitis?
A: Gram-positive: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus and epidermidis, Streptococcus pyogenes. Gram-negative: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Moraxella, enteric bacilli (Proteus, Klebsiella, Serratia), Haemophilus, Neisseria. Others: Nocardia and atypical mycobacteria. In India pneumococcus and Pseudomonas are the commonest.

Q: Which organisms can invade an intact epithelium?
A: Neisseria gonorrhoeae and meningitidis, Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Haemophilus influenzae (aegyptius) and Listeria.

Q: What is ulcus serpens?
A: An acute hypopyon ulcer caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae: a central grey-white ulcer that creeps across the cornea with one undermined advancing edge while the other edge heals; early large hypopyon (sometimes haemorrhagic), marked iritis and early perforation. It is classically linked to chronic dacryocystitis.

Q: What are the features of Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus, Moraxella and Nocardia ulcers?
A: Pseudomonas: rapid liquefactive melting, sticky greenish-yellow discharge, ground-glass oedema of the surrounding cornea, ring ulcer, perforation within 48 hours; contact lens wearers. Staphylococcus aureus: well-defined round or oval cream-white infiltrate with little surrounding oedema. Moraxella: indolent inferior ulcer in alcoholics, diabetics and debilitated patients. Nocardia: superficial wreath-like ulcer with pin-head infiltrates.

Q: What are the stages of a corneal ulcer?
A: Infiltration, active ulceration (progression), regression and cicatrisation; the final scar may be a nebula, macula or leucoma depending on depth.

### Examination and signs
Q: How do you differentiate bacterial from fungal keratitis?
A: Bacterial: rapid, symptoms more than signs, purulent discharge, wet ulcer with sharp epithelial edge, dense suppuration and surrounding oedema, mobile hypopyon with a horizontal level. Fungal: vegetable trauma, slow, signs more than symptoms, dry raised grey-white infiltrate with feathery margins, satellite lesions, immune ring, endothelial plaque, pigment in dematiaceous fungi, thick fixed hypopyon with a convex upper border.

Q: How do Acanthamoeba and herpetic keratitis differ from bacterial keratitis?
A: Acanthamoeba: contact lens or muddy water, pain out of proportion, pseudodendrites, radial keratoneuritis, ring infiltrate, waxing and waning course; needs calcofluor white and non-nutrient agar. Herpes simplex: recurrences, dendritic or geographic ulcer with terminal bulbs, reduced sensation, disciform keratitis with keratic precipitates; hypopyon is uncommon.

Q: How do you test the mobility of a hypopyon and what does it tell you?
A: Note the level, ask the patient to tilt the head or lie on one side for about 10 minutes, and look again: a fluid bacterial hypopyon shifts and levels again; a thick fungal one stays fixed. Measure the height in mm at the slit lamp; a falling height is a sign of healing.

Q: What signs show the ulcer is healing or worsening?
A: Healing: less pain, blunted, well-defined margins, smaller and less dense infiltrate, less oedema and AC reaction, falling hypopyon, re-epithelialisation, vessels growing in. Worsening: more pain, enlarging dense infiltrate, thinning, rising hypopyon, new satellites, scleral spread, perforation.

Q: Why do you check the lacrimal sac and syringe?
A: Chronic dacryocystitis is a reservoir of pneumococcus and causes ulcus serpens and recurrence. Press over the sac and syringe; if mucopus regurgitates, treat the sac by dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR), or dacryocystectomy in an emergency, before corneal or intraocular surgery.

### Investigations
Q: How do you take a corneal scraping?
A: Before antibiotics if possible. Topical proparacaine 0.5% (least bactericidal), speculum, slit lamp or microscope; wipe away loose mucus; scrape the base and the advancing edge with a Kimura spatula, a no. 15 blade or a 26 G needle (pneumococcus lies at the edge, Moraxella in the base), avoiding lids and lashes. Make slides first, then inoculate the media in C-shaped streaks without cutting the agar; flame the spatula or change the blade between samples.

Q: Which stains and culture media do you use?
A: Stains: Gram (bacteria), KOH 10% mount (fungi), Giemsa (cells and fungi), calcofluor white or acridine orange under fluorescence (fungi, Acanthamoeba), acid-fast stain (Nocardia, mycobacteria). Media: blood agar (most bacteria and fungi), chocolate agar (Haemophilus, Neisseria, Moraxella), thioglycollate broth (aerobes and anaerobes), brain–heart infusion broth, Sabouraud's dextrose agar (fungi, Nocardia; room temperature), non-nutrient agar with Escherichia coli overlay (Acanthamoeba), Löwenstein–Jensen medium (mycobacteria).

Q: How long are cultures kept and when is a growth significant?
A: Bacterial cultures 7 days, anaerobic up to 14 days, fungal 2 weeks, mycobacterial 4–6 weeks. A growth is significant if the same organism grows on two media, or on one solid medium with a matching smear, or as confluent growth of a known pathogen on one solid medium (Jones criteria).

Q: What do you do if the ulcer is not responding and the culture is negative?
A: Check compliance and drug toxicity; stop antibiotics for 24 hours and scrape again; corneal biopsy with a 2–4 mm skin-biopsy trephine at the edge, split for culture and histopathology; confocal microscopy and polymerase chain reaction (PCR); rethink the diagnosis (fungus, Acanthamoeba, Nocardia, atypical mycobacteria, Pythium, herpes, neurotrophic ulcer).

### Management
Q: What is the empirical treatment?
A: Admit severe ulcers. Fortified cefazolin 5% (50 mg/mL) with fortified tobramycin 1.3% (about 14 mg/mL), or a fourth-generation fluoroquinolone with tobramycin. Loading dose: one drop every 5 minutes for 30 minutes, then every 30–60 minutes day and night for 48 hours, the two drugs 5 minutes apart; taper on clinical response, and change drugs by the culture report only if the ulcer is not improving.

Q: When is fluoroquinolone monotherapy acceptable?
A: In small (under 3 mm), peripheral or mid-peripheral, superficial (under 50% depth) ulcers: moxifloxacin 0.5% or gatifloxacin 0.3% hourly. Central, deep, larger or worsening ulcers need fortified combination therapy. Fluoroquinolones are cheap, stable and less toxic, but older ones cover pneumococcus poorly and resistance is rising.

Q: How do you prepare fortified drops?
A: Cefazolin 5%: dissolve a 500 mg vial in 10 mL of sterile water or artificial tears. Tobramycin (or gentamicin): add 2 mL of injection (80 mg) to a 5 mL bottle of 0.3% drops (15 mg), giving about 13.6 mg/mL. Vancomycin 5% for methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA); ceftazidime 5% for resistant Pseudomonas. Refrigerate; use within about a week.

Q: What are the adjuncts?
A: Atropine 1% two or three times a day (or homatropine 2%) relieves ciliary spasm and pain, prevents posterior synechiae and reduces exudation; oral analgesics; timolol 0.5% or oral acetazolamide for raised IOP; dark glasses. No eye pad (it warms the eye and traps discharge) and no topical steroid at the start.

Q: When are subconjunctival and systemic antibiotics used?
A: Subconjunctival (cefazolin 100 mg, gentamicin 20 mg, vancomycin 25 mg) only when frequent drops are impossible or fortified drops unavailable. Systemic: perforation or impending perforation, scleral spread, endophthalmitis, gonococcal or Haemophilus infection, and children; for example oral ciprofloxacin 750 mg twice daily, or intravenous ceftriaxone for Neisseria.

Q: What did the Steroids for Corneal Ulcers Trial (SCUT) show?
A: In 500 culture-positive bacterial ulcers, prednisolone phosphate 1% added after 48 hours of moxifloxacin gave no overall difference in 3-month best-corrected acuity, scar size or perforation. Ulcers with very poor vision at entry (counting fingers or worse) or central deep ulcers did better with steroid; Nocardia ulcers did worse. So use steroid only after 48 hours of effective antibiotic, with a known sensitive organism that is not fungus, Nocardia or Acanthamoeba, in an improving ulcer.

Q: How do you manage impending or actual perforation?
A: Descemetocele or perforation under 2 mm: cyanoacrylate glue after debriding necrotic tissue and drying the bed, with a bandage contact lens (BCL); multilayered amniotic membrane for small perforations or thinning; tectonic patch graft for 3–5 mm; therapeutic keratoplasty (TPK) for 5 mm or more or uncontrolled infection. Add aqueous suppressants, a shield and systemic antibiotics, and avoid pressure on the eye.

Q: What are the principles of therapeutic keratoplasty?
A: Excise all infected tissue with a 1 mm clear margin; oversize the donor by 0.75–1 mm; interrupted sutures; peripheral iridectomy; AC wash; send the host button for culture and histopathology; continue antibiotics. The aim is to eradicate infection and save the globe; an optical graft can follow later.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: List the complications of a bacterial corneal ulcer.
A: Descemetocele, perforation with iris prolapse, adherent leucoma, anterior staphyloma, corneal fistula, secondary glaucoma (inflammation, peripheral anterior synechiae, pupillary block), complicated or anterior polar cataract, purulent iridocyclitis, endophthalmitis and panophthalmitis, expulsive haemorrhage after perforation, scleritis, phthisis bulbi; on healing, a scar, vessels and irregular astigmatism.

Q: What is a descemetocele and how do you recognise it?
A: Descemet's membrane bulging through the floor of an ulcer after the stroma has melted: a clear, dark-looking blister surrounded by grey infiltrate, which does not take fluorescein while the rim does. It signals imminent perforation: glue with a BCL or a tectonic graft, and lower IOP.

Q: What are the signs of perforation?
A: Sudden relief of pain with a gush of fluid, a shallow or flat AC, a soft eye, iris prolapse or iris plugging the ulcer, a peaked pupil, a positive Seidel test and a falling hypopyon.

Q: What is the prognosis?
A: It depends on the organism (Pseudomonas and pneumococcus are the worst), size, depth, site, delay in treatment, steroid misuse and host factors. Most ulcers heal in 2–3 weeks with a scar; a central ulcer leaves a leucoma that may need an optical keratoplasty once the eye has been quiet for some months; perforation and endophthalmitis carry a poor outlook.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: When do you suspect drug toxicity?
A: When the infiltrate is settling but the epithelial defect will not close, with punctate staining, chemosis and a quiet AC after days of hourly fortified aminoglycoside. Reduce the frequency or change the drug. Ciprofloxacin drops can leave white crystalline deposits in the ulcer bed that are not infection.

Q: What newer treatments and diagnostics are there?
A: Photoactivated chromophore for keratitis–corneal cross-linking (PACK-CXL; riboflavin with ultraviolet A, which kills organisms and resists melting), rose bengal photodynamic therapy, fortified linezolid 0.2% for MRSA, intrastromal antibiotic injection; diagnosis by PCR, matrix-assisted laser desorption ionisation time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry, in vivo confocal microscopy, and anterior segment optical coherence tomography for depth.

Q: What did the Mycotic Ulcer Treatment Trial (MUTT) show, and why does it matter here?
A: For filamentous fungal keratitis, natamycin 5% did better than voriconazole 1%, especially for Fusarium. If the scraping of a "bacterial" ulcer shows hyphae, switch to natamycin.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Calling every hypopyon ulcer bacterial without asking about vegetable trauma and looking for feathery margins and satellites.
- Forgetting to press over the lacrimal sac and syringe; missed dacryocystitis means pneumococcal recurrence.
- Measuring the ulcer once: give the defect and the infiltrate separately, in two meridians, with depth.
- Starting steroid at presentation; SCUT supports it only after 48 hours of effective antibiotic in a known, non-fungal infection.
- Using Goldmann tonometry or pressing on a thin, perforating cornea.
- Gluing a large perforation: glue suits holes under 2 mm; larger ones need a patch graft or TPK.
- Leaving the eye padded: no pad in an infected ulcer.
:::
