## Case as presented
**Mr X, 73, a businessman.**
- **Complaints:** double vision for 7 days; drooping of the left upper lid for 3 days.
- **The diplopia:** sudden and painless; images side by side with one slightly higher; present for distance and near; worse on looking to the right; relieved by closing one eye. Once the lid dropped completely, the diplopia went.
- **Negative history** was long, each item aimed at something:
  - redness, pain, discharge;
  - fatigability or evening worsening (myasthenia);
  - scalp tenderness or jaw claudication (giant cell arteritis);
  - headache, vomiting, loss of consciousness (raised intracranial pressure);
  - hearing, smell, taste, swallowing or speech problems, numbness, weakness, involuntary movements, gait change (other cranial nerves or brainstem).
- **Past history:**
  - a similar episode in the **right eye 5 years ago** (complete ptosis with diplopia, "a nerve problem") that recovered within a year;
  - diabetes for 20 years, tablets **stopped a year ago**, current control unknown;
  - coronary bypass 4 years ago, on anticoagulants;
  - smoker, 1 pack a day for 10 years.
- **Vitals:** **BP 220/110 mm Hg**. Other cranial nerves, motor, sensory and cerebellar examination normal.

## Think like this
1. **Read your own summary slide before the examiner does** — he went straight to the diagnosis slide and the vitals to find mistakes.
2. **Localise a third nerve lesion from the orbit back to the nucleus** (the examiner asked for this order because it is easier to follow):
   - **Orbit:** the nerve has split. The superior division supplies levator and superior rectus; the inferior division supplies medial rectus, inferior rectus, inferior oblique and the pupil. So you see a palsy of one division, often with proptosis or a mass.
   - **Superior orbital fissure and cavernous sinus:** several cranial nerves together (III, IV, V1, V2, VI). Tolosa–Hunt syndrome is painful. Cavernous sinus thrombosis brings fever and proptosis. At the orbital apex the optic nerve joins in.
   - **Subarachnoid space:** **posterior communicating artery aneurysm** (painful, pupil involved); basal meningitis (fever, meningeal signs); granulomas (sarcoidosis, tuberculosis).
   - **Fascicle (midbrain):**
     - **Nothnagel** — with cerebellar ataxia;
     - **Claude** — with contralateral ataxia;
     - **Benedikt** — with contralateral tremor or involuntary movements;
     - **Weber** — with contralateral hemiparesis.
   - **Nucleus:** **bilateral ptosis** (one central nucleus for both levators) and weakness of the **opposite superior rectus**.
3. **Rule to remember (examiner's dictum):** an **isolated medial rectus palsy or an isolated dilated pupil is almost never a partial third nerve palsy**.
4. **Why the pupil is spared or involved:** the pupillary fibres run on the surface of the nerve, fed by pial vessels. Microvascular (vasa nervorum) ischaemia damages the core and **spares the pupil**. Compression from outside (an aneurysm) hits the surface first and **involves the pupil**.
5. **A palsy only one week old may still be evolving**: a pupil-sparing palsy can become pupil-involving if an aneurysm enlarges, so recheck the pupil.

## Viva: questions and expected answers
Q: What type of ptosis is this, and how do you grade ptosis? (2:00:24)
A: Neurogenic ptosis, part of the third nerve palsy. (added) Grade by the amount of droop: mild ≤ 2 mm, moderate 3 mm, severe ≥ 4 mm. This is severe (MRD1 −1 mm, fissure 5 mm), with poor levator function.
Q: Why do you call it isolated, partial and pupil-sparing? (2:02:34)
A: Isolated — no other cranial nerve or neurological sign. Partial — movements limited, not abolished. Pupil-sparing — pupils equal and reacting.
Q: If it were not isolated, which syndromes would you think of? (2:03:21)
A: The midbrain (fascicular) syndromes: Nothnagel, Claude, Benedikt and Weber. Also nuclear palsy (bilateral ptosis, opposite superior rectus weak), cavernous sinus and orbital apex lesions.
Q: How do you localise a third nerve lesion, from the orbit to the nucleus? (2:05:19)
A: Orbit — single-division palsy. Fissure or cavernous sinus — several cranial nerves. Subarachnoid space — aneurysm (painful, pupil involved) or meningitis. Fascicle — midbrain syndromes. Nucleus — bilateral ptosis and opposite superior rectus weakness.
Q: Where do you think the lesion is in your patient? (2:08:42)
A: Not the orbit or fissure (no proptosis, other nerves normal, vision normal), not the nucleus. Most likely microvascular ischaemia of the nerve in its subarachnoid or cavernous course.
Q: Which nerves lie next to the third nerve in the cavernous sinus? (2:10:33)
A: In the lateral wall, from above down: III, IV, V1, V2. The VI nerve lies inside the sinus beside the internal carotid artery.
Q: How do you test the fourth nerve when the third is paralysed? (2:11:05)
A: Ask the patient to look down. If the superior oblique works, the eye **intorts** — watch a conjunctival vessel or iris landmark at the slit lamp. The examiner noted this is hard to judge in a partial palsy.
Q: Why does a microvascular palsy spare the pupil? (2:14:02)
A: Pupillary fibres are superficial and supplied by pial vessels. Ischaemia of the vasa nervorum affects the centre of the nerve. Compression affects the surface first.
Q: What is the pupil like when it is involved? (2:13:37)
A: Fixed and dilated — an efferent defect.
Q: What is the likely cause here? (2:14:43)
A: Microvascular ischaemia — elderly, long-standing uncontrolled diabetes, uncontrolled hypertension, smoker, and a similar recovered palsy of the other eye 5 years ago.
Q: Why does pupil sparing matter for management? (2:15:12)
A: Pupil-sparing — conservative care with the physician controlling diabetes, BP and smoking, and close follow-up of the pupil. Pupil-involving — an aneurysm until proved otherwise: urgent angiography (CT angiography, MR angiography or digital subtraction angiography) and a neurosurgeon for clipping or coiling. It can be life-threatening.
Q: When is a third nerve palsy an emergency? (2:16:00)
A: A "third nerve plus" picture — pupil involvement, sudden severe headache, meningeal signs, other neurological deficits such as hemiparesis. Refer to a neurosurgeon at once.

- Ptosis in a third nerve palsy = **neurogenic**, part of the palsy.
- Localise orbit → fissure/cavernous sinus → subarachnoid → fascicle (Nothnagel, Claude, Benedikt, Weber) → nucleus.
