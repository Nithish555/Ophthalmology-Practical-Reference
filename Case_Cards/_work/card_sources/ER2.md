## At a glance
- **Retinitis pigmentosa (RP)**: an inherited, progressive rod-cone dystrophy (about 1 in 4,000): night blindness, then a shrinking field, with central vision lost late. Triad: **arteriolar attenuation, bone-spicule pigment, waxy disc pallor**.
- **Stargardt disease**: the commonest inherited macular dystrophy (autosomal recessive, ABCA4 gene): central loss in childhood or youth, a beaten-bronze macula, pisciform flecks and a dark choroid.
- Kept because the signs never change, both eyes match, and it tests pedigree drawing, systemic syndromes and electrophysiology.
- **S.No 3 and 27**: "Both eyes typical retinitis pigmentosa (rod-cone dystrophy) with arteriolar attenuation, mid-peripheral bone-spicule pigmentation and waxy disc pallor" — add macular oedema, cataract and the likely inheritance if present.
- **S.No 9 ★ and 37**: RP and Stargardt are separate diseases that rarely co-exist; a fundus showing both is usually **one** disorder, a **cone-rod dystrophy** or **advanced ABCA4 disease** (Stargardt spreading to the periphery). Write "Both eyes inherited retinal dystrophy with Stargardt-like maculopathy and retinitis pigmentosa-like peripheral pigmentation, probably ABCA4-related cone-rod dystrophy", adding "with consecutive optic atrophy" for S.No 37.

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Define RP.
A: A clinically and genetically varied group of inherited disorders in which rods degenerate first and cones and RPE later, causing night blindness, progressive field loss and finally central loss; it is a dystrophy, not an inflammation, despite the name.

Q: What is the triad and which sign comes first?
A: Arteriolar attenuation (most consistent, 90–95%), mid-peripheral bone-spicule pigment, and waxy disc pallor (about half, late). The earliest visible change is fine dust-like pigment and RPE mottling in the mid-periphery.

Q: Give the inheritance patterns and their prognosis.
A: Simplex (no affected relative) 40–50%, mostly recessive; autosomal dominant (AD) 15–25%; autosomal recessive (AR) about 20%; X-linked 5–15%; rarely digenic (PRPH2 with ROM1) or mitochondrial. Prognosis: AD best, then simplex, AR, and X-linked worst.

Q: Name the commonest genes.
A: AD: RHO (rhodopsin). AR: USH2A; also ABCA4, PDE6B and RPE65. X-linked: RPGR, then RP2.

Q: Why does pigment collect around the vessels?
A: As photoreceptors die the inner retina comes to lie on the RPE; contact with retinal vessels makes RPE cells migrate into the retina and cuff the vessels, giving the spicule pattern. It starts in the mid-periphery, where rods are densest. Arterioles narrow because the dying outer retina uses less oxygen, so inner retinal oxygen rises.

Q: What are the atypical forms?
A: Sectoral (usually inferonasal, often AD, slow), pericentral or inverse, RP sine pigmento (an early stage), unilateral (exclude pseudo-RP), retinitis punctata albescens (white dots), and RP with Coats-like exudative vasculopathy.

Q: What causes Stargardt disease?
A: Biallelic mutations in ABCA4 (chromosome 1p), a transporter in rod and cone outer-segment discs that clears used retinal. When it fails, bisretinoids such as A2E build up in the RPE as lipofuscin, the RPE dies and photoreceptors follow. A dominant look-alike is due to ELOVL4.

### Examination and signs
Q: Which syndromes with RP do you look for first?
A: Usher syndrome: RP with sensorineural deafness, the commonest syndromic form, AR; type 1 profound congenital deafness with absent balance, type 2 moderate deafness, type 3 progressive deafness. Bardet–Biedl syndrome: polydactyly, truncal obesity, hypogonadism, kidney disease, learning difficulty, often a bull's-eye macula.

Q: Which forms are metabolic or mitochondrial?
A: Refsum disease (phytanic acid excess: neuropathy, ataxia, deafness, loss of smell, ichthyosis, heart disease); abetalipoproteinaemia or Bassen–Kornzweig syndrome (fat malabsorption, acanthocytes, ataxia, low vitamins A and E); Kearns–Sayre syndrome (mitochondrial deletion, onset before 20: ptosis, external ophthalmoplegia, heart block); neuropathy, ataxia and retinitis pigmentosa (NARP, mitochondrial ATP6 mutation).

Q: Which of these can be treated?
A: Abetalipoproteinaemia: high-dose vitamins A and E with a low-fat diet halts the retinopathy. Refsum disease: a diet free of phytanic acid (no dairy or ruminant fat) and plasma exchange. Kearns–Sayre: an electrocardiogram and a pacemaker for heart block can save life.

Q: Why is central vision lost in RP?
A: CME (reported in 10–70%), epiretinal membrane, macular atrophy, PSC cataract and, late, cone loss.

Q: What other ocular associations occur?
A: PSC cataract (about 40–50%), myopia, keratoconus, open-angle glaucoma (about 3%), disc drusen, vitreous pigment cells, intermediate uveitis, and a golden tapetal reflex in female carriers of X-linked RP.

Q: Why is the disc waxy pale in RP?
A: Consecutive optic atrophy: ganglion cells and axons are lost after the outer retina degenerates, and a glial membrane grows over the disc; with narrowed vessels this gives a yellow waxy look. RAPD is usually absent because both eyes are alike.

Q: What is the differential diagnosis of pseudo-RP?
A: Congenital rubella and syphilis (salt-and-pepper), thioridazine and chloroquine toxicity, a settled exudative detachment, trauma, old panretinal laser, chronic uveitis, cancer-associated retinopathy, choroideremia, and gyrate atrophy (raised ornithine).

Q: Describe the fundus in Stargardt disease.
A: At first normal or a dull foveal reflex while vision is already poor (often mislabelled as malingering); then an oval beaten-bronze atrophic macula, later bull's-eye or geographic atrophy; yellow-white pisciform (fish-tail) flecks at RPE level around the macula and out to the mid-periphery, sparing the peripapillary retina; temporal disc pallor late.

Q: How does fundus flavimaculatus differ?
A: It is the same ABCA4 disease: flecks spread widely with the macula relatively spared, later onset and better vision. Stargardt disease is the macular-atrophy end of the spectrum.

Q: Give the differential diagnosis of a bull's-eye macula.
A: Stargardt disease, cone and cone-rod dystrophy, chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine toxicity, Bardet–Biedl syndrome, neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis, benign concentric annular dystrophy and central areolar choroidal dystrophy.

### Investigations
Q: What does the ERG show in RP?
A: The full-field ERG falls before the fundus changes: rod (scotopic) b-wave first, then 30 Hz cone flicker with a delayed implicit time; advanced disease is extinguished (below 10 µV). It also detects carriers. Other causes of an extinguished ERG: Leber congenital amaurosis, total detachment, ophthalmic artery occlusion, siderosis.

Q: What does the EOG show?
A: It measures the standing potential of the RPE; the light-peak to dark-trough (Arden) ratio is normally about 2 and abnormal below 1.7. It is subnormal in RP but adds little to the ERG; a very low EOG with a normal ERG means Best disease.

Q: Describe the fields in RP.
A: On Goldmann kinetic perimetry, scattered scotomas at 20–25° join into a ring; its outer edge spreads to the periphery and its inner edge closes on fixation, leaving tunnel vision. A field under 10° from fixation counts as blindness even with 6/6 acuity.

Q: What do OCT and FAF show in RP?
A: OCT: loss of the ellipsoid zone and external limiting membrane outside a central island whose width follows the field; cystoid spaces and epiretinal membrane. FAF: a bright ring around the fovea marks the edge of working photoreceptors and shrinks with time; patchy dark areas in the periphery.

Q: Which systemic tests would you order?
A: Guided by the history: audiometry, serum phytanic acid, lipid profile and blood film for acanthocytes, vitamins A and E, electrocardiogram, ornithine, syphilis serology, renal function, and a genetic panel.

Q: What does FFA show in Stargardt disease?
A: A dark (silent) choroid in 60–80%: lipofuscin in the RPE masks choroidal fluorescence, so retinal vessels stand out on a black background. Fresh flecks block; old flecks and the macular atrophy give window defects.

Q: What do FAF, OCT and ERG show in Stargardt disease?
A: FAF, the best test: bright flecks, dark atrophy, high background signal and peripapillary sparing. OCT: foveal outer retinal and ellipsoid loss, flecks as deposits at the RPE. ERG groups: 1, normal full-field with abnormal pattern ERG; 2, added cone loss; 3, added rod loss, with the worst prognosis.

Q: Give the Fishman stages.
A: Stage 1: macular pigment change and flecks within 1 disc diameter of the fovea, ERG normal. Stage 2: flecks beyond the arcades, cone or rod responses may be low. Stage 3: flecks resorbed with macular choriocapillaris atrophy, ERG reduced. Stage 4: extensive RPE atrophy throughout the fundus, rod and cone ERG reduced, field restricted.

### Management
Q: How do you manage RP?
A: Explain the course; genetic counselling and examination of relatives; ultraviolet-blocking amber filters; low-vision aids (magnifiers, prisms or field expanders, bright torches) and mobility training; treat CME, cataract and glaucoma; review every 6–12 months with fields and OCT; advise on driving and work; disability certification.

Q: What is the role of vitamin A?
A: Vitamin A palmitate 15,000 international units (IU) daily slightly slowed ERG decline in adults; avoid it in pregnancy, children, liver disease and ABCA4 disease, and check liver function and vitamin A levels yearly. High-dose vitamin E is harmful; docosahexaenoic acid and lutein may help a little.

Q: How do you treat CME in RP?
A: Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors first: oral acetazolamide 250–500 mg daily or topical dorzolamide 2% three times daily; then intravitreal triamcinolone or dexamethasone implant. The cysts often leak little on FFA, and anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) drugs help less.

Q: What precautions do you take in cataract surgery?
A: Operate for PSC, which usually helps; expect weak zonules (capsular tension ring), anterior capsule shrinkage (large capsulorhexis) and postoperative CME (topical non-steroidal drops); reduce microscope light exposure; warn of a guarded result.

Q: How do you manage Stargardt disease?
A: No cure: avoid vitamin A supplements (including the high-dose vitamin formulas sold for macular degeneration), stop smoking, wear ultraviolet-blocking sunglasses and a hat, provide low-vision aids, screen siblings and counsel; treat rare choroidal neovascularisation with anti-VEGF.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: What is the prognosis in RP?
A: Slowly progressive; most become legally blind by field in middle age, while central acuity may last into the sixth decade in AD and simplex cases. X-linked males are worst, often blind by the third or fourth decade.

Q: What is the prognosis in Stargardt disease?
A: Once acuity falls below 6/12 it drops quickly to about 6/60 and then stabilises; the peripheral field is kept, so total blindness does not occur. Early onset and rod involvement on ERG predict a worse course.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: What is voretigene neparvovec?
A: The first approved retinal gene therapy (United States Food and Drug Administration, 2017): an adeno-associated virus 2 vector carrying normal RPE65, injected under the retina after vitrectomy (1.5 × 10¹¹ vector genomes in 0.3 mL per eye, eyes treated separately). It is for biallelic RPE65 dystrophy (Leber congenital amaurosis or RP) with enough surviving cells, and improves navigation in dim light.

Q: What other new treatments are there?
A: Retinal prostheses (Argus II epiretinal implant, now discontinued), optogenetics, stem-cell and RPE transplants, antisense oligonucleotides, gene therapy trials for X-linked RPGR disease, and deuterated vitamin A for Stargardt disease.

Q: The fundus shows both RP and Stargardt features. How do you present it?
A: Describe, do not label: macula (atrophy, flecks, beaten-bronze sheen), periphery (spicules), vessels, disc. Then offer one unifying diagnosis: cone-rod dystrophy or advanced ABCA4 disease (Fishman stage 4) rather than two diseases. If the department calls it "RP with Stargardt", accept the description, note the unusual pairing, and say ERG, FAF and genetic testing will settle it.

Q: How does cone-rod dystrophy differ from RP?
A: Cones fail first: early central loss, photophobia, colour defect and day blindness, a bull's-eye macula and temporal disc pallor; night blindness and peripheral pigment come later. The ERG loses photopic responses more than scotopic, the reverse of RP. ABCA4 is the commonest gene for recessive cone-rod dystrophy.

Q: Can ABCA4 cause an RP picture?
A: Yes. Severe ABCA4 genotypes give a pan-retinal rod-cone degeneration (recessive RP) with macular atrophy, so one gene explains "RP with Stargardt"; vitamin A is harmful in these patients.

Q: What does optic atrophy add in S.No 37?
A: In RP it is waxy consecutive pallor from ganglion cell loss; in cone-rod dystrophy and late Stargardt it is temporal pallor from loss of the papillomacular bundle. Chalky pallor out of proportion to the retina suggests a separate optic neuropathy: check RAPD, colour vision and the visual evoked potential.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Calling every pigmented fundus RP: drugs, rubella, syphilis, old detachment and laser scars are mimics.
- Presenting "RP plus Stargardt" as two diseases without suggesting a unifying cone-rod or ABCA4 diagnosis.
- Not drawing a pedigree or not asking about hearing, balance, extra digits and heart block.
- Prescribing vitamin A to a Stargardt or ABCA4 patient.
- Missing CME or cataract as treatable causes of central loss.
- Saying RAPD is expected in RP, or calling waxy pallor "glaucomatous".
- Forgetting that a field under 10° is legal blindness even with 6/6 acuity.
:::
