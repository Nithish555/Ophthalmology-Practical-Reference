## At a glance
- **What it is:** an inherited, bilateral, symmetrical, non-inflammatory build-up of abnormal protein in the corneal stroma. Granular dystrophy is **autosomal dominant** and caused by mutations in the **transforming growth factor beta-induced (TGFBI) gene** on chromosome **5q31**.
- **Why examiners keep it:** a stable, bilateral, easy-to-see case that opens the whole dystrophy viva: the International Committee for Classification of Corneal Dystrophies (IC3D) system, the granular–lattice–macular table, stains, recurrence in grafts and the risk of refractive surgery.
- **Pattern to recognise:** discrete white **breadcrumb or snowflake opacities** in the central anterior stroma, **clear stroma between them and a clear periphery**, normal thickness, and vision better than the cornea looks.
- **Two types:** granular type 1 (classic; Arg555Trp, arginine replaced by tryptophan at codon 555) and granular type 2 (**Avellino**, combined granular–lattice; Arg124His, arginine to histidine at codon 124).
- **Diagnosis line for S.No 28:** "Both eyes (BE) granular corneal dystrophy, probably type 1, with discrete anterior stromal breadcrumb opacities, clear intervening and peripheral stroma and no recurrent erosions; best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) right eye (RE) 6/… and left eye (LE) 6/…".
- If mid-stromal lattice lines are also present: "BE granular corneal dystrophy type 2 (Avellino)".

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
Mr X, a 38-year-old clerk, has mild glare and slowly increasing blur in both eyes for five years; his father and one sister have "white spots" on the cornea. He has no pain or watering on waking, no refractive surgery and no systemic illness. Best-corrected vision is 6/9 in each eye and intraocular pressure is 14 and 15 mm Hg. Both corneas show many discrete, sharply outlined, white crumb-like opacities in the central anterior stroma, opaque on direct illumination and translucent on retroillumination, with clear stroma between them and a clear peripheral zone of about 2–3 mm. There are no lattice lines; the epithelium is intact and corneal sensation, thickness and endothelium are normal. Anterior chamber, iris, lens and fundus are normal.

**Diagnosis: Both eyes granular corneal dystrophy type 1, with discrete anterior stromal opacities and clear intervening and peripheral stroma, not yet affecting vision significantly.**

If lattice lines lie in the mid-stroma with anterior haze: **Both eyes granular corneal dystrophy type 2 (Avellino, combined granular–lattice).**
:::

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Define a corneal dystrophy.
A: An inherited, usually bilateral and symmetrical, slowly progressive, non-inflammatory loss of corneal clarity; typically central and avascular, not caused by environmental or systemic disease; it starts in one layer with a characteristic deposit or pattern.

Q: How does a dystrophy differ from a degeneration?
A: Dystrophy: inherited, early onset, bilateral and symmetrical, central, avascular, primary. Degeneration: acquired, age-related or secondary to disease or environment, often unilateral or asymmetric, peripheral, may be vascularised.

Q: What is the IC3D classification?
A: A system (2008; second edition 2015; third edition 2024) naming each dystrophy by anatomy and genetics. The 2015 groups: epithelial and subepithelial; epithelial–stromal TGFBI; stromal; endothelial. Evidence categories: C1 gene and mutation known; C2 locus mapped, gene unknown; C3 clinical entity without a locus; C4 suspected new dystrophy.

Q: What does the TGFBI gene make, and which dystrophies does it cause?
A: Keratoepithelin, an extracellular adhesion protein made by epithelium and keratocytes (old gene name BIGH3); the mutant protein aggregates. Hot spots at codons 124 and 555: Reis–Bücklers (Arg124Leu), Thiel–Behnke (Arg555Gln), lattice type 1 (Arg124Cys), granular type 1 (Arg555Trp), granular type 2 (Arg124His).

Q: Which of these dystrophies are not autosomal dominant?
A: Macular dystrophy (autosomal recessive, CHST6 on 16q22); also recessive are gelatinous drop-like dystrophy, lattice type 3 and congenital hereditary endothelial dystrophy. Granular, lattice type 1, Avellino, Reis–Bücklers and Thiel–Behnke are dominant.

Q: Why is type 2 called Avellino dystrophy?
A: It was first described in families tracing their roots to Avellino in Italy. It combines granular deposits and lattice lines in one cornea; commonest in Korea and Japan; homozygotes have severe early disease.

Q: What is the deposit in granular dystrophy?
A: Non-collagenous hyaline protein (mutant keratoepithelin) forming sharply outlined extracellular aggregates, mainly in the anterior stroma; electron microscopy shows rod-shaped bodies. Type 2 also contains amyloid.

### Examination and signs
Q: Describe the slit-lamp picture of granular type 1.
A: Bilateral discrete grey-white crumb-, snowflake-, ring- or popcorn-shaped opacities in the central anterior stroma; white on direct illumination, translucent on retroillumination; clear stroma between them and a clear 2–3 mm periphery. Later they enlarge, coalesce and go deeper, and the stroma between turns ground-glass.

Q: Why does vision stay good for so long?
A: The deposits are discrete, the stroma between them is clear and the surface stays regular; vision falls only with confluence, superficial spread or haze, usually after 40–50 years.

Q: How does granular type 2 differ from type 1?
A: Fewer, larger star- or ring-shaped anterior stromal deposits come first; lattice lines follow in the mid and deep stroma; anterior haze develops with age; onset is earlier, with more erosions and glare; it worsens dramatically after LASIK.

Q: How do you tell lattice lines from granular deposits?
A: Lattice lines are fine, branching, refractile filaments with a clear core and double contour on retroillumination, with dots on a hazy background; granular deposits are opaque discrete crumbs with clear stroma between them.

Q: Which stromal dystrophy thins the cornea and reaches the limbus?
A: Macular dystrophy: diffuse haze, ill-defined grey-white spots out to the limbus, reduced thickness and Descemet membrane or endothelial involvement; it needs keratoplasty earliest.

Q: What is lattice type 2?
A: Meretoja syndrome (familial amyloidosis, Finnish type): gelsolin gene on chromosome 9, autosomal dominant; a few thick lattice lines running in from the periphery; facial palsy, lax facial skin, dermatochalasis, cranial and peripheral neuropathy. IC3D says it is not a true corneal dystrophy.

Q: What is recurrent corneal erosion, and in which dystrophies?
A: Sudden pain, watering and photophobia on opening the eyes in the morning, as the lid tears off loosely attached epithelium. Commonest with epithelial basement membrane dystrophy, Reis–Bücklers and lattice; less with granular type 2; rare and mild in granular type 1.

Q: What is the differential diagnosis of granular-looking stromal opacities?
A: Other TGFBI dystrophies; macular, Schnyder and fleck dystrophies; paraproteinaemic keratopathy (monoclonal gammopathy or myeloma: check serum protein electrophoresis); healed nummular keratitis; drug or lipid deposits.

### Investigations
Q: Name the histology stains for the three stromal dystrophies.
A: Granular: hyaline, bright red with Masson trichrome. Lattice: amyloid, orange-pink with Congo red, apple-green birefringence and dichroism under polarised light, fluorescent with thioflavin T, metachromatic with crystal violet. Macular: glycosaminoglycan (keratan sulphate), blue with Alcian blue and colloidal iron, periodic acid–Schiff positive.

Q: How is macular dystrophy subtyped?
A: By antigenic keratan sulphate: type I absent from cornea and serum (commonest); type IA present only in keratocytes; type II present in cornea and serum. They look identical clinically.

Q: What does AS-OCT add?
A: Deposit depth and the clear stroma beneath; this decides PTK (disease in the anterior 100–150 µm) or DALK or PK. It also measures thickness and the depth of recurrence in a graft.

Q: When is genetic testing useful?
A: Not routinely. It confirms the TGFBI mutation in atypical cases, screens relatives, detects homozygotes (worse prognosis) and screens refractive-surgery candidates for Avellino in high-prevalence populations.

Q: What does confocal microscopy show?
A: Granular type 1: hyper-reflective, sharply bordered anterior stromal deposits; type 2: these plus linear branching lattice deposits; lattice: branching filaments with changing reflectivity; macular: diffuse, ill-defined reflective material.

Q: Why do specular microscopy in a stromal dystrophy?
A: To judge the endothelium before choosing DALK or PK; essential in macular dystrophy, where subclinical endothelial disease favours PK.

### Management
Q: How do you manage S.No 28 if vision is good?
A: Explain the disease and its inheritance; observe; spectacles; tinted lenses for glare; lubricants; advise against LASIK; yearly review with BCVA and slit-lamp photographs; examine relatives.

Q: How do you treat recurrent erosions?
A: Acute: lubricants, cycloplegic, bandage contact lens and antibiotic cover. Prevention: 5% sodium chloride ointment at night for months. Persistent cases: epithelial debridement, anterior stromal puncture outside the visual axis, or PTK.

Q: What is PTK and what are its limits?
A: Excimer laser (argon fluoride, 193 nm) ablation of the anterior stroma to remove superficial opacities and smooth the surface; suits deposits in the anterior 100–150 µm while leaving at least 250 µm of stroma. Problems: hyperopic shift, haze, delayed healing, infection and recurrence; it can be repeated.

Q: When DALK and when PK?
A: DALK when opacities are deep but the endothelium is healthy (granular, lattice): no endothelial rejection, early steroid taper, stronger wound. PK when Descemet membrane or endothelium is involved (often macular) or when DALK perforates.

Q: Do dystrophies recur in a graft? Why, where and how treated?
A: Yes: host epithelium and invading host keratocytes lay down fresh protein in the donor. Recurrence is subepithelial or at the interface, first at the graft–host junction, after about 1–19 years; commonest with Reis–Bücklers and lattice, frequent in granular (earlier in type 2), least in macular. Superficial recurrence is treated with PTK.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: Why can LASIK worsen Avellino dystrophy?
A: The flap cut and stromal wound healing make epithelium and keratocytes produce more mutant TGFBI protein, which deposits at the flap interface; dense, progressive opacities appear months to years later and may need flap lift with debridement, PTK or keratoplasty. PRK can also provoke deposits, so refractive surgery is contraindicated.

Q: What is the prognosis of granular type 1?
A: Good: most keep useful vision into middle age; keratoplasty is needed late, if at all; recurrences after surgery are usually superficial and treatable by PTK. Macular dystrophy has the worst visual prognosis but the fewest recurrences.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: Contrast Reis–Bücklers and Thiel–Behnke dystrophies.
A: Both are autosomal dominant Bowman layer TGFBI dystrophies with painful erosions from childhood. Reis–Bücklers (granular type 3, Arg124Leu): geographic map-like opacities, worse vision, rod-shaped bodies staining red with Masson. Thiel–Behnke (Arg555Gln): honeycomb pattern, milder, curly fibres on electron microscopy. Both recur early after keratoplasty; PTK is preferred.

Q: Contrast Fuchs endothelial dystrophy with granular dystrophy.
A: Fuchs is posterior: endothelial cell loss with central guttae (beaten-metal look), Descemet thickening, then stromal and epithelial oedema with bullae; women 4:1, after 50; blur worst on waking; thick cornea. Granular is anterior stromal deposits with normal thickness and endothelium.

Q: What is the genetics of Fuchs dystrophy?
A: Late-onset: mostly a cytosine–thymine–guanine (CTG) trinucleotide repeat expansion (CTG18.1) in the transcription factor 4 (TCF4) gene on chromosome 18; early-onset: mutations in collagen VIII alpha-2 (COL8A2). Autosomal dominant with variable penetrance; many cases seem sporadic.

Q: How is Fuchs dystrophy managed?
A: Early: 5% sodium chloride drops 4–8 times a day and ointment at night; a hairdryer at arm's length in the morning; bandage contact lens for ruptured bullae. Surgery: Descemet membrane endothelial keratoplasty (DMEK) or Descemet stripping automated endothelial keratoplasty (DSAEK); PK if the stroma is scarred; combine with cataract surgery when needed.

Q: What newer treatments exist for Fuchs dystrophy?
A: Descemet stripping only (remove the central guttae and let peripheral cells migrate), helped by the Rho-kinase inhibitor ripasudil; injection of cultured endothelial cells with a Rho-kinase inhibitor.

Q: What is Schnyder corneal dystrophy?
A: An autosomal dominant disorder of corneal lipid metabolism (UbiA prenyltransferase domain-containing 1 gene): central subepithelial cholesterol crystals in a ring or disc, stromal haze and early arcus; about half have raised serum cholesterol, so check the lipid profile. Some have no crystals.

Q: What treatments for TGFBI dystrophies are under research?
A: Allele-specific gene editing and small interfering ribonucleic acid, both silencing the mutant TGFBI allele, are experimental; femtosecond-assisted DALK and repeat PTK with mitomycin C are current refinements.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Calling it "macular dystrophy" because it is central: macular dystrophy is autosomal recessive, hazy between spots, reaches the limbus and thins the cornea.
- Describing only "white spots": examiners want **clear stroma between the deposits and a clear periphery**, seen with retroillumination and an optical section.
- Forgetting to ask about LASIK or PRK, and to warn affected relatives against refractive surgery without screening for Avellino.
- Saying keratoplasty cures it: TGFBI dystrophies recur in grafts; PTK for superficial disease, DALK when the endothelium is healthy.
- Mixing up the stains: Masson trichrome red for granular, Congo red for lattice, Alcian blue for macular.
- Calling Reis–Bücklers "honeycomb": in IC3D the honeycomb pattern belongs to Thiel–Behnke.
- Saying macular dystrophy recurs most in grafts: it recurs least, although it needs keratoplasty earliest.
:::

:::recall
- Granular: TGFBI on 5q31, autosomal dominant, hyaline, Masson red, clear between and around the crumbs.
- Avellino (granular type 2): Arg124His, granular crumbs plus lattice lines; LASIK makes it worse.
- Lattice: amyloid, Congo red with apple-green birefringence, early erosions; type 2 is gelsolin systemic amyloidosis.
- Macular: autosomal recessive, CHST6, keratan sulphate, Alcian blue, hazy to the limbus, thin; needs keratoplasty earliest, recurs least.
- Superficial disease and graft recurrence: PTK; deep disease with healthy endothelium: DALK.
- Fuchs is the endothelial contrast: guttae, oedema, TCF4; treated with endothelial keratoplasty.
- Mnemonic for stains: "Marilyn Monroe Always Gets Her Man in LA City" (Macular, Mucopolysaccharide, Alcian blue; Granular, Hyaline, Masson; Lattice, Amyloid, Congo red).
:::
