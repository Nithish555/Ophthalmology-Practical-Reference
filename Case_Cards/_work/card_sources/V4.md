## Case as presented
**Mr X, 34.** Before the presentation the examiner asked for a **brief history**: "say 'nothing contributory on systemic examination' rather than listing every normal".
- **Complaint:** prominence of the right eye for 1 year, slowly increasing; mild aching pain behind the eye for 3 months.
- **Negative history:** no blurring, double vision, postural or diurnal variation, redness, discharge, difficulty moving the eye, weight loss, night sweats, heat or cold intolerance, tremor, or lumps elsewhere.

**Diagnosis given:** right axial proptosis with compressive optic neuropathy. Differentials: thyroid eye disease (TED) first; then intraconal masses — cavernous haemangioma, optic nerve sheath meningioma, optic nerve glioma, circumscribed lymphangioma, orbital lymphoma. Plan: investigate.

## Think like this
1. **The commonest cause of proptosis in an adult** — unilateral or bilateral, man or woman, 30 or 50 — is TED. So it is the right *first thought*.
2. **Then test it against the case:**
   - Signs of inflammation? None.
   - Lid retraction, lid lag? None.
   - Restricted movements? None.
   - Thyroid profile? Normal.
   - Axial proptosis with resistance on retropulsion? Yes.

   So drop TED and say "intraconal mass".
3. **What else can push the eye forward?**
   - Vascular: a carotid–cavernous fistula gives pulsation and congestion; orbital varix gives postural variation.
   - Inflammatory: myositis, idiopathic orbital inflammation.
   - Parasitic.
   - Trauma.

   Ask and examine for each.
4. **Optic nerve function in every proptosis:** vision, RAPD, colour (red desaturation), fields. Disc oedema with an RAPD and normal acuity = early compressive optic neuropathy.
5. **The examiner's summary of this case:** a young man, symptom-free, vision normal, cosmetic proptosis growing slowly over a year, axial, no postural change, resistance on retropulsion → **intraconal mass**. The CT showed a well-defined, homogeneous globular intraconal mass with normal muscles.

## Viva: questions and expected answers
Q: How do you examine for proptosis? (1:23:21)
A: **Naffziger method:** stand behind the seated patient, tilt the head back and look along the brow — the affected globe shows beyond the orbital margin. **Worm's-eye view:** look up from below with the head tilted back. **Ruler test** for minimal proptosis. Measure with an **exophthalmometer**.
Q: Name the signs of thyroid eye disease. (1:24:10)
A:
- Dalrymple — lid retraction.
- von Graefe — lid lag on downgaze.
- Stellwag — infrequent blinking.
- Kocher — staring look.
- Ballet — restricted muscle movement.
- Braley — IOP rises on upgaze.
- Möbius — **weak convergence, one of the earliest signs**.

The examiner keeps a list of about 28 signs.
Q: What is the commonest cause of unilateral and of bilateral proptosis in adults? (1:26:55)
A: Thyroid eye disease, for both.
Q: Which features of your case are against TED? (1:25:36)
A: No inflammation (no redness, chemosis or lid oedema), no lid retraction or lid lag, full movements, axial proptosis, normal thyroid tests.
Q: How do you make a definitive diagnosis in proptosis? (1:28:28)
A: Blood tests and thyroid profile with antibodies, then imaging — a **CT** is enough here. An **orbital B-scan** shows the mass's reflectivity, how well defined it is, and its relation to the optic nerve and muscles.
Q: Which vascular causes did you exclude, and how? (1:30:15)
A: Carotid–cavernous fistula — no pulsation, bruit or congestion. Orbital varix — no change in proptosis with posture or Valsalva.
Q: What is absolute and relative exophthalmometry? (1:31:49)
A: Absolute — compared with the normal for age and race. Relative — the difference between the two eyes. (added) A difference of more than 2 mm is significant; here it is 4 mm.
Q: Which exophthalmometer can you use after an orbitotomy? (1:32:47)
A: One that rests on the superior and inferior orbital rims instead of the lateral rim — (added) the Naugle exophthalmometer — because the lateral rim may have been removed.
Q: What CT changes would TED show? (1:35:18)
A: **Spindle-shaped (fusiform) enlargement of the muscle bellies with sparing of the tendons**, more orbital fat, crowding at the apex.
Q: If the tendons were also thickened? (1:36:51)
A: Think of **orbital myositis**, an inflammatory condition.
Q: How do you assess the optic nerve in this patient? (1:39:01)
A: Vision, pupils (RAPD), colour vision (Ishihara plates **and red desaturation** compared between the eyes), visual fields.
Q: What is pseudoproptosis? (1:40:27)
A: The eye looks prominent without true forward displacement. Causes: **axial myopia (most common)**, lid retraction, enophthalmos of the other eye.
Q: What is the pseudo-Graefe sign? (1:41:06)
A: Lid elevation on attempted downgaze or adduction from **aberrant regeneration of the third nerve**.

:::say
Mr X, a 34-year-old man, has noticed slowly increasing prominence of his right eye for one year, with an ache behind the eye for three months and no double vision, redness or postural change. He has right axial proptosis, 23 mm against 19 mm on Hertel exophthalmometry at a base of 105, with resistance on retropulsion and full ocular movements. There are no signs of thyroid eye disease. Vision is 6/6 in each eye, but the right eye has a grade 1 relative afferent pupillary defect and disc oedema. My diagnosis is right axial proptosis due to an intraconal mass lesion, most likely a cavernous haemangioma, with early compressive optic neuropathy. Optic nerve sheath meningioma is the main differential. I would confirm with a CT scan of the orbits.
:::

## Images and their points

:::recall
- TED = commonest cause; **then test it** against the signs and drop it if they are absent.
- Describe retropulsion ("resistance felt"), do not just say "positive".
- CT is enough. TED on CT: fusiform muscle bellies, tendons spared. Tendon involved → myositis.
- Optic nerve: VA, RAPD, **red desaturation**, fields.
- Pseudoproptosis: axial myopia. Pseudo-Graefe: aberrant third nerve regeneration.
:::
