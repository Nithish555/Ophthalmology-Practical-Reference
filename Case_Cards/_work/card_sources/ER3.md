## At a glance
- **Branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO)**: thrombosis of a branch vein at an arteriovenous (AV) crossing, where a thickened artery compresses the vein inside their shared sheath; about three times commoner than **central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO)**. Hypertension is the main association.
- Kept because old occlusions are stable and full of signs (sectoral haemorrhage, collaterals, a **sclerosed white venule**, laser scars) and they test the Branch Vein Occlusion Study (BVOS), anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) treatment and the link with glaucoma.
- **S.No 7**: "Right eye inferotemporal branch retinal vein occlusion, non-ischaemic (or ischaemic), with (or without) macular oedema, no neovascularisation."
- **S.No 43 and 46 ★**: "Right eye (S.No 43) or left eye (S.No 46) old superotemporal branch retinal vein occlusion with a sclerosed venule beyond the AV crossing and collaterals", adding macular oedema, new vessels or laser scars as found.
- **S.No 34**: "Left eye old branch retinal vein occlusion with both eyes proliferative diabetic retinopathy and glaucoma" — name the glaucoma (open-angle or neovascular) from gonioscopy; see also card ER4.
- **S.No 52 ★**: "Left eye old central retinal vein occlusion with optociliary shunt vessels, sclerosed veins and macular pigmentary change; right eye primary open-angle glaucoma with a vertical cup–disc ratio of 0.9."

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
Mrs X, a 64-year-old woman with hypertension, noticed sudden painless blurring of the lower half of her vision in the left eye 2 years ago and received three injections elsewhere; she now has mild distortion. Best-corrected visual acuity is 6/6 in the right eye and 6/18 in the left; the pupils react normally with no RAPD, the IOP is 16 mmHg in each eye, and undilated gonioscopy shows no new vessels. In the left eye the superotemporal vein is white and sheathed beyond an AV crossing one disc diameter from the disc, with collaterals crossing the horizontal raphe, dot haemorrhages, microaneurysms and hard exudates in that sector, and retinal thickening at the fovea; there are no new vessels or laser marks. The right fundus shows arteriolar narrowing and AV nicking. My diagnosis is left eye old superotemporal branch retinal vein occlusion with a sclerosed venule and collaterals, with macular oedema, and both eyes hypertensive retinopathy. I would do OCT and FFA for oedema and non-perfusion, treat centre-involving oedema with anti-VEGF injections, watch for new vessels, and control her blood pressure with her physician.

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Define BRVO and give its types.
A: Occlusion of a branch of the central retinal vein, almost always at an AV crossing. Types: major (a first-order branch draining a quadrant), macular (a small macular branch) and peripheral; a hemiretinal occlusion at the disc drains half the retina and behaves like CRVO. By perfusion: non-ischaemic or ischaemic.

Q: Describe the pathogenesis.
A: At most crossings the artery lies in front of the vein in a common adventitial sheath; a thick arteriosclerotic artery narrows the vein, causing turbulence, endothelial damage and thrombosis (Virchow's triad). The raised venous pressure causes haemorrhage, oedema and ischaemia, and ischaemic retina releases VEGF.

Q: Why is the superotemporal quadrant affected most?
A: It has the most AV crossings, and temporal occlusions drain the macula and so cause symptoms; nasal occlusions are usually silent and missed.

Q: What are the risk factors?
A: Age over 60, hypertension (strongest), diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, smoking, obesity, glaucoma and a short axial length; in the young, thrombophilia, hyperhomocysteinaemia, antiphospholipid syndrome, oral contraceptives, vasculitis (Behçet disease, sarcoidosis, tuberculosis) and dehydration.

Q: How do you define ischaemic BRVO and CRVO?
A: On FFA, BRVO is ischaemic with more than 5 disc diameters of capillary non-perfusion (BVOS), and CRVO with more than 10 disc areas (Central Vein Occlusion Study, CVOS). Ischaemic CRVO also has acuity below 6/60, a dense RAPD, many cotton-wool spots and an electroretinogram (ERG) b:a ratio below 1.

### Examination and signs
Q: What are the signs of an old BRVO?
A: Haemorrhages fade over 6–12 months, leaving a sclerosed or sheathed venule, collaterals, microaneurysms, telangiectasia, hard exudate rings, macular pigment change or epiretinal membrane and a narrowed sheathed arteriole; ischaemic eyes may show NVE, vitreous haemorrhage or tractional detachment.

Q: What is a sclerosed venule and what does it mean?
A: The occluded vein beyond the crossing becomes a white thread, or a vessel with white lines along both walls, because its wall thickens and its lumen narrows or closes after thrombosis. It marks an old occlusion (months to years), usually with a poorly perfused sector; FFA shows delayed or absent filling and late staining of the vein wall.

Q: How do you tell it from vasculitis?
A: In BRVO the sheathing starts at an AV crossing and stays in one sector with collaterals and old haemorrhage. Periphlebitis (Eales disease, sarcoidosis, tuberculosis) affects younger patients with patchy sheathing in several quadrants, often peripheral, with vitreous cells and no relation to crossings.

Q: How do you tell collaterals from NVE?
A: Collaterals are existing capillaries that enlarge to bypass the block: they lie within the retina, are smooth and of larger calibre, often cross the horizontal raphe, carry no fibrous tissue and do not leak on FFA, so they must never be lasered. NVE are fine lacy fronds on the retinal surface at the edge of non-perfused retina, may carry fibrous tissue and leak heavily.

Q: Why does BRVO cause retinal new vessels and CRVO iris new vessels?
A: In BRVO a moderately ischaemic sector next to healthy retina grows NVE (about 40% of ischaemic eyes). In ischaemic CRVO the whole retina is too damaged to grow vessels, but VEGF reaches the front of the eye, so NVI and NVA dominate.

Q: What are the signs of an old CRVO?
A: Optociliary shunt vessels on the disc (the footprint of a past CRVO), sclerosed sheathed veins in all quadrants, narrowed arterioles, a few residual haemorrhages, macular pigment mottling, chronic cystoid oedema or epiretinal membrane, disc pallor after ischaemia, and possibly NVD, NVE, NVI or laser scars.

Q: Give the differential diagnosis of optociliary shunts.
A: Old CRVO, optic nerve sheath meningioma (with visual loss and optic atrophy, the classic triad), chronic papilloedema, glaucoma, optic nerve glioma, and congenital shunts.

Q: Give the differential diagnosis of an acute BRVO.
A: Diabetic and hypertensive retinopathy, ocular ischaemic syndrome, radiation retinopathy, retinal arterial macroaneurysm and vasculitis; a sectoral wedge with its apex at a crossing decides.

### Investigations
Q: How do you work up an elderly patient?
A: Blood pressure, fasting and postprandial blood sugar, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), lipid profile, complete blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, renal function and electrocardiogram; IOP and gonioscopy in both eyes.

Q: What extra tests for a young or bilateral case?
A: Thrombophilia screen (prothrombin and partial thromboplastin times, protein C, protein S, antithrombin III, factor V Leiden), homocysteine, antiphospholipid antibodies, antinuclear antibody, serum angiotensin-converting enzyme, chest X-ray, Mantoux test, syphilis and human immunodeficiency virus serology, and serum protein electrophoresis.

Q: When and why do you do FFA?
A: After the haemorrhages clear (about 3 months): delayed venous filling, blocked fluorescence, the area of non-perfusion (ischaemic or not), leaking macula versus ischaemic macula (enlarged foveal avascular zone), collaterals (no leak) versus NVE (leak), and late vein-wall staining. Wide-field FFA maps peripheral non-perfusion.

Q: What does OCT show?
A: Acute: cystoid spaces, diffuse thickening, subretinal fluid and shadowing from haemorrhage. Old: epiretinal membrane, foveal thinning and inner retinal thinning in the ischaemic sector. It is the main tool for following oedema; OCTA shows capillary dropout without dye.

### Management
Q: What did the BVOS show?
A: Grid argon laser for perfused macular oedema of at least 3 months with acuity 6/12 or worse: about two-thirds gained 2 lines versus about one-third of controls at 3 years. Sector scatter laser once new vessels appeared roughly halved vitreous haemorrhage (about 60% to 30%), but laser before new vessels appear is not advised.

Q: Why not give prophylactic sector laser?
A: Only about 40% of ischaemic BRVOs ever grow new vessels, and treating them when they appear works as well, so prophylaxis over-treats the majority. Exception: when follow-up cannot be assured.

Q: Describe grid laser for BRVO oedema.
A: After 3 months and an FFA showing leakage with a perfused macula: 532 nm green, 50–100 µm spots, 0.05–0.1 s, light-grey burns one burn-width apart over the leaking area of the affected sector, not inside the edge of the foveal avascular zone, not beyond the arcade, and not on haemorrhage or collaterals.

Q: Describe sector laser for new vessels.
A: Scatter burns over the whole non-perfused sector seen on FFA: 500 µm, 0.1 s, moderate grey-white, one burn-width apart, from 2 disc diameters outside the fovea to the periphery, sparing collaterals and the macula.

Q: What is the first-line treatment of macular oedema now?
A: Intravitreal anti-VEGF for centre-involving oedema with acuity 6/12 or worse: ranibizumab 0.5 mg, aflibercept 2 mg or bevacizumab 1.25 mg, monthly until stable (usually 3–6 doses), then as needed or treat-and-extend. Second line: dexamethasone implant 0.7 mg, or grid laser for persistent oedema.

Q: Give the results of BRAVO, CRUISE and SCORE.
A: Monthly ranibizumab for 6 months gained about 18 letters versus 7 with sham in BRVO (BRAVO) and about 15 versus 1 in CRVO (CRUISE). SCORE: intravitreal triamcinolone 1 mg was no better than grid laser in BRVO but beat observation in CRVO (27% versus 7% gained 15 letters); 4 mg caused more cataract and raised IOP. Aflibercept gave similar gains (VIBRANT, GALILEO, COPERNICUS).

Q: When do you prefer the dexamethasone implant?
A: Pseudophakic eyes, patients who cannot attend monthly, recent stroke or heart attack, pregnancy, or poor anti-VEGF response. The effect peaks at about 2 months and lasts 4–6 months (GENEVA); about a quarter need drops for raised IOP, and phakic eyes develop cataract.

Q: How do you treat neovascularisation after CRVO?
A: Prompt full panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) once NVI reaches 2 clock hours or any NVA, NVD or NVE appears, with anti-VEGF for quick regression and IOP control; review monthly for the first 6 months. CVOS found prophylactic PRP did not reliably prevent NVI; it is given only when follow-up is impossible.

Q: When is vitrectomy needed?
A: Non-clearing vitreous haemorrhage, tractional detachment, and epiretinal membrane or vitreomacular traction with persistent oedema.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: What are the complications of BRVO?
A: Macular oedema (the commonest cause of poor vision), macular ischaemia, NVE with vitreous haemorrhage, tractional or rhegmatogenous detachment and epiretinal membrane; NVI is rare (about 1%).

Q: What is the prognosis?
A: BRVO does well: over half reach 6/12 or better, many as collaterals form; macular ischaemia or foveal haemorrhage limit recovery. In CRVO presenting acuity predicts outcome; about a third of non-ischaemic eyes become ischaemic within 3 years, and ischaemic eyes carry a high risk of neovascular glaucoma (the "90-day glaucoma"). The fellow eye risk is about 1% a year.

Q: What is the link with primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG)?
A: Raised IOP and posterior bowing of the lamina cribrosa compress the central retinal vein, so glaucoma or ocular hypertension is the commonest ocular risk factor for CRVO and hemiretinal occlusion. CRVO in turn causes neovascular glaucoma, and steroid treatment can raise IOP.

Q: How does POAG change the plan in S.No 52?
A: Measure IOP and examine the disc and angle in both eyes; gonioscopy for NVA in the CRVO eye; treat both eyes to a target pressure, since controlling IOP may lower the risk of a second occlusion; avoid steroids in a steroid responder; follow fields and disc.

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: Why does grid laser help BRVO but not CRVO?
A: In BRVO the oedema is sectoral, with a healthy perifoveal circulation and collaterals across the raphe; in CRVO leakage is diffuse in all four quadrants around the fovea, so CVOS grid laser dried the angiogram without improving vision.

Q: What do OCTA and wide-field imaging add?
A: OCTA shows non-perfusion and collaterals without dye; ultra-wide-field FFA maps peripheral non-perfusion, which predicts oedema recurrence and guides targeted laser.

Q: What treatments have failed in retinal vein occlusion?
A: Systemic anticoagulants and haemodilution (no proven benefit), tissue plasminogen activator, laser chorioretinal venous anastomosis, radial optic neurotomy and AV sheathotomy (cutting the shared sheath at the crossing).

Q: Why is S.No 34 a trap?
A: It combines an old BRVO with proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) and glaucoma: tell sectoral collaterals from diabetic NVE, look for NVA on gonioscopy, and plan PRP for the PDR while sparing collaterals.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Lasering collaterals as if they were new vessels.
- Calling a sheathed venule "vasculitis" without relating it to an AV crossing.
- Dilating before checking RAPD, the pupil margin and gonioscopy for NVI and NVA.
- Advising prophylactic sector laser in ischaemic BRVO (BVOS says treat only when new vessels appear).
- Quoting 10 disc areas for ischaemic BRVO: it is over 5 for BRVO and over 10 for CRVO.
- Forgetting IOP and glaucoma in the fellow eye of a CRVO (S.No 52), and systemic work-up in the young.
- Doing FFA through dense fresh haemorrhage instead of waiting about 3 months.
:::
