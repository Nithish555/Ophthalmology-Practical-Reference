## At a glance
- **Ectopia lentis**: displacement of the crystalline lens from its normal place behind the pupil (the patellar fossa). **Subluxation**: partly displaced, some zonules intact, lens still in the pupil area. **Dislocation (luxation)**: all zonules gone, lens wholly out of the fossa — in the anterior chamber, in the vitreous (floating or on the retina), under the conjunctiva (phacocele) or out of the eye.
- **Trauma** causes about half; **Marfan syndrome** is the commonest heritable cause. Bilateral, symmetrical, non-traumatic ectopia lentis means a systemic cause until proved otherwise.
- Kept because the signs are vivid and the viva covers systemic disease, glaucoma mechanisms and the choice of surgery.
- **S.No 39 ★**: "Left eye posterior dislocation of the crystalline lens into the vitreous cavity (clear or cataractous, mobile or fixed), with functional aphakia; cause — traumatic, or the systemic cause found; right eye normal (or subluxated, with direction)."
- If the lens lies in front of the iris: "Left eye anterior dislocation of the lens into the anterior chamber with pupil-block glaucoma" — an emergency.

## Diagnosis and summary to say
:::say
Mr X, a 24-year-old man, has had painless blurring of the left eye for 6 months; he denies injury and there is no family history of heart disease or sudden death. He is tall with long fingers and positive thumb and wrist signs. The left anterior chamber is deep, the iris tremulous and the pupil black; indirect ophthalmoscopy shows a clear, mobile lens in the inferior vitreous, with an attached retina and lattice degeneration inferotemporally. Corrected vision is 6/9 with +10.50 D sphere, IOP is 16 mmHg (millimetres of mercury) with an open angle, and the eye is quiet. The right lens is subluxated superotemporally, with stretched zonules visible at its edge.

**Diagnosis (S.No 39):** Left eye posterior dislocation of a clear crystalline lens into the vitreous with functional aphakia, and right eye superotemporal subluxation — bilateral ectopia lentis, probably Marfan syndrome; no glaucoma or uveitis at present.

**If traumatic:** Left eye traumatic posterior dislocation of the lens into the vitreous, with (angle recession or other injuries), right eye normal.
:::

## Viva questions and answers
### Basics
Q: Define ectopia lentis, subluxation and dislocation.
A: Ectopia lentis: displacement of the lens from its normal position. Subluxation: partial displacement with some zonules intact, the lens still within the lens space. Dislocation (luxation): complete separation, the lens lying outside the patellar fossa — anterior chamber, vitreous, retinal surface, under the conjunctiva or extruded.

Q: Classify the causes of ectopia lentis.
A: Trauma (commonest). Heritable without systemic disease: familial ectopia lentis, ectopia lentis et pupillae, aniridia. With systemic disease: Marfan, homocystinuria, Weill–Marchesani, sulphite oxidase deficiency, hyperlysinaemia, Ehlers–Danlos. Eye disease: pseudoexfoliation, high myopia, buphthalmos, megalocornea, hypermature cataract, uveitis, tumour, syphilis.

Q: Compare Marfan syndrome and homocystinuria.
A: Marfan: autosomal dominant, FBN1 (fibrillin-1) on 15q21; superotemporal subluxation; zonules stretched but present; normal intelligence; aortic root dilatation, mitral valve prolapse; nitroprusside negative. Homocystinuria: autosomal recessive, cystathionine beta-synthase deficiency; inferonasal subluxation; zonules broken; learning difficulty, fits, fair hair, osteoporosis, thromboembolism; nitroprusside positive.

Q: How often does ectopia lentis occur in these conditions?
A: About 50–80% of people with Marfan syndrome, bilateral, usually stable after childhood. In untreated homocystinuria about 90% by the mid-teens, and often progressive. Microspherophakia is almost constant in Weill–Marchesani syndrome.

Q: What is Weill–Marchesani syndrome, and what is inverse glaucoma?
A: Short stature, brachydactyly, stiff joints; autosomal recessive (ADAMTS10) or dominant (FBN1); a small spherical lens with high lenticular myopia that moves forward and causes pupil block. Miotics worsen this (inverse glaucoma) by relaxing the zonules; cycloplegics tighten them and pull the lens back. Treat with laser iridotomy, then lens removal.

Q: Why does a subluxated lens cause myopia and astigmatism?
A: Loss of zonular tension lets the lens round up and gain power (lenticular myopia); uneven loss tilts it and curves it unequally, giving lenticular astigmatism, often irregular. When the edge crosses the pupil there is a phakic and an aphakic zone, so the patient has uniocular diplopia.

### Examination and signs
Q: What are the signs of a subluxated lens?
A: Uneven anterior chamber depth; iridodonesis and phacodonesis; the lens edge visible in the pupil with stretched or broken zonules; a dark crescent on the red reflex; vitreous in the anterior chamber; lens tilt; high myopic astigmatism.

Q: What are iridodonesis and phacodonesis?
A: Iridodonesis is quivering of the iris on eye movement, from loss of lens support (subluxation, dislocation, aphakia). Phacodonesis is quivering of the lens itself, seen at the slit lamp with the pupil dilated, from zonular weakness.

Q: What does a lens dislocated into the anterior chamber look like?
A: A clear lens looks like a large oil droplet with a golden rim in front of the iris; a cataractous lens looks like a white disc. It causes pupil block with high IOP, pain, and corneal oedema from endothelial touch.

Q: What are the signs of a lens dislocated into the vitreous?
A: Deep anterior chamber, iridodonesis, black pupil, aphakic refraction (+10 to +12 D), Purkinje images 3 and 4 absent, no surgical scar, and the lens seen in the vitreous — mobile in a liquid vitreous or lying on the inferior retina.

Q: What is the usual direction of displacement?
A: Marfan: superotemporal; homocystinuria: inferonasal; Weill–Marchesani: forward into the chamber or downward; trauma: any direction, away from the torn zonules; ectopia lentis et pupillae: opposite to the pupil. Direction suggests the cause but does not prove it.

### Investigations
Q: Which systemic investigations would you order?
A: Urine cyanide–nitroprusside test (purple-red colour with homocystine), confirmed by plasma total homocysteine and amino acids (high methionine); echocardiography (aortic root size, mitral valve prolapse) and electrocardiogram (ECG); FBN1 genetic testing; urine sulphite and S-sulphocysteine for sulphite oxidase deficiency; syphilis serology in adults; examination of relatives.

Q: What are the revised Ghent criteria?
A: Without family history, Marfan is diagnosed by: aortic root dilatation (Z-score 2 or more) with ectopia lentis; or aortic dilatation with an FBN1 mutation; or aortic dilatation with a systemic score of 7 or more; or ectopia lentis with an FBN1 mutation linked to aortic disease. With a family history: ectopia lentis, or a systemic score of 7 or more, or aortic dilatation.

Q: What is in the systemic score?
A: Out of 20: wrist and thumb signs 3 (one alone 1); pectus carinatum 2; hind-foot deformity 2; pneumothorax 2; dural ectasia 2; protrusio acetabuli 2; reduced upper-to-lower segment ratio with increased arm span 1; scoliosis 1; myopia over 3 D 1; mitral valve prolapse 1; facial features, skin striae and reduced elbow extension 1 each.

Q: Which eye investigations help?
A: B-scan (lens position, retinal detachment) when the media are hazy — the lens appears as an oval echogenic body; UBM for zonules and ciliary body; biometry, remembering readings may be unreliable; specular microscopy if the lens touched the cornea; macular optical coherence tomography.

### Management
Q: How is a subluxated lens managed without surgery?
A: Careful refraction through both zones; when the edge crosses the pupil, correcting the aphakic part (about +10 to +12 D) often gives the best vision; contact lens; occlusion for amblyopia in children; IOP control; regular retinal review.

Q: What are the indications for surgery?
A: Lens in the anterior chamber; pupil-block or lens-induced glaucoma; lens-induced uveitis; cataract; uncorrectable vision or amblyopia not responding to treatment; lens touching the cornea; RD needing surgery.

Q: How do you manage a lens in the anterior chamber?
A: As an emergency: lower IOP (intravenous mannitol, oral acetazolamide); constrict the pupil with pilocarpine to trap the lens in front of the iris; remove it whole through a limbal incision (intracapsular extraction with cryoprobe or vectis), with anterior vitrectomy and peripheral iridectomy; place a secondary intraocular lens (IOL) later.

Q: How do you manage a lens dislocated into the vitreous?
A: A clear lens in a quiet eye with normal IOP may be watched, with aphakic correction. Operate for inflammation, glaucoma, cataract, RD, or to fit an IOL: three-port pars plana vitrectomy, perfluorocarbon liquid (PFCL) to float the lens off the retina, fragmentation with a fragmatome (or limbal removal of a hard lens), laser to breaks, then a scleral-fixated, glued or iris-claw IOL.

Q: How is a nucleus dropped during cataract surgery managed?
A: The cataract surgeon should not chase it: anterior vitrectomy, an IOL if support allows, watertight wound closure, steroids and IOP control, and referral. Vitrectomy with fragmentation (PFCL to protect the macula) is best within about 2 weeks, sooner for uncontrolled inflammation, IOP or RD; small cortical pieces may be watched.

Q: How do you plan surgery for a subluxated cataractous lens?
A: By clock hours of zonular loss: up to 3, phacoemulsification with an in-the-bag foldable IOL; 3–5, add a capsular tension ring (CTR); 5–7, a Cionni ring sutured to the sclera or capsular tension segments; 7 or more, intracapsular extraction or lensectomy with anterior vitrectomy and a scleral-fixated or anterior chamber IOL.

Q: What precautions apply during surgery on a subluxated lens?
A: Dispersive viscoelastic to plug the dialysis; capsulorhexis started away from it; capsular hooks to support the bag; low flow, vacuum and bottle height ("slow-motion" phacoemulsification); cortex stripped tangentially; little IOL rotation; ready to do an anterior vitrectomy.

Q: What does a capsular tension ring do?
A: An open polymethyl methacrylate ring placed in the bag after capsulorhexis; it spreads force to the intact zonules, keeps the bag round, and reduces decentration and capsular contraction. It is not enough for progressive zonulopathy without scleral fixation, and is contraindicated with a torn rhexis or posterior capsule.

Q: How do you treat ectopia lentis in a child with Marfan syndrome?
A: Mostly optically, with careful refraction (often aphakic correction through the clear zone) and occlusion. Surgery if the edge bisects the pupil and vision is uncorrectable: lensectomy with anterior vitrectomy, aphakic contact lens or later a scleral-fixated IOL; intralenticular aspiration keeps a capsular rim for a later sulcus IOL.

Q: What are the anaesthetic risks in homocystinuria and Marfan syndrome?
A: Homocystinuria: thromboembolism (stroke, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction) around general anaesthesia — prefer local anaesthesia, lower homocysteine first (pyridoxine, folate, vitamin B12, betaine), avoid nitrous oxide, hydrate well, compression stockings, early mobilisation. Marfan: aortic dissection and valve disease — cardiology review, continue beta-blockers, avoid hypertension and tachycardia.

### Complications and prognosis
Q: What are the complications of ectopia lentis?
A: Amblyopia; glaucoma (pupil block, phacolytic, lens-particle, phacoanaphylactic, angle recession); lens-induced uveitis; corneal decompensation from lens touch; cataract; RD (5–11% in Marfan, 8–38% when ectopia lentis is present); vitreous haemorrhage.

Q: What is phacolytic glaucoma?
A: A hypermature lens, often dislocated, leaks high-molecular-weight protein through an intact capsule; protein-laden macrophages block the trabecular meshwork. Open angle, very high IOP, white or iridescent particles in the aqueous, no keratic precipitates. Lower IOP, then remove the lens.

Q: What is phacoanaphylactic uveitis?
A: A granulomatous immune reaction to lens protein released through a breached capsule (trauma, retained fragments): mutton-fat keratic precipitates, flare, sometimes hypopyon. Treat with steroids and complete removal of lens matter.

Q: Why is retinal detachment common, and how do you watch for it?
A: Axial myopia, vitreous liquefaction, lattice degeneration, and traction from a mobile lens on the vitreous base. Examine the periphery with indentation at each visit, laser any breaks, and repair RD by vitrectomy, often with lensectomy.

Q: What is the prognosis?
A: Vision is good if amblyopia is prevented and RD is caught early. Life expectancy in Marfan depends on the aorta (beta-blockers or losartan, elective root replacement); in homocystinuria on thrombosis control (about half respond to pyridoxine).

### Recent advances and favourite examiner questions
Q: How is homocystinuria treated?
A: High-dose pyridoxine (vitamin B6), with folate and vitamin B12; betaine for non-responders; a low-methionine diet with cystine supplements, aiming to keep plasma total homocysteine low. Early treatment reduces lens dislocation, thrombosis and learning difficulty.

Q: What newer devices help with weak zonules?
A: Cionni ring, Ahmed capsular tension segments, capsular anchors, sutureless intrascleral IOL fixation (glued IOL, Yamane flanged technique), and retropupillary iris-claw lenses.

Q: What is sulphite oxidase deficiency?
A: A rare autosomal recessive disorder (alone or as molybdenum cofactor deficiency) with intractable infantile fits, developmental delay and early ectopia lentis; diagnosed by sulphite in fresh urine and raised S-sulphocysteine; the outlook is poor.

Q: Why do miotics harm and cycloplegics help in microspherophakia?
A: Miotics contract the ciliary muscle, relaxing the zonules so the round lens moves forward and tightens the pupil block; cycloplegics relax the muscle, tighten the zonules and flatten and pull the lens back.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Saying "dislocated lens" without saying where it lies (anterior chamber or vitreous) and whether it is clear, mobile or inflamed.
- Skipping the general examination and the fellow eye: bilateral non-traumatic ectopia lentis means a systemic cause.
- Giving pilocarpine for pupil block in microspherophakia.
- Planning general anaesthesia in homocystinuria without thrombosis precautions, or Marfan surgery without an echocardiogram.
- Treating the direction of displacement as diagnostic.
- Rushing to remove a clear, quiet lens in the vitreous.
- Not examining the peripheral retina.
- Forgetting to refract through the aphakic zone.
:::
