## At a glance
- **Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) with a glaucoma drainage device (GDD)**: a tube drains aqueous to a plate at the equator. It usually follows a **failed trabeculectomy**.
- Why it is kept: a stable patient, a tube and plate easy to show, and a long surgical viva.
- What examiners test: tube, plate cover, motility, intraocular pressure (IOP) control, the devices, the operation and its complications.

For primary open-angle glaucoma itself, see card G1.

**Keywords the examiner listens for:** alloplastic tube · equatorial reservoir · fibrous capsule · Ahmed glaucoma valve · Venturi effect · Molteno implant · Baerveldt implant · superotemporal quadrant · scleral patch graft · tube exposure · tube–corneal touch · pars plana tube · hypertensive phase · pseudo-Brown's syndrome · cyclophotocoagulation

## History — positive and negative, with reasons
**Chief complaint:** "Mr X, 65-year-old male, has come for regular follow-up."

**History of present illness (house wording):**
"Patient was apparently normal __ years before, after which he had defective vision in both eyes. It was gradual in onset, progressive in nature, not associated with pain or headache. He was found to have increased pressure in both eyes and started on eye drops. He underwent trabeculectomy in the right eye __ years ago. As the pressure rose again, a glaucoma drainage device was put in the right eye __ years ago at __. Then he came to our institution for further follow-up."

### Step 1 — Visual acuity
**Do:** Test each eye with the Snellen chart, then pinhole, then near vision. Ask him to cover the other eye without pressing.

**Record:** "RE (right eye) 6/18, PH 6/12; LE (left eye) 6/9; N8 at 33 cm."

Q: Why does useful vision matter here?
A: **Potential for useful vision** is a must before device surgery. An eye without it is treated by cyclodestruction instead.
Q: Why may vision fall after a device?
A: **Cataract progression**, hypotony maculopathy and chorioretinal folds from prolonged hypotony, and corneal oedema or graft failure. Exclude glaucoma progression too.

### Step 2 — Ocular motility and diplopia
**Do:** Cover test in the primary position, then movements in nine gazes, asking about double vision in each.

**Record:** "Orthophoric; EOM full in all gazes; no diplopia."

Q: Why test motility in a device eye?
A: The plate lies between two recti under a bulky bleb. **Diplopia** comes from the bleb displacing the globe or from muscle fibrosis.
Q: Why is the plate put superotemporally, not superonasally?
A: The **superotemporal quadrant** is preferred; inferonasal is next. A superonasal plate causes vertical strabismus (**pseudo-Brown's syndrome**) and can impact on the optic nerve.
Q: What is Brown's syndrome?
A: **Brown's syndrome** is absent elevation in adduction with normal elevation in abduction; forced duction is positive. A superonasal plate can mimic it.

### Step 3 — The plate and tube: conjunctival cover, patch graft, exposure
**Do:** Ask him to look down and away from the plate; lift the upper lid gently. Inspect the bleb, the patch graft and the conjunctiva over the tube. Then look at 12 o'clock for the old trabeculectomy bleb.

**Record:** "Superotemporal bleb over the plate; patch graft covered; no tube or plate exposure; no dellen. Old bleb at 12 o'clock: flat, opaque, highly vascular with corkscrew vessels. No microcysts; subconjunctival fibrosis present. Suggestive of a failed bleb."

Q: How does a device bleb differ from a trabeculectomy bleb?
A: A **device bleb** is more posterior, with an organised capsule separable from Tenon's capsule. It has no conjunctival microcysts.
Q: Why a scleral patch graft, and its complications?
A: A **scleral patch graft** covers the 3–5 mm of tube exposed to lid contact. It prevents tube erosion, hypotony, tube–corneal touch and scleral perforation. Complications: dellen and scleral melt. A dellen is treated with artificial tears and patching.
Q: Why does tube exposure matter, and how is erosion managed?
A: **Tube exposure** is associated with endophthalmitis. Erosion follows graft melting with poor lid closure or dry eye. A **stent** (Storz silastic tube) lengthens and protects the tube.
Q: How do you describe the old trabeculectomy bleb?
A: Height, clarity, horizontal extent, location over the limbus, vascularity, microcysts, subconjunctival fibrosis and sutures. A flat, vascular, fibrosed bleb without microcysts suggests a **failed bleb**. The signs of a **failing bleb** are reduced height, a thicker wall, vascularisation, loss of microcysts and raised pressure.

### Step 4 — Anterior chamber and the tube: position, touch, occlusion
**Do:** At the slit lamp, note the tube's clock hour, length, bevel, course, gaps from endothelium and iris, and tip. Grade the chamber by Van Herick.

**Record:** "Tube at 10 o'clock, 2 mm, bevel up, parallel to the iris, clear of cornea and iris, tip patent; AC VH grade IV (ND), quiet."

Q: What is the correct position of the tube?
A: It enters through a **23-gauge needle tract** at the posterior edge of the blue limbus, **bevel up**. About **1.5–2 mm** lies inside, parallel to the iris; this prevents migration.
Q: When does the tube migrate?
A: In **postoperative hypotony** it moves forward onto the cornea, lens or iris. With poor plate fixation, the plate slides back and the tube extrudes.
Q: What blocks the tube?
A: **Blood, fibrin, iris or vitreous**. Vitreous in the chamber is noted before surgery. In uveitis, steroid cover stops exudates blocking the lumen.
Q: How do you examine the old iridectomy?
A: A **surgical iridectomy** has sharp, well-defined edges and normal surrounding iris. A laser iridotomy has ragged edges with pigment dispersion. On **retroillumination**, a light reflex seen through the defect means it is patent.

### Step 5 — Cornea and endothelium
**Do:** Examine the whole cornea, above all near the tube tip, for oedema and Descemet's folds. Stain with fluorescein 1% for surface toxicity.

**Record:** "Cornea clear; no oedema near the tube; no tube–corneal touch."

Q: Why does the cornea decompensate after a device?
A: **Endothelial cell loss**, from needle-tip trauma at surgery or tube–corneal touch. Oedema and graft failure occur with or without touch.
Q: How do you assess the endothelium?
A: **Specular microscopy** gives the endothelial cell density per square millimetre. A normal cornea has 60–80% hexagonal cells; pleomorphism means stress.

### Step 6 — Tonometry
**Do:** Goldmann applanation before gonioscopy and dilatation, with anaesthetic, fluorescein and cobalt-blue light; align the green mires.

**Record:** "Tn by GAT 14 mm Hg (RE), 16 mm Hg (LE) at 10 am; timolol 0.5% BD (twice daily)."

Q: Why tonometry before gonioscopy and dilatation?
A: **Gonioscopy** presses on the cornea and lowers the next reading. **Dilatation** raises the pressure by 4–5 mm Hg for a while.
Q: What causes a high pressure with a device?
A: A **tied tube**, a **blocked tube**, a **thick fibrous capsule**, or the hypertensive phase. Exclude poor compliance.
Q: What causes a low pressure?
A: **Hypotony** from a leak around the tube or excessive run-off. Look for a flat chamber, choroidal effusion and hypotony maculopathy.

### Step 7 — Gonioscopy
**Do:** After tonometry, use a Goldmann 2-mirror lens; a 4-mirror lens for indentation. Note the deepest structure in each quadrant.

**Record:** A cross diagram for each eye with the deepest structure seen.

RE | S=SS | T=SS | I=SS | N=SS

Q: What do you look for on gonioscopy in a device eye?
A: The **tube tip** (free or blocked), **PAS** (areas to avoid for a tube) and **new vessels in the angle**.
Q: What is done about angle new vessels before surgery?
A: They are treated first, to halt new growth, by **panretinal photocoagulation** or goniophotocoagulation.

### Step 8 — Optic disc and fundus
**Do:** Dilate after tonometry and gonioscopy. Examine the disc with a +90 dioptre (D) lens and the periphery with the indirect ophthalmoscope.

**Record:** "Disc vertically oval, CDR 0.8, rim thin superiorly and inferiorly; no folds; periphery normal."

Q: Which posterior-segment signs follow a device?
A: **Chorioretinal folds** and hypotony maculopathy from prolonged hypotony; choroidal effusion; retinal detachment after scleral perforation or with diabetic retinopathy.
Q: How is a scleral perforation at surgery recognised and treated?
A: A **gush of vitreous gel** is seen. Cryotherapy is applied to the area under the indirect ophthalmoscope.

### Step 9 — Fields
**Do:** Confrontation in each eye; then request automated perimetry.

**Record:** "Fields by confrontation: nasal constriction in RE; full in LE."

Q: Why do fields matter after a device?
A: **Fields** show whether glaucoma still progresses at this pressure. Progression despite treatment is the trigger to step up.
Q: How often do you repeat them?
A: **Every 6 months** in mild to moderate disease; every 3 months in advanced disease.

## How I will manage
### Aim
Keep the pressure at target, keep the device working and covered, and catch tube, corneal and motility problems early.

### The ladder
1. **Device working, pressure at target:** I will continue his drops. Every visit, I will check the tube, cornea, plate cover, motility and pressure.
2. **Pressure above target:** I will look for poor compliance, a tied tube, a blocked tip or a thick capsule. I will add drops, never a miotic or an alpha agonist.
3. **Early high pressure with a tied tube:** this rise is a risk to a nerve with advanced damage. The tie holds for 2–3 weeks while the capsule forms. Then I will release it by argon laser suturolysis, or pull the rip cord.
4. **Tube exposure or erosion:** I will repair it urgently with a stent, because exposure is associated with endophthalmitis.
5. **Uncontrolled after repeated surgical failure, or poor visual potential:** I will advise transscleral diode cyclophotocoagulation.

## What you must know
### Definition
A **glaucoma drainage device** (aqueous shunt device) "consists of an alloplastic tube leading to a large equatorial reservoir that drains aqueous humour from the anterior chamber". In plain words, a tube takes aqueous to a plate near the equator, where a bleb absorbs it.

**Parts:** a tube (anterior chamber or pars plana), a plate (explant) at the equator, and a valve if valved. Over them lie the fibrous capsule (bleb) and a scleral patch graft. All devices use materials fibroblasts cannot firmly adhere to.

**Related terms:**
- **Seton**: non-lumened; aqueous flows along its surface.
- **Shunt**: a hollow tube allowing two-way flow.
- **Valve**: one-way flow that needs an activating pressure.
- **Hydraulic conductivity**: drainage rises with capsule area; resistance rises with capsule thickness.
- **Hypertensive phase**: a transient pressure rise soon after surgery, with poor control although the device works.

### Pathogenesis
1. The plate stimulates a **fibrous capsule**, which becomes the wall of the bleb.
2. Aqueous drains down the tube into the space between plate and capsule.
3. It passes by passive diffusion into the periocular tissues.
4. Lymphatics and venous capillaries take it up.

A valved device works at once. A non-valved tube is tied for 2–3 weeks while the capsule forms and resists outflow.

### Indications and preoperative checks
- **Refractory glaucomas:** uveitic, neovascular, congenital, refractory juvenile, after keratoplasty, aniridia, post-traumatic, iridocorneal endothelial (ICE) syndrome, pseudophakic, after vitrectomy.
- **Failed filtering surgery:** failed trabeculectomies, failure with antimetabolites, or conjunctiva too scarred to dissect. Also failed primary glaucomas.
- **Checks:** useful vision (a must); lid position; tear film; blepharitis; peripheral corneal clarity; vitreous in the AC; cataract; gonioscopy.
- **Drugs:** stop pilocarpine or echothiophate and warfarin; continue other glaucoma drops.

### The operation — key steps
1. Limbus-based or fornix-based conjunctival flap.
2. **Superotemporal quadrant.**
3. Plate centred at the equator between two recti, front edge **8–10 mm** behind the limbus, sutured to episclera.
4. Non-valved tube tied for **2–3 weeks**: rip cord, laser-released tie, or nylon to a spacer.
5. AC tube: **bevel up**, through a **23-gauge needle tract** at the posterior edge of the blue limbus. About **1.5–2 mm** lies inside, parallel to the iris.
6. Or **pars plana tube**: complete vitrectomy; **3–4 mm** behind the limbus; **bevel down**.
7. Full-thickness **scleral patch graft** over the tube.
8. Reform the AC with sodium hyaluronate (Healon).

### Prognosis
- **Good:** useful vision; a quiet eye; good lid closure; in neovascular glaucoma, anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) and photocoagulation first.
- **Poor:** active inflammation; angle recession; silicone oil, which can migrate up the tube; dry eye; a thick fibrous capsule.

### Recent advances
- *(extra)* Neovascular glaucoma with vitreous haemorrhage: inject anti-VEGF into the vitreous (bevacizumab 1.25 mg/0.05 ml). Do it 2–3 days before vitrectomy, photocoagulation and a valve; success is high.

## Viva questions
### Why did you ask…?
Q: Why did you ask about previous trabeculectomy?
A: **Multiple failed trabeculectomies** and extensive conjunctival scarring are the main indications for a device.
Q: Why did you ask about diabetes and retinal laser?
A: They point to **neovascular glaucoma**, where a device is often the primary operation. Diabetic retinopathy also raises the risk of retinal detachment.
Q: Why did you ask about warfarin?
A: **Warfarin** is stopped before device surgery, with pilocarpine or echothiophate (to reduce postoperative inflammation). Other glaucoma drops continue until surgery.
Q: Why did you ask about a sticky discharge?
A: **Blepharitis** raises the risk of late infection after device surgery. Endophthalmitis is associated with tube exposure, so a discharge sends me looking for a bare tube.

### Diagnosis and differentials
Q: Define a glaucoma drainage device.
A: A **glaucoma drainage device** "consists of an **alloplastic tube** leading to a large **equatorial reservoir** that drains aqueous humour from the anterior chamber".
Q: What are the indications for a device?
A: **All refractory glaucomas**, such as uveitic, neovascular, congenital and post-traumatic. Also multiple failed trabeculectomies, extensive conjunctival scarring and failed primary glaucomas.
Q: What is the principle of a drainage device?
A: The plate stimulates a **fibrous capsule**, the bleb wall. Aqueous collects between plate and capsule, diffuses into periocular tissues, and lymphatics and veins take it up.
Q: Why did his trabeculectomy fail?
A: **Subconjunctival fibrosis** is the most common cause of bleb failure. Other causes are a fistula blocked by clot, iris, ciliary body, lens or vitreous, and poor surgical technique.
Q: What are setons, shunts and valves?
A: A **seton** is non-lumened. A **shunt** is hollow, with two-way flow. A **valve** allows one-way flow and needs an activating pressure.
Q: What decides how well a device drains?
A: **Hydraulic conductivity**: drainage is directly proportional to capsule area; resistance is directly proportional to capsule thickness.

### Investigations
Q: Why do diurnal pressures matter?
A: **Diurnal variation** shows the baseline, the swings and the peak. Keep the pressure below target with a swing under 5 mm Hg.
Q: What do you check before device surgery?
A: **Useful vision**, lid position, scleral exposure, tear film, blepharitis and peripheral corneal clarity. Then the lens, vitreous in the chamber, and gonioscopy.

### Management — drugs
Q: Which drops are contraindicated after a device?
A: **Miotics and alpha agonists**, because they increase vascularisation of the bleb capsule.
Q: Why avoid prostaglandin analogues in some device eyes?
A: In **neovascular glaucoma** they may increase inflammation. In **Sturge–Weber syndrome** they are of no benefit, as episcleral venous pressure is high; they may cause anterior uveal effusion.
Q: Why steroid cover after a valve in uveitic glaucoma?
A: The **Ahmed valve** goes in only when inflammation is controlled. **Steroid cover** stops exudates blocking the tube early after surgery.

### Management — laser and surgery
Q: How does the Ahmed glaucoma valve work?
A: The **Ahmed glaucoma valve** has two silicone membranes acting as a one-way valve by the **Venturi effect** (Bernoulli's principle). It allows outflow only above 8–10 mm Hg. At a high pressure it opens fully; as the pressure falls, the opening narrows.
Q: Compare valved and non-valved devices.
A: A **valved device** is a simple one-step procedure. Safety and efficacy otherwise appear equal; in Sturge–Weber syndrome the Ahmed does better than the Molteno.
Q: Why is a non-valved tube tied?
A: The **tie** gives 2–3 weeks for the capsule to form and resist outflow. Without it, early drainage is unrestricted.
Q: How does a device compare with trabeculectomy?
A: The **percentage pressure fall is smaller** with a device. A device follows failed filtering surgery; trabeculectomy follows failed medical treatment.
Q: Why a device rather than a repeat trabeculectomy with mitomycin C?
A: After failed filtration, the options are a **repeat filtering procedure with antimetabolites** or a **drainage implant**. Mitomycin C is used at 0.2–0.5 mg/ml for 1–5 minutes. A device is chosen when the conjunctiva is too scarred to dissect, or when surgery with antimetabolites has already failed.
Q: What if the pressure stays high despite the device?
A: **Cyclodestruction**: transscleral diode or endolaser cyclophotocoagulation reduces aqueous production. Risks: hypotony, phthisis bulbi, retinal detachment.
Q: What are the indications for cyclophotocoagulation?
A: **Cyclophotocoagulation** relieves refractory pain from high pressure in a blind eye. It also treats repeated failure of other glaucoma surgeries and glaucoma after keratoplasty. It suits high-risk eyes where other surgery is not feasible.
Q: When do you choose a pars plana tube?
A: A **pars plana tube** suits aphakic or pseudophakic eyes, eyes after keratoplasty, a shallow chamber or poor endothelium.
Q: What are the advantages of the Baerveldt implant?
A: The **Baerveldt implant** is easy to implant, drains more than the **Molteno implant** and fits the globe. Its fenestrations lower the bleb and reduce diplopia.
Q: Why a needle tract rather than a sclerectomy?
A: A **21- or 23-gauge needle tract** gives a tight fit and prevents aqueous leak around the tube.
Q: Why reform the chamber with Healon?
A: **Sodium hyaluronate** reduces postoperative hypotony and a flat chamber. It shows the tube position and slows early drainage.

### Complications
Q: What are the complications of valve implantation?
A: **Early or late failure**, corneal decompensation, diplopia, hypotony, hyphaema, scleral perforation, tube erosion with endophthalmitis, and plate migration or expulsion.
Q: What causes hypotony after device surgery?
A: **Leak around the tube** where it passes the eye wall, in both one- and two-stage surgery. A wide sclerotomy or excess run-off adds to it.
Q: The chamber is flat after device surgery. How do you find the cause?
A: **Tonometry** first. A low pressure with choroidal effusion means hypotony from excess run-off. A diffusely shallow chamber with normal or high pressure suggests **aqueous misdirection** (malignant glaucoma). Ultrasound biomicroscopy shows the ciliary processes rotated forward. B-scan tells a serous effusion (anechoic) from a haemorrhage (echoic).
Q: What are the phases after device surgery?
A: A **hypertensive phase** and a **hypotensive phase**. The hypertensive phase is a transient pressure rise soon after surgery, with poor control although the device works.

## Examiner traps
:::trap
- Calling the bleb over the plate a trabeculectomy bleb, or expecting microcysts.
- "Tube in the AC" without clock hour, length, bevel, course, gaps and tip.
- Not lifting the upper lid to see the plate, graft and any exposure.
- Skipping extraocular movements and diplopia.
- Missing the old trabeculectomy bleb at 12 o'clock and its iridectomy.
- Doing gonioscopy before tonometry.
- Advising pilocarpine or brimonidine after a device.
- Offering a device to an eye without useful vision.
:::

## Say-it script
### Opening (about 1 minute)
:::say
Mr X, a 65-year-old man, has come for regular follow-up. He was apparently normal 10 years ago. Then he had defective vision in both eyes, gradual in onset and progressive, without pain or headache. He was found to have raised pressure in both eyes and was started on eye drops.

Six years ago he had a trabeculectomy in the right eye. The pressure rose again, and three years ago an Ahmed glaucoma valve was put in. He had double vision for two weeks after surgery, which settled.

He has no double vision now, and no redness, pain, watering or discharge. He has no headache with vomiting, coloured haloes, trauma or frequent change of glasses. He has had no retinal laser, injections into the eye, or red eye treated with steroids.

He has been diabetic for eight years, on tablets. He has no asthma or sulpha allergy and takes no warfarin. He uses timolol 0.5 percent twice daily in both eyes, regularly and without side effects.
:::

### Closing (2 minutes)
:::say
To summarise, Mr X is a 65-year-old man with primary open-angle glaucoma in both eyes. He had a failed trabeculectomy and then an Ahmed glaucoma valve in the right eye.

Visual acuity is 6/18, improving to 6/12 with pinhole, on the right and 6/9 on the left. Eye movements are full, without diplopia. In the right eye, a bleb lies over the plate in the superotemporal quadrant. The scleral patch graft is covered, with no tube exposure. The old trabeculectomy bleb at twelve o'clock is flat and vascular, suggestive of a failed bleb. The tube enters the chamber at ten o'clock, two millimetres long, bevel up and parallel to the iris. There is no tube–corneal touch, and the tip is patent. At ten in the morning, Goldmann applanation pressure is 14 millimetres of mercury on the right. It is 16 on the left. Gonioscopy shows open angles. The cup–disc ratio is 0.8 on the right and 0.6 on the left.

My diagnosis is: right eye primary open-angle glaucoma, severe stage, status post trabeculectomy with failed bleb and glaucoma drainage device implantation. The tube is well positioned and patent. Intraocular pressure is under control on medical management. There is grade 2 nuclear sclerosis. Left eye primary open-angle glaucoma, moderate stage, on medical management.

I will confirm control with diurnal tonometry, and stage the damage with fields and optical coherence tomography. I will continue his drops, avoiding miotics and alpha agonists. If the pressure rises despite this, I will consider cyclophotocoagulation.
:::

## Quick recall
:::recall
- Device: alloplastic tube + equatorial plate; aqueous → capsule space → tissues → lymphatics and veins.
- Drainage ∝ capsule area; resistance ∝ capsule thickness.
- **Ahmed**: valved, **184 mm²**, outflow above **8–10 mm Hg**. **Molteno**, **Baerveldt**: non-valved, **tied 2–3 weeks**.
- Plate **superotemporal**, **8–10 mm** behind the limbus; superonasal → pseudo-Brown's.
- AC tube: **bevel up**, **1.5–2 mm**, parallel to iris. Pars plana: **3–4 mm**, **bevel down**, after vitrectomy.
- Device bleb: posterior, **no microcysts**; IOP fall smaller than trabeculectomy.
- After a device: **no miotics, no alpha agonists**.
- Erosion → **stent**; exposure → endophthalmitis.
- Uncontrolled or no useful vision → **cyclophotocoagulation**.
**Mnemonic:** none in your mnemonics file for this case.
:::
